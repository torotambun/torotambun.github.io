How Many Times Can You Say the Same Thing Before It's Clear You Never Meant It?

The same sentence, word for word, across three years — followed by apologies that centre on the sender and invitations that never produce a meeting. It is not disrespect. It is form displacing substance, and when the pattern is named the reply apologises for lateness rather than for the pattern. That is not behaviour. It is conditioning, and it is what education trains whether it intends to or not.

Opening

Three different years, one identical sentence.

"What's your reaction when you receive a message that looks kind, respectful, even poetic — but then you realize… it's been sent to you every year, word for word?"

Situation

"What is your wow when a student or colleague repeats the same message, again and again, over the span of three years?"

Complication

"Here's what I found scrolling through my own chat history with a former student" — the same sentence, three times: "I hope you're always in a state filled with the grace of health and also a burning spirit."

Question

"Three different years. One identical sentence." Is that rudeness, carelessness, or something that needs a different name?

Answer

"At first, it felt warm. Later, it felt rehearsed. Eventually, it felt hollow." "This wasn't disrespect. But it showed something deeper: a pattern. A comfort with performance over presence. Words without weight. Apologies without ownership. Invitations without commitment."

The Record

Three message types, and none of them ever produced a meeting.

What the chat history actually contained, in the three shapes the article sorts it into.

RECORDED A
The recycled greeting
"I hope you're always in a state filled with the grace of health and also a burning spirit." Sent in three different years, identical each time — the same words offered as though newly meant.
RECORDED B
The self-indicting delay apology
"Sorry for slow response pak toro.. I was riding from Lebak Bulus to Bandung this morning.." and variations on it, each explaining a personal disruption. "Always centered on personal disruption. Never tied to follow-up."
RECORDED C
The invitation that never lands
"Hopefully we can meet again someday hehe" "Is it possible pak if we meet together again in Jakarta this year? hehe" "I'd love to catch up as well.. hopefully it will be asap" — and then: "Polite. Cheerful. And completely unfulfilled."
RECORDED D
Zero follow-through, over years
"Statistically, if meeting invitations are made repeatedly with 0% follow-through over years, that is not noise. That is a low-intent behavioral pattern — often interpreted as social ritualism, not commitment (Feldman & Pentland, 2003)."

The Lenses

Lexical stagnation across time is rarely random.

"And what does this mean — from a behavioral and analytical lens?" Four readings, each named to a source.

1  ·  Communication science
"In communication science, lexical stagnation across time is rarely random. If someone reuses the same formalized phrase over years, it often indicates performative intent or habitual signaling — not real engagement (van Dijk, 2006). Language, when unchanging, signals low cognitive investment."
2  ·  Attribution theory
"In psychology, Weiner's attribution theory (1985) explains how people explain failure. Repeated use of external excuses — “I was traveling,” “I was sleepy,” “I didn't bring my laptop” — reflects external locus of control and avoidance of accountability."
3  ·  Impression management
"In professional behavior, impression management refers to how individuals shape external perceptions, often using rehearsed language or social scripts to maintain image rather than engage authentically. Repetition of formal phrases and hollow meeting gestures reflect this behavioral scripting (Leary, 2003)."
4  ·  Organizational routines
Feldman and Pentland's work on routines supplies the last reading: repeated invitation without follow-through is a routine being performed rather than an intention being formed. "Some students learn to sound polite. But they never practice responsibility. They know how to greet — but not how to grow."

The Reply

He apologised for lateness, not for the pattern.

"After posting this reflection, the student replied…"

The reply apologised for delay and promised to be more responsive. "At first glance, it sounds humble. But again — the pattern holds."

"He didn't recognize the real issue. He apologized only for lateness — not for templated messages, recycled greetings, or years of hollow follow-up."

"He centered the reply on himself: "I'll try to be more responsive." The deeper problem — disengagement masked as politeness — never registered." "That's when it became clear: this wasn't just behavior. It was habit."

The Diagnosis

The form has displaced the substance, and it is conditioning, not malice.

"The performance has become subconscious."

"Saying “pak,” adding 🙏😢, and inserting polite phrases — this has become his internal measure of respect."

"The form has displaced the substance. This is not malice. It's conditioning. A cultural rhythm that rewards politeness over presence."

"And unless disrupted, it stays like this — polite, apologetic, and empty." The article is explicit that the target is not the individual: "This is not just about one student. This is about what we train — intentionally or not."

What is asked of educators

"As educators, we need to teach students how ritualized speech can hide relational avoidance. Not to blame. But to wake up." And earlier, on the same point: "As mentors, we must notice this. Not to shame — but to teach."

Conclusion

Respect is not in the phrase; it is in the presence.

"Because progress isn't made by repeating polite sentences. Progress is made by showing up."

Progress isn't about intention. It's about follow-through. And respect isn't in the phrase. It's in the presence.

— How Many Times Can You Say the Same Thing Before It's Clear You Never Meant It?
Why the distinction matters for teaching
A student who has learned that politeness is respect will keep producing politeness and believe the obligation discharged. The correction is not to demand less courtesy but to stop letting courtesy stand in for the thing it was meant to accompany.
What the article refuses to do
"They say “let's meet” — but never plan to. They apologize — but never change." And still: not to shame, not to blame. The diagnosis is offered as something to teach against, not as a verdict on a person.
