Lessons in Case Study Teaching: Insights from a Case Series on "EB"

A directive teaching style that works while a class is still learning the case method becomes the source of its resistance once the group has cohered. The correction is not a softer manner but a change in the base of authority — from legitimate and coercive power toward expert and referent power — and that shift requires the instructor to be visibly willing to learn from the students.

Opening

An instructor's board plan was his method: facts left, analysis centre, recommendations right.

A case series followed one instructor through a course, and the boards are where his teaching philosophy is legible.

The article opens by refusing the simple account of the craft: "Teaching case studies effectively involves more than just delivering content—it's about adapting to the dynamics within the classroom, understanding the implicit learning contracts with students, and being responsive to their evolving needs."

The instructor's method is concrete and visible. "His meticulous approach involves dividing the boards into three distinct sections: case facts on the left, analysis in the center, and recommendations on the right." The rationale is equally clear: "This structured board plan ensures that every discussion follows a logical progression, helping students to clearly see the flow from facts to conclusions."

The article is explicit that this was not a mistake at the outset. The board usage "reflects his directive teaching style, which worked effectively in the early stages of the course. By providing a clear visual structure, he helped his students navigate complex material, especially when they were still getting accustomed to the case method."

The Turn

The structure that carried the class early became the thing they resisted later.

The article locates the failure in timing rather than in method, which is what makes it a lesson rather than a criticism.

"However, as the course progressed, the need for this rigid structure began to clash with the students' growing desire for more autonomy in their learning process." Nothing about the board plan changed; the students did. The scaffolding that made an unfamiliar method navigable in week one was still standing when it was no longer load-bearing.

The resistance was collective, and the article says so plainly: "As students became more cohesive, they collectively resisted the teaching style. Earlier recognition and adaptation to these dynamics could have mitigated the resistance." A cohering group is ordinarily read as a teaching success. Here it is the mechanism by which a working style stops working — which is the counter-intuitive part and the reason the series is worth studying.

The instructor is unnamed

The article identifies neither the instructor nor the case series beyond the initials "EB," and the deck adds nothing. What is at issue is a pattern in case teaching, not a person's competence.

Details

A style that fits the first week is not the style the eighth week needs.

Five of the article's nine takeaways sit before the question of power, and each names a thing that has to move when the class does.

1  ·  Adaptability in teaching style
"A successful teaching style at the start of a course may need to evolve as students' needs change." The initial directive approach worked; as students gained confidence they needed more autonomy, "and the rigid structure led to tension." The lesson is about expiry, not about error.
2  ·  Understanding and responding to group dynamics
"Group dynamics play a crucial role in the classroom." The article's diagnosis is that the resistance was legible before it surfaced, and that reading it earlier was the available intervention.
3  ·  Clear communication and learning contracts
"The implicit and explicit learning contracts between teachers and students must be clear and mutually understood." The instructor's struggle with those contracts "led to confusion and resistance when his expectations didn't align with the students'." A contract that is implicit is one neither side can be held to.
4  ·  Balancing rigor with approachability
"While academic rigor is important, it must be balanced with approachability and sensitivity to students' stress levels." Emphasising rigor without those factors "can increase tension and disrupt classroom dynamics" — rigor becomes a stressor rather than a standard.
5  ·  Feedback and self-reflection
"Regularly seeking and reflecting on student feedback is essential for continuous improvement." What eventually moved the instructor was student feedback, which the article treats as evidence for responsiveness rather than as a rebuke.

Framework

He drew on legitimate and coercive power; expert and referent power were available and unused.

The article reads the whole episode through Raven's five sources of power, which turns a style problem into a question of where authority is being drawn from.

1  ·  Legitimate
"Derived from the instructor's formal role; essential for establishing authority." The article does not treat this as optional — it is the base an instructor starts from.
2  ·  Reward
"The ability to offer positive reinforcement; crucial for motivating students."
3  ·  Coercive
"The capacity to enforce discipline; must be used sparingly to avoid resentment." The article's judgement is that this was one of the two the instructor leaned on.
4  ·  Expert
"Gained through knowledge and expertise; this can be strengthened by showing vulnerability and a willingness to learn from students." The mechanism is counter-intuitive: expertise is reinforced by admitting its limits, not by defending them.
5  ·  Referent
"Built on respect and admiration; achieved by being approachable and genuinely engaging with students." It is the only one of the five the students confer rather than the institution.
The article's own verdict

"The instructor initially relied heavily on legitimate and coercive power, but he could have benefited from building expert and referent power by showing a genuine willingness to learn from his students and by fostering a more collaborative classroom environment."

The Comparison

Ted Klever did not interrupt, and the room engaged more.

The article sets one named counter-example against the case-series instructor, and the difference between them is a single behaviour.

The directive instructor
•Boards divided in advance: facts, analysis, recommendations
•Discussion follows the structure the plan laid down
•Worked "effectively in the early stages of the course"
•Loosened only later, and the article records the result: "The decision to loosen the directive style later in the semester allowed for more effective and engaging discussions"
Ted Klever, non-directive
✓A non-directive style in which "he did not interrupt students and allowed them to engage with each other more freely"
✓"This approach fostered a highly engaged and interactive classroom environment"
✓"Ted's method shows the benefits of stepping back and letting students take the lead in discussions"
✓The article names what that produces: "encouraging deeper engagement and peer learning"
Address the cause, not the symptom

"When faced with resistance or challenges in the classroom, it's important to address the underlying issues rather than just the symptoms. Understanding the root causes of resistance can lead to more effective solutions."

Conclusion

Daring to be vulnerable is a teaching method, not a personality trait.

The series ends on collaboration, and on a claim that costs the instructor something.

This means daring to be vulnerable, demonstrating genuine curiosity, and having the energy to learn together with the students.

— Lessons in Case Study Teaching: Insights from a Case Series on "EB", 15 August 2024
The students already hold expertise
"Recognizing that students often bring their own expertise to the classroom can create a more dynamic and productive learning environment." This is the premise the whole power argument rests on — expert power can only be built by learning from students if the students actually know something.
The shift worked when it came
"When the instructor began to see the value in learning from his students and adjusted his approach accordingly, the classroom dynamic improved. The students responded positively to this shift, leading to better outcomes for both the instructor and the class."
What the series is for
The closing sentence names three requirements and puts them in one line: "continuous reflection, a willingness to adapt, and daring to be vulnerable are key to success in the classroom." Two are dispositions the instructor controls; the third is one he has to be willing to be seen doing.
