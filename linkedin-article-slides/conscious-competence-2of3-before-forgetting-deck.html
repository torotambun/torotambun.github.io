(2 of 3) Conscious Competence as Pre-Requisite Before Forgetting

Forgetting presupposes competence, so entrepreneurship education first has to move learners from uncalibrated confidence to usable, eventually tacit, knowledge. An adapted Competence-Perception Learning Loop separates actual from perceived competence and splits advanced competence into Tacit Mastery and Conscious Expertise, and because a beginner and a master can sound alike, only performance can tell them apart.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

If the dialectic begins with Recall, entrepreneurship education must first explain how knowledge becomes competence worth recalling.

Continued from Part 1, Learn to Forget.

Situation

Prof. Laksono’s dialectic begins with Recall, which presupposes knowledge already acquired.

Complication

"Knowledge has first to be acquired, used, tested, and sometimes practised long enough to become automatic."

Question

"how does knowledge move from ignorance, to awareness, to usable competence, and eventually into tacit practice?"

Answer

An adapted Conscious Competence model, the Competence-Perception Learning Loop (Tambun, 2025).

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

The loop plots actual against perceived competence, adapting the Conscious Competence model rather than the Dunning-Kruger effect.

"The educational issue is therefore not confidence itself, but calibration between perceived competence and actual competence."

Learners differ "not only in what they know but also in how accurately they can judge what they know" (Nierenberg & Dahl, 2021). "However, I no longer describe my framework as Dunning-Kruger, particularly because recent work warns against treating the effect as a simple or universal law of human learning (Gignac, 2024)."

The architecture is closer to Conscious Competence (Keeley, 2021), adapted with a transitional Framework Learner stage and two advanced forms: Tacit Mastery and Conscious Expertise.

Status of the model

"The result is therefore not a standard published five-stage model. It is my teaching synthesis built on established learning concepts."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Learners move from an overconfident beginner, through humbled realisation, to a framework learner whose competence is still effortful.

"The first educational task is therefore calibration."

Stage ①
Overconfident Beginner
"I don’t know that I don’t know." Attitude is mistaken for ability, and frameworks seem unnecessary.
Stage ②
Humbled Realization
"I know that I don’t know." Also called Awareness of Gaps, deliberately not Recall, because the learner sees knowledge not yet developed.
Stage ③
Framework Learner
Concepts organise problems, but "Competence exists, but it remains effortful, deliberate, and explicit."
Academic and practical expertise

"Academic expertise is genuine expertise, but it is not identical to entrepreneurial practice."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

Repeated application turns declarative knowledge into procedural knowledge that grows fluent and harder to articulate.

Saks et al. (2021) distinguish declarative knowledge, what can be stated, from procedural knowledge, knowing how to perform.

"A framework that never leaves the classroom remains primarily declarative knowledge". Politis (2005), Bell and Bell (2020) and Hägg and Kurczewska (2019) place experience at the centre of entrepreneurial learning.

An experienced driver, mechanic or salesperson acts before explaining. Repeated customer contact, negotiation and cash-flow trouble make patterns "visible almost immediately." "I sometimes call this muscle memory in class", though procedural knowledge and tacit competence are more precise.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Performing well and explaining why are different capabilities, so advanced competence splits into tacit mastery and conscious expertise.

"Practical competence can exist without full verbal articulation, while explicit explanation can also exist without equivalent depth of practical experience."

④ Tacit Mastery
•"I don’t know that I know."
•High competence with little need to reconstruct knowledge before acting
•A street entrepreneur negotiating, a fisherman reading a storm
•Limit: the knowledge may not be explicit enough for others to examine or learn
⑤ Conscious Expert
•"I know that I know."
•Fluent performance plus the ability to reconstruct its reasoning
•Can move between explicit knowledge and experienced judgment
•"expertise and teachability are not identical"
Why experienced founders are underestimated

"What appears unstructured from the outside may represent a substantial accumulation of practical knowledge that has become difficult to reconstruct verbally."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Gap

The overconfident beginner and the tacit master can both say “I just do it”, so only actual performance can tell them apart.

"The similarity in language can conceal opposite underlying conditions."

The know-it-all who knows nothing
✗Too little knowledge to recognise what has not been learned
✗Dismisses frameworks before being able to evaluate them
✗"uncalibrated confidence"
The tacit master
✓Years of experience compressed into automatic judgment
✓Explicit reconstruction no longer needed for every action
✓"proceduralised competence"
What the educator must look at

"Competence must eventually become visible through decisions, actions, outcomes, adaptation, and the ability to respond when assumptions fail."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Competence that becomes automatic solves one learning problem and creates the next, which Part 3 takes up.

"This sequence is not a claim that every learner follows exactly the same path. It is a conceptual map for distinguishing forms of competence that can otherwise look deceptively similar."

Once knowledge becomes automatic, another problem appears.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, Conscious Competence (2 of 3)
The five stages
Overconfident Beginner, Humbled Realization, Framework Learner, then Tacit Mastery or Conscious Expertise, with movement between the two depending on context.
