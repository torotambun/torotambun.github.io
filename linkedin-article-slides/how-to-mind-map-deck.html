How to Mind Map ?

Decades of note-taking failed and the author had blamed the tools — paper, then app after app. The diagnosis he arrives at is that the problem was never the software but the linearity he had been conditioned into, while the brain processes radiantly and visually. Buzan's five elements, learned at Harvard's General Management Program alongside a hundred and thirty-four other executives, are the corrective. Taught to eighty participants as a map of their own identity, the technique turns out to be less a filing system than a mirror held between impostor syndrome and excessive self-awareness.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

The problem was never the software.

The article opens on a long-running personal failure, and names what it had wrongly blamed.

"Despite years of study and attending numerous schools and executive courses, I've consistently found myself in a challenging situation when it comes to taking notes and organizing the clutter of my memory."

The goal is described in the terms it kept failing on: "My goal has always been to neatly categorize this information in my mental “drawer,” striving to retain as much as possible or to recall it effectively over time. However, as I've grown older, I've realized that the techniques I've employed have not fully met my needs."

Digitisation changed nothing. "This issue persisted despite the digitization of note-taking. The lack of a proper filing and retrieval system only compounded my frustration." The tools are listed by name — paper notes and digital ones "made using various apps like Apple Notes, Word, Text, Noteshelf, PDF Reader, or drawing apps" — and "the solution seemed elusive."

The admission, in one sentence: "In hindsight, I understand that the problem wasn't with the software; rather, it was with my approach to processing information."

The Turn

I had been conditioned to think linearly while the brain works radiantly.

The diagnosis, and where the corrective came from.

"Over the years, I was conditioned to think and absorb information linearly, not realizing that our brains naturally process information in a radiant, non-linear manner. Additionally, as visual creatures, we are not fully utilizing our brain's capacity to absorb and present information."

The corrective arrived as part of a curriculum, not a self-help discovery. "During my time at the Harvard Business School's General Management Program in 2018-2019, they taught us, the executives, several relevant skills to aid in our studies and preparation for returning to our businesses."

The full list is worth keeping because mind-mapping sits inside it rather than above it: "networking (even for an introvert), case study preparation, note-taking using the split-page format, presenting using “tripling” and synthesis techniques, listening, questioning, presenting an argument, critical thinking in discussions, optimal learning at HBS, and mind-mapping."

"All of these skills were imparted to the 135 executives by Ms. Genet Jeanjean MA MEd." And the author's own use of it was immediate and practical: "Throughout the course, I primarily used mind maps to dissect case studies."

Framework

Buzan's five elements: central image, colour, single keywords, curved branches, pictures.

The technique as the article distils it, credited to its creator.

1  ·  Central image
"A strong, central visual that encapsulates the main idea."
2  ·  Use of colour
"Colors are employed to differentiate and highlight connections."
3  ·  Print single keywords
"Each branch is labeled with a single, significant keyword." One word per branch — the constraint that does the most work and is broken most often.
4  ·  Branches
"These are curved and connected, emanating from the central image." Curved, not straight; connected, not floating.
5  ·  Pictures throughout
"Visuals are integrated across the map to enhance memory and understanding."
What a mind map is, and who owns the term

"A Mind Map is a user-friendly system designed for thought processing that aids in managing information, linking ideas, and organizing thoughts through a graphic organizer." Its uses run to "planning, studying for exams, organizing, preparing for writing and speaking, note-taking, project management", and for the author "quick mental preparation, such as for elevator pitches." The article notes that "The Buzan Organization holds the registered trademark for Mind Mapping."

Field Notes

Eighty participants mapped their own identity in coloured pencil.

The classroom application, and what the author was actually trying to achieve with it.

"In an engaging classroom activity, around eighty participants were encouraged to create a Mind Map on paper using colorful pencils. The task was to reflect and map out their personal identity."

The exercise had two audiences at once: it "was not only a moment of self-reflection but also an opportunity for participants to consider how others might perceive them and what aspects of their personality may intrigue others."

Why a self-map rather than a CV: "While it's unlikely that the formal presentation of our CVs will deviate from the traditional formats seen on platforms like LinkedIn, a self Mind Map offers a unique perspective. It allows individuals to view themselves as dynamic beings with potentially limitless potential and areas for growth."

And the author states the aim in his own terms: "My goal is for them to see themselves as their Creator intended, recognizing the purpose placed upon them in this life's journey."

Details

I criticise my own maps harder than theirs.

The article's most unusual move: the worked negative examples are the author's own.

He names the tools he used — "Ayoa For Kids, Ayoa, and MindNode" — and then marks his own output against Buzan's five elements rather than presenting it as a model.

On the first: confusing, missing images, not distinguishing higher from lesser importance, and the keyword not placed on the line. Four of the five elements identified as unmet, in his own work, in public.

On the third: he says plainly that he does not like it, that many things are wrong with it, and that this is so "despite using the handsomely paid software" — which closes the loop on the article's opening claim that the tool was never the problem.

Only one example is offered as better, and it is introduced as such rather than as correct. The article ends its demonstration with a section headed as what he wishes he had explained.

Why self-critique is the right method here

A teacher who shows only good examples teaches recognition, not diagnosis. Marking his own maps against the five elements shows the reader how to fail and how to notice it — which is the transferable part.

Conclusion

The mind map is a mirror held between impostor syndrome and excessive self-awareness.

What the technique turns out to be for, once it has stopped being a filing system.

The Mind Map serves as a mirror, not just for self-assessment, but also as a reminder of their intrinsic value and the unique path they are on.

— How to Mind Map ?, 4 March 2024
The two failure modes it sits between
A self map "provides a reflective overview of where they have been, where they are, and where they could go, helping them to gauge their position between feelings of impostor syndrome and excessive self-awareness." Both are distortions of self-estimate, in opposite directions.
How far the article travels
It opens on a filing problem — where to put notes so they can be found again — and closes on how a person estimates their own worth. The technique is the same object throughout; only what it is being asked to organise has changed.
Where the technique recurs
Mind mapping is named as the first tool of the idea-generation layer in the author's teaching manual of 26 March, where the instruction is to supply clean paper and coloured pencils — the same materials as this class.
