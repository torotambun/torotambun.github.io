Edgar Schein's and Geert Hofstede's hypothetical evolutionary anthropological path

This article takes a sketch the author had already made and does two things to it. It attaches a dated primary work to every stage, turning a list of positions into a bibliography that can be checked: Tylor 1871, Boas 1911, Malinowski 1922, Levi-Strauss 1962, Geertz 1973, Hofstede 1980 and Schein 1985. Then it adds a seventh stage the earlier version omitted, post-structuralism with Derrida 1967 and Foucault 1969, which changes what the path means. A sequence ending at Schein reads as an ascent toward organisational culture. A sequence that continues past him into Foucault and Derrida says the search for universal structures was itself challenged, and that anything built on Schein has to answer for it. The article states its own status honestly as a simplification for illustrative purposes.

Introduction

The article opens by admitting it is a simplification, which is what makes it usable.

It begins with two words that place it inside a working sequence rather than at the start of one. "As reminder:" is how it opens, five minutes after the Hofstede piece that carried the bare version of the same sketch.

The purpose is stated without inflation: "To sketch a hypothetical evolutionary path of anthropological theories leading to the work of Edgar Schein and Geert Hofstede, we'll start with foundational anthropological concepts and gradually move to more contemporary ideas, culminating in organizational culture theories."

And so is the caveat, in the same breath rather than in a footnote. "This is a simplification for illustrative purposes, as the actual historical and theoretical trajectories are more complex."

What this version adds to the earlier one is evidence. Each stage now carries a named work and a date, which turns a defensible narrative into a set of claims a reader can go and check.

Framework

Seven stages, each now anchored to a dated work.

The elaboration gives every stage a thinker, a main idea, an evolutionary significance and, new in this version, a primary text.

1871
Classical evolutionism: Morgan and Tylor
Tylor's Primitive Culture. Cultures move through fixed stages, "such as from savagery to civilization", which the article calls "a somewhat linear view of cultural progression". Credited with founding the systematic study of cultures.
1911
Historical particularism: Franz Boas
The Mind of Primitive Man. Boas "posited that each culture possesses a unique historical trajectory and should be understood on its own terms". The article describes this as a paradigm shift that "emphasized cultural relativism and rejected overarching stages of development".
1922
Functionalism: Malinowski and Radcliffe-Brown
Malinowski's Argonauts of the Western Pacific, which "showcased how cultural practices maintained social equilibrium". The proposal is that each element of a culture has a specific function ensuring societal stability.
1962
Structuralism: Claude Levi-Strauss
The Savage Mind. Levi-Strauss "argued for universal mental structures that influenced cultural symbols and narratives, often rooted in binary oppositions". The focus moves "from the visible functions of culture to the underlying cognitive scaffolds that shaped them".
1973
Interpretive anthropology: Clifford Geertz
The Interpretation of Cultures. Rejecting overarching structures, Geertz emphasised meanings within specific cultures, "underlining the necessity to understand culture from within, through its symbols and meanings".
1980 / 1985
Organizational anthropology: Hofstede and Schein
Hofstede's Culture's Consequences "mapped out dimensions that differentiated national cultures, while Schein's “Organizational Culture and Leadership” (1985) explored the multilayered nature of organizational cultures". Anthropological insight turned on modern organisations.
1967 / 1969
Post-structuralism: Derrida and Foucault
The stage the earlier sketch omitted, placed last though its works are earlier. Derrida's Of Grammatology and Foucault's The Archaeology of Knowledge.

Gap

The seventh stage is what changes the meaning of the other six.

A path that stops at Schein reads as an ascent. Anthropology matures, turns its attention to organisations, and delivers a model of culture that managers can use. The earlier version of this sketch ended exactly there.

The addition reverses the direction of travel. The article describes it as an upheaval rather than a development: post-structuralism arrived with scholars who "radically transformed the academic landscape by critiquing the very foundations upon which knowledge and meaning were based".

Foucault supplies the political half. Through The Archaeology of Knowledge he examined "the intricate relationships between power, knowledge, and discourse, questioning how knowledge is constructed and who gets to define “truth”".

Derrida supplies the linguistic half. In Of Grammatology he "introduced the method of deconstruction, challenging the stability of language and meaning", holding that "meaning is not fixed but is constantly deferred, revealing the inherent contradictions and biases within texts".

The stated significance of the stage

A "Departure from the search for universal structures (as pursued by structuralists) to an exploration of how structures are constructed, deconstructed, and influenced by power dynamics." The consequence for the thread is direct: shared values are not simply found, they are made, and by someone.

Conclusion

The summary still ends at Schein, so the seventh stage sits outside the story it disrupts.

The closing paragraph is carried over unchanged from the earlier version, which leaves a visible seam between the elaborated path and its conclusion.

This hypothetical trajectory shows how the field expanded its lens from broad cultural evolutions to the intricate dynamics of modern organizations.

— Edgar Schein's and Geert Hofstede's hypothetical evolutionary anthropological path, 27 September 2023
What the summary covers
Fixed stages, then unique histories, then how cultural elements maintain stability, then the cognitive structures beneath them, then meanings within specific cultures, and finally the application to organisations. Six stages, recounted in order.
What it leaves out
Post-structuralism does not appear in the closing summary at all, though it has just been given the longest single elaboration in the article. The seam is left as it stands here rather than smoothed over.
Where it is resolved
Twelve hours later the same day, the capstone article takes the omission as its subject and argues that structuralism and post-structuralism should be scoped alongside each other rather than sequenced.
The status of the whole
Hypothetical, by the article's own word, used three times. It is offered as a way of ordering a field for a reader, not as an account of how the field actually developed.
