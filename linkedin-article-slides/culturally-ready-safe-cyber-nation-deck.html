Fostering a Culturally Ready of an Innovative and a Safe Cyber Nation: Culture First

The article argues that a country faces two distinct AI failures and that most policy attention goes to the wrong one. Missing the technology carries geopolitical, cultural and cybersecurity exposure, and that risk is widely understood. Adopting it without cultural readiness carries a different and less discussed cost: economic disparity, technological dependency, job displacement, ethical gaps, a skills shortfall and data inequality, which together produce dependency rather than capability. Since the condition that separates the two outcomes is cultural rather than technical, the remedy the article proposes is educational and generational, starting in early childhood and taking one or two generations to arrive. Because that is far too slow to meet the present moment, it proposes a parallel track of actions available now, and the argument's title states the priority: culture first.

Introduction

The apprehension about AI is old enough to have a pattern, and the pattern is the argument.

The article opens by refusing novelty as a frame. The printing press threatened the leaders of its society, industrial automation in the 1800s raised job displacement fears, and a calculator disrupted mathematics education in the 1970s. AI's reception belongs to that series.

The historical point has a sharper edge for Indonesia specifically. "Artificial Intelligence is not new after all. It was first coined in 1950, while Indonesia was still in its infancy as a sovereign nation." Seventy-three years of development preceded the moment of alarm.

From that the article draws its position on resistance rather than on adoption. "The inexorable march of progress cannot be halted. Attempting to resist technological advancements is akin to battling against the tides of change."

It also names the failure mode it wants avoided, in Javanese rather than in English, which locates the problem culturally from the first page: the article warns against acting "gumun", which it glosses as trend-following combined with only superficial knowledge.

The epigraph the article chooses

Mark Twain, quoted in the opening section: "It ain't what you don't know that gets you into trouble. It's what you know for sure that just ain't so." The line frames superficial certainty, rather than ignorance, as the danger.

Framework

Missing the boat is the risk everyone names, and it runs wider than economics.

The article sets out five consequences for countries that fall behind, and only the first is the one usually discussed.

1  ·  Geopolitical implications
"AI advancements can lead to a strategic advantage for nations that possess superior AI capabilities." The existing landscape is disrupted as dominance shifts toward AI-driven military or economic advantage.
2  ·  Cultural impact and demolition
"The adoption of AI technologies might lead to the erosion or dilution of traditional practices, values, and cultural identities, potentially diminishing cultural diversity and uniqueness." The heading the article gives this is demolition, not dilution.
3  ·  New imperialism
"Powerful AI technologies could contribute to a new form of imperialism, where nations with advanced AI capabilities exert influence over others." Economic, political or technological dependency produces unequal power and limited sovereignty.
4  ·  Cybersecurity risk, and the domino effect
AI systems widen the attack surface, exposed to "vulnerabilities in AI algorithms, data poisoning, or adversarial attacks". And the exposure is not contained: "A significant cyber incident or the compromise of AI-enabled systems in one country could quickly spread across borders, impacting regional or even global stability."

Gap

Jumping in unready is the opposite failure, and it is the one the article is actually about.

This section names a phenomenon it says is "shared by many underdeveloped and developing countries" and calls it a careless act. Six threats follow.

1  ·  Economic disparity and technological dependency
The cost of AI infrastructure, research and development "could widen the economic gap, hindering the underdeveloped countries' ability to compete and catch up". Relying on AI without domestic capability creates dependence on external entities that "could compromise their sovereignty and limit their decision-making autonomy".
2  ·  Job displacement
Automation threatens labour-intensive sectors first, which "could intensify unemployment, exacerbate social inequalities, and impede economic growth if alternative job opportunities or retraining programs are not readily available".
3  ·  Ethical concerns
Without robust regulatory frameworks, the article warns of "bias in AI algorithms, infringement of privacy rights, or unethical use of AI in surveillance systems", which it ties directly to human rights rather than to compliance.
4  ·  Skills gap and data inequality
Limited access to quality education, research facilities and skilled professionals constrains the ability to use AI at all. And since AI "heavily relies on vast amounts of high-quality data", countries with poor data infrastructure face a second, compounding disadvantage.
Where the article breaks with the standard remedy

It lists the usual answers, robust cybersecurity, responsible development, international collaboration and ethical frameworks, and then declines them as a starting point: "However, that is not where it should start."

Implications

If the constraint is cultural, the intervention is early education and it arrives a generation late.

The article proposes eleven initiatives in early education and is explicit about the timescale before listing any of them.

1  ·  The foundation
Early childhood programmes nurturing creativity, critical thinking and problem-solving, with STEM introduced to young learners. Equal access to quality education, with policies bridging the urban and rural gap and prioritising marginalised communities.
2  ·  The delivery system
Investment in facilities and technology to match the evolving landscape, continuous professional development so teachers can integrate technology into the curriculum, and technical and vocational training aligned with emerging industries.
3  ·  The precondition
"Addressing fundamental needs like nutrition and healthcare to support students' physical and mental well-being, enabling better engagement in education and creativity." Basic needs are placed inside the education plan rather than beside it.
4  ·  The ecosystem
Recognising indigenous knowledge and local innovations, public-private partnerships, research and development funding for national challenges, support for entrepreneurs through grants and incubators, and digital inclusion to close the divide.
The timescale, stated without softening

"It may take one or two generations, but we must equip future generations with the necessary skills and mindset for technological advancements" The honesty about duration is what forces the parallel track that follows.

Details

A generational remedy needs something running alongside it, so the second list is for individuals rather than ministries.

The article is clear that the early-education programme cannot be the whole answer, because the people who need capability now will not benefit from it. "It is understood that we must do something now, while broader societal changes are underway."

The twelve actions it proposes are addressed to individuals and communities rather than to the state: continuous learning and upskilling through online courses and workshops, technology awareness programmes, local innovation hubs where people collaborate on projects, and support for local entrepreneurs building technological solutions.

Others are civic rather than technical. Advocating for STEM in schools and community centres, volunteering in tech initiatives and mentorship, engaging in advocacy for responsible and inclusive tech policy, and supporting digital inclusion for underserved communities.

The last group is about reach: exploring virtual learning, remote work and collaboration tools to access global opportunities, attending conferences and virtual events to connect with practitioners, and supporting initiatives that turn technology on social and environmental problems.

What the two lists have in common

Neither is about acquiring AI. The early-education list builds the capacity to absorb it and the parallel list builds the habits of engaging with it, which is what the title means by culture first.

Conclusion

Innovation and security are one programme, not two, because both rest on the same cultural foundation.

The closing section brings the security argument back together with the education argument, which is what the full title has been promising since the first page.

By combining initiatives in early education and proactive engagement with current technology changes, our nation can foster an innovative culture that empowers future generations to thrive in a technology-driven world.

— Fostering a Culturally Ready of an Innovative and a Safe Cyber Nation, 27 September 2023
Why security is not a separate chapter
The article holds that the plan for resilience "complements the administration's initiatives in early education and proactive engagement with current technology changes", making cybersecurity education part of the same foundation rather than a later addition.
What it asks of the state
Prioritising cybersecurity education, fostering collaborations, investing in cybersecurity infrastructure, and developing a skilled cybersecurity workforce, so that the nation "can confidently navigate the digital landscape while ensuring the security and privacy of its citizens and critical assets".
The claim it ends on
"With these strategic measures in place, Indonesia can stand at the forefront of technological innovation while safeguarding against evolving cyber threats." This is the only point where the country is named in the conclusion rather than referred to generically.
How to read the register
The article speaks throughout of what "the administration" proposes and recognises, which is the voice of a policy memorandum rather than of a personal essay. That framing is what the Jakarta Post published a month before this version appeared.
