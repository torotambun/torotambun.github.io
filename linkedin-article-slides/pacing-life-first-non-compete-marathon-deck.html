Pacing Life: Lessons from My First Non-Compete Marathon

The happiness I had been running toward lasted about as long as the Cheng Tng. What the forty-two kilometres actually showed is that the joy was distributed across the miles — the storm, the fall, the strangers in the park — and that the self who remembers the finish is not the self who did the living.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

I walked the first kilometre of my marathon through a storm I had not planned for.

East Coast Park, Singapore. A route I knew, with jetties and detour sections I had never taken.

Within the first kilometre the sky opened. Instead of the strong start I had imagined, I walked under a gentle rain, my pace slowed by wet shoes and wet clothes — the way life slows a person down at the moment they least expect it.

The route had been planned carefully: nine kilometres out and nine back, from Marine Parade to Marina Bay MRT, then twelve out and twelve back heading east toward Changi. The second leg became another nine and nine, which left me with a six-kilometre void to fill, mentally and physically, that the plan had no answer for.

The line the article turns on

"Everyone has a plan until they get punched in the mouth." The marathon's lesson, on the article's own account, is not planning but adjusting — it is not about how fast we go but how we adapt to the pace, conditions and changes we cannot control.

Field Notes

Eight years of five to seven kilometres a day is what made the distance possible.

Preparation was not a last-minute effort. It was years in the making, built on discipline and consistency.

Since 2016 I have averaged 5.9 to 7.4 kilometres daily, showing up whether or not the conditions were good — except on Christmas and New Year, or when I fly.

That week alone: 14.4 kilometres toward Changi's Terminal 2, minutes before Pope Francis arrived; 25.88 kilometres toward Hillview MRT via town and the old railway corridor; 26.8 kilometres toward Changi Village over Tanah Merah and Changi Beach; and ten kilometres in the Botanic Garden. The month before: 42.9 kilometres run and 31.5 kilometres jogged across a week in Boston and Tokyo.

The daily repetition became something other than fitness. It worked as a form of meditation — sometimes opening space for reflection, at other times producing plain mental fatigue.

The Turns

My doctor said I am not thirty anymore; I answered with a body age of forty-two.

Even with the preparation, she was not on board.

She cautioned me against a challenge this rigorous at 54, having seen too many injuries in older people who stretched the body too far. Her warning was stern: "You're not 30 anymore."

I reminded her, gently, that while my chronological age is 54, my body age is closer to 42 after years of disciplined training — and that I would not be running at full speed but jogging and walking at a steady pace. The challenge was never speed. It was the endurance to finish.

What the exchange is doing in the piece

Her concern reflects a broader expectation that physical endurance belongs to the young. Read through autoethnography, a private medical conversation becomes a place to examine how societal frameworks shape perceptions of aging — and the choice to weigh internal markers of health over the number society assigns is the resistance the article records.

Field Notes

At 17.5 kilometres I fell hard enough that the bystanders went silent, and I got up.

What the body did across forty-two kilometres, as recorded on the day.

RECORDED A
The fall at 17.5 kilometres
Exhausted, I saw a green light at a crossing and my brain moved quicker than my feet. I missed the curb and went down. The bystanders went silent, as if watching a sack of rice hit the pavement. Betadine on the scars, dry clothes and socks from the fanny pack, and on.
RECORDED B
Steady to thirty
For the first thirty kilometres I held a consistent pace of around ten minutes per kilometre. From the start to the 26-kilometre mark I felt in control — each step measured, the energy balanced, the plan holding.
RECORDED C
The decay after thirty
By 32 kilometres the pace had slipped past eleven minutes; at 35 it was nearly twelve; by 38 it was over thirteen. What had been a physical challenge became a mental one, legs heavy and the mind asking whether it could continue.
RECORDED D
The last two kilometres
Around forty kilometres a small reserve appeared and the pace improved slightly. The final stretch was not about speed or timing. Having come that far, stopping was not an option.

What It Revealed

Dopamine paid out at the finish and was gone; the other three did the work before it.

Four chemicals, produced across the frontal cortex and the amygdala, dividing the labour of a long effort between them.

01
The reward driver
Dopamine
It fuelled each milestone, offering brief joy after every kilometre. At 42.195 the Garmin signalled the finish and the anticipated happiness was already fleeting — selfies, carbs, a well-earned Cheng Tng, and the rush gone almost as soon as it arrived.
02
The pain relievers
Endorphins
Released in response to sustained exertion, they dulled the physical strain and produced the moments of euphoria called the runner's high. By 32 kilometres that relief was fading, leaving mental fortitude to do what physical comfort no longer could.
03
The power hormone
Serotonin
It supplied the mental strength for the long stretch, and shifted the frame from performance to endurance — allowing the sound of rain on pavement and the languages in the park to register as part of the run rather than a distraction from it.
04
The bonding hormone
Oxytocin
It arrived through empathy: watching domestic workers find joy on a rare day off, and parents guiding children. It turned a solitary achievement into an expression of shared resilience.

The Scene

East Coast Park is where the marathon stopped being a solitary achievement.

The backdrop turned out to be a microcosm of cultural and social interaction, not scenery.

The park was full of groups of domestic workers, predominantly women, gathering in their rare free hours, and a church congregation celebrating a wedding. These women find solidarity not through formal mental health interventions but through friendship, song and shared cultural experience. My respect for their quiet endurance grew as I watched: their resilience, like mine, was built on community and connection rather than individual strength alone.

It was a melting pot of languages — Scandinavian, German, French, Spanish, Chinese, Burmese, Javanese, Indonesian, Malay, Tagalog, Australian English, Singlish — and of ages, with people fishing off the jetties, cycling, jogging, sunbathing and barbecuing, volleyball in full swing, children playing soccer and cricket, dogs socialising while their owners exchanged greetings.

Overhead, planes from Cebu Pacific, Scoot, AirAsia, Singapore Airlines, Emirates, Qantas, British Airways, Qatar Airways and Cathay Pacific. On the horizon, an unbroken line of ships stretching nearly eighty kilometres, waiting for a turn into one of the world's busiest harbours. Life in constant motion, ever-evolving, and beyond anyone's full control.

What I Carry

The remembering self takes the finish line, and the experiencing self did all the living.

The distinction the article ends on, and what it settles.

Odd as it may seem, I am my remembering self, and the experiencing self, who does my living, is like a stranger to me

— Daniel Kahneman, quoted in the article
Waiting for the end would have cost the whole race
Had I waited until the finish to feel fulfilled, I would have missed the moments of joy along the way — the rain-soaked miles, the quiet reflection, and even the painful fall.
The pattern is not confined to marathons
It is the shape of promotions, awards and milestones worked hard for, only to find the joy at the finish line short-lived. The remembering self dominates, and the richness of the present is what gets overlooked.
Which is what retirement clarified
I sprinted for twenty-five years in my career and, like the final kilometres, slowed. What the marathon confirmed is that the joy is not in how quickly success arrives but in the experience itself — the struggles alongside the victories.
