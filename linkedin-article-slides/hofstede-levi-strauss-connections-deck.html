Hofstede's Theory and Levi-Strauss Structuralism: Any connections?

The article asks whether Hofstede belongs to the same project as Levi-Strauss and finds a shared ambition sitting on incompatible foundations. Both hunt for patterns beneath observable behaviour, but Levi-Strauss locates them in the innate architecture of the mind while Hofstede locates them in what emerges from a socio-cultural environment, which is a different kind of object requiring different evidence. The article then reports the anthropological case against Hofstede without softening it: overgeneralisation from national scores, dimensions that imply static cultures, and unresolved questions about how the data were gathered and how culture was defined. Its placement of him is structural-functionalist rather than interpretive, which puts him at the opposite end of the discipline from Geertz, and it closes by sketching the six-stage path that leads from classical evolutionism to Hofstede and Schein.

Introduction

Six dimensions, influential and controversial in equal measure, are the object under test.

The article sets out Hofstede's framework in full before asking where it sits, and notes at the outset that the work "has been both influential and controversial in various fields, including anthropology, sociology, and business studies".

1  ·  Power Distance Index
"This dimension expresses the degree to which the less powerful members of a society accept and expect that power is distributed unequally."
2  ·  Individualism versus Collectivism
"This dimension deals with the degree to which people in a society are integrated into groups."
3  ·  Masculinity versus Femininity
Differentiates cultures by values associated with achievement and assertiveness on one side and cooperation and caring on the other.
4  ·  Uncertainty Avoidance Index
"This dimension relates to the extent to which a society feels threatened by uncertain or ambiguous situations."
5  ·  Long Term Orientation, and Indulgence versus Restraint
The fifth concerns how far a society values long-standing traditions against short-term or present-oriented thinking. The sixth "focuses on the extent to which members of society try to control their desires and impulses".

Framework

Both seek patterns beneath behaviour, but only one locates them in the mind.

The shared ambition is stated first, and it is genuine rather than rhetorical. Both Hofstede and Levi-Strauss aim to identify underlying structures or patterns that guide human behaviour, which is more than most pairings of a management theorist and an anthropologist have in common.

The divergence is where those patterns are held to live. Levi-Strauss sought universal cognitive structures manifested in myths, kinship systems and binary oppositions, which makes them properties of the human mind as such.

Hofstede sought something else entirely, and the article is precise about it: patterns "in national cultures as reflected in values and beliefs about power, individuality, uncertainty, etc." Those are properties of populations, not of cognition.

The consequence is a difference in kind rather than degree. "However, Hofstede's approach is less focused on the innate, universal structures of the mind and more on patterns that emerge from socio-cultural environments." One is looking for what every mind contains; the other for what a particular society has arrived at.

Gap

The anthropological case against Hofstede is reported without being softened.

The article records three objections raised by anthropologists and other scholars, and it does not argue them away.

CRITIQUE A
Overgeneralisation
"reducing a nation or a large cultural group to a set of scores on certain dimensions can be overly simplistic and might not capture the complexity and diversity within that group." The objection is about what a national average conceals.
CRITIQUE B
Static dimensions
"Cultures are dynamic and ever-changing. Hofstede's dimensions might give an impression of static or unchanging cultural traits, which can be problematic." A score presented without a date reads as a permanent property.
CRITIQUE C
Method and definition
"Some critics have pointed out issues with how Hofstede gathered and analyzed his data, as well as concerns about how he defined “culture.”" This is the deepest of the three, because it questions the construct rather than its application.
CRITIQUE D
Where the article places him
Closer to structural-functionalism, in that he identifies stable structures with roles in guiding behaviour, and explicitly distant from the interpretive tradition: it "differs significantly from the more interpretive or hermeneutic approaches in anthropology (like that of Geertz)".
Why the placement matters to the wider thread

Positioning Hofstede as structural-functionalist and Geertz as his opposite sets up the tension the capstone article resolves. The same day's closing piece argues that a single lens is not enough, and this is where the two lenses are first pulled apart.

Details

Six stages run from fixed cultural stages to organisational culture, and Hofstede sits at the end of them.

The article closes with a sketch of how anthropology arrived at Hofstede and Schein, offered as a simplification and labelled as one.

Late 19th c.
Classical evolutionism
Morgan and Tylor. Cultures evolve through fixed stages, from savagery through barbarism to civilisation. Credited with laying the groundwork for systematic study of cultures.
Early 20th c.
Historical particularism
Boas. Each culture has its own historical development and must be studied on its own terms. The shift is from universal stages to particular histories, with cultural relativism as the principle.
Early to mid 20th c.
Functionalism
Malinowski and Radcliffe-Brown. Cultural practices serve functions that maintain social equilibrium, so the interest moves to how elements of a culture hold together.
Mid 20th c.
Structuralism
Levi-Strauss. "Universal mental structures shape cultural symbols and narratives, often through binary oppositions." The shift is from cultural function to underlying cognitive structure.
Late 20th c.
Interpretive anthropology
Geertz. Culture as a system of shared symbols and meanings, with thick description as the method, and specific meanings valued over universal structures.
Late 20th c.
Organizational anthropology
Hofstede and Schein. Anthropological insight applied to organisations, with the recognition "that organizations, like societies, have deep cultural layers".
The same sketch appears three times that day

This six-stage path is reproduced in two further articles published the same day, once elaborated with primary works and dates and a seventh post-structuralist stage added, and once appended as a closing note. The repetition is the author working a passage rather than three independent statements of it.

Conclusion

Shared goals, incompatible methods: the connection is real and it is limited.

The verdict is the same shape as the Kahneman piece published five minutes earlier, which suggests the test rather than the answer is what the author was working out.

while there are some parallels between Hofstede's cultural dimensions and certain anthropological theories, especially in the shared goal of identifying underlying patterns or structures, the methodologies, focus, and applications differ.

— Hofstede's Theory and Levi-Strauss Structuralism, 27 September 2023
What survives the test
The shared goal of finding what lies beneath observable behaviour, which is enough to let Hofstede be read alongside anthropological theory rather than in isolation from it.
What does not
Any claim that Hofstede's dimensions are cognitive universals. They are patterns emerging from socio-cultural environments, and the article keeps them there.
What is left unresolved
The three critiques are recorded and not answered. The article neither defends Hofstede against overgeneralisation nor abandons him, which leaves the dimensions usable but qualified.
