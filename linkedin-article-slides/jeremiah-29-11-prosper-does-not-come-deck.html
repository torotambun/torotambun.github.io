When “Prosper” Does Not Come: Rereading Jeremiah 29:11 from Babylon to a Congregation Living with Economic Precarity

Jeremiah 29:11 cannot support a guarantee of individual material prosperity: it was addressed to a displaced Jewish community, its “you” is plural, its shalom means communal welfare, and its good future included seventy years of exile. What Christians can carry over is God’s faithfulness through adversity and the instructions for living while waiting, which give poor congregations practices rather than a promise that leaves continued poverty looking like failed faith.

Introduction

Read as a promise of personal prosperity, Jeremiah 29:11 fails both competing believers and congregations whose poverty persists.

For friends who serve as deacons and church leaders among materially precarious congregations.

Situation

"The usual reading seems straightforward. God has a plan, the plan is good, and the word “prosper” suggests that difficult circumstances will eventually give way to something better."

Complication

"Each can pray and quote Jeremiah 29:11, but the verse cannot guarantee the same indivisible outcome to everyone." And "If prosperity means material improvement, continued poverty creates a theological problem produced by the interpretation itself."

Question

Who received the promise, what future were they told to expect, and what can legitimately cross the distance to a Christian congregation today?

Answer

"My reading is that Jeremiah 29:11 cannot support a guarantee of individual material prosperity."

Framework

The letter addressed a displaced Jewish community, and its “you” is plural, so any personal application has to come after the communal address.

Historical, literary, lexical, canonical and practical readings each answer a different question. "Application comes after those steps because contemporary need cannot determine the ancient meaning in advance."

Jeremiah wrote to Judeans deported to Babylon, commonly linked to the surrender of Jehoiachin in 597 BCE. Wessels (2016) calls his response "prophetic realism". "Jeremiah was therefore not addressing someone deciding between two career opportunities or wondering whether a personal ambition would eventually receive divine approval."

"The “you” addressed in the letter is plural." Otherwise "the interpretation changes the referent from an exiled people to one modern individual without explaining why that change is legitimate."

Evidence

Jeremiah’s good future included seventy years in Babylon, long enough that many who heard the promise would die before the return.

Build houses, plant gardens, form families, increase, seek the welfare of the city. "Those instructions assume duration."

Other prophets forecast rapid restoration; Jeremiah opposed them and gave a horizon of seventy years. "The letter tells them to organize life around decades rather than months." Hildebrandt (2021) places the "good plans" inside that dispute.

"An adult deportee could hear Jeremiah's promise and die before the return." "If the good plan itself includes decades of exile, the English word “prosper” cannot simply mean rapid personal advancement."

On the seventy years

"Scholars debate how the seventy years should be calculated historically, but the rhetorical effect on the exiles is straightforward."

Details

Inside the chapter, shalom means communal welfare set against calamity, not wealth set against poverty.

The NIV translates shalom in verse 11 as “prosper”, a word with "a strong economic association."

The modern reading of “prosper”
✗To do well materially, professionally or socially
✗A promise of individual advancement
✗Immunity from harm
✗True for one believer only if another loses the same scarce outcome
Shalom in Jeremiah 29
✓Peace, welfare, security, well-ordered communal life (Fischer, 2015)
✓Verse 7: seek the shalom of Babylon, "The word already concerns collective social conditions."
✓Set against ra'ah, adversity or calamity, for people already deported
✓"Jeremiah 29:11 can therefore remain true while a particular Christian remains poor, loses a business opportunity, does not receive a promotion, or becomes seriously ill."

Gap

Christians inherited Jeremiah as Scripture, not ownership of every promise to Israel, and a Christian reading must not erase the first address.

"That history explains how Christians inherited the texts. It does not grant unrestricted ownership over every promise."

Christianity emerged within Judaism, and the New Testament itself rereads Israel’s language for Christian communities, as Liebengood (2023) shows for First Peter. Yet appropriation "has often developed into supersessionism", and "Jeremiah 29:11 provides a useful test of whether that mistake is being repeated on a smaller scale."

Moberly (2007) argues for Christian rereading that takes historical scholarship seriously. "Christian reception does not erase the earlier address."

Framework

What carries into Christian theology is God’s faithfulness through adversity and the community’s responsibility while it waits.

"The historical circumstances cannot be transferred intact." A congregation today is not exilic Judah, and a modern state is not Babylon.

01
Faithfulness
Adversity does not end the relationship
The exiles remain a community with a future after deportation destroyed much of their world.
02
Delay
A timetable is not a test of faith
"Delay cannot by itself be treated as evidence that God has failed."
03
Responsibility
Hope tied to present work
Build, grow food, preserve continuity and contribute to the welfare of the city, people outside their own community.
The New Testament horizon

Romans 8 and First Peter place suffering and vulnerability within hope "without promising political control or material security."

Implications

For a congregation living hand-to-mouth, Jeremiah supplies practices to begin before circumstances improve, formed from childhood.

"People with too few resources give urgent needs priority because those needs are real." "Jeremiah does more than tell the exiles to remain optimistic."

Build
Build while conditions remain
Stabilise households and educate children without waiting for ideal conditions.
Plant
Plant for a later harvest
Tie long horizons to visible intermediate steps, such as a child learning to read well.
Preserve
Preserve the next generation
Education, competence and service as part of communal identity, not only escape.
Seek
Seek the welfare of the society
"Prosperity is not a prerequisite for contribution."
Form
Form the mental model early
"A long-horizon communal identity cannot be created by quoting Jeremiah 29:11 during a crisis."
On scarcity research

Scarcity directs attention to urgent demands, while evidence for general cognitive reductions is mixed (de Bruijn & Antonides, 2022). Tikkun olam is a later concept and "should not be projected backward into Jeremiah."

Details

Wiesel guards against making suffering neat, so poverty must be treated neither as failed faith nor as a spiritual advantage.

"A theology of hope does not require suffering to become retrospectively good."

Night, Dawn and Day do not convert catastrophe into an orderly lesson (Levin, 2021), and Wiesel’s relationship with God resists simple classification (Quigley, 2021). "Poverty should not be romanticized as a spiritual advantage. Economic insecurity can damage education, health, opportunity, and family stability."

A smaller test: a friend whose years of public contribution brought no expected institutional opportunity. "Jeremiah does not turn prior contribution into a claim upon future recognition." The comparison holds only at the level of interpretation; "Missing an institutional opportunity is not equivalent to material insecurity."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Jeremiah gave the exiles instructions for living faithfully while restoration stayed distant, a reading that does not make poverty look like failed faith.

"Those practices do not solve poverty. They do prevent poverty from becoming the only category through which a congregation understands its future."

Jeremiah's exiles did not receive a promise that every individual would become prosperous. They received instructions for living faithfully during a period when restoration remained distant.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, When “Prosper” Does Not Come
For church leaders
"For church leaders serving poor congregations, that reading can be taught without making poverty look like a failure of faith."
What children need
Competence, a longer time horizon and contribution to society, understood as responsibilities "before material security arrives."
