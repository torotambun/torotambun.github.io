For Lifelong Learners. Not Everything Called a "Theory" Is One

Producing frameworks is legitimate professional work and an illegitimate claim to theory. The distinction is not academic snobbery: theory earns standing by surviving attack and by being produced through approaches designed to build it. An idea that diffuses through certification rather than refutation is a professional practice community, not a self-correcting body of theory.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

I brought industry frameworks into academia and was told they had no standing.

In my recent study, I made a mistake — and the scare quotes around that word are the author's own.

Situation

I was coming directly from industry. Producing frameworks was what I did professionally: I diagnosed problems, abstracted patterns, and packaged them into models that travelled.

Complication

So when I entered academia, I did the same. I produced frameworks and assumed this was legitimate scholarly work. "I was rebuked."

Question

I argued that this was how ideas worked in practice. What exactly was I being told I had got wrong?

Answer

That in an academic environment, "a framework cannot be claimed or promoted without a body of theory behind it. No lineage. No cumulative debate. No standing." I was reminded that "I had entered a different industry altogether. The research industry. Academic standards apply."

The Contrast

The scholars I learned from never claimed their frameworks were theories.

That correction forced a distinction I had not previously made, and it became sharper when I looked back at my own learning trajectory.

Over the years I had completed more than three dozen executive education courses across multiple leading institutions, including several long-form programs and numerous advanced modules taught by senior professors who authored influential books and developed widely used frameworks. I valued them and applied many in practice. But they were taught explicitly as tools for action and decision-making, not as theory.

What struck me later was this. Many of the frameworks I learned, admired and continue to use were developed by scholars who were careful about what they were and were not claiming. "They were presented as frameworks, methods, or lenses for action. They never insisted on being theories, and they never needed to. Their value came from clarity, usefulness, and intellectual honesty."

By contrast, a particular construct — one I was never formally taught in any executive program — later circulated globally under the explicit label of theory, accompanied by high-fee programs, certifications and affiliated alumni. It travelled widely outside academic settings, yet remained marginal within the scholarly community itself. "That contrast, between conceptual humility and theoretical overclaim, is what raised concern for me."

What is deliberately not named

The article does not identify the construct, its author, or the programs that certify it. That omission is the author's, and it is preserved here: no candidate is named, hinted at, or inferred in this deck.

The Research Onion

Theory is not declared; it emerges from approaches designed to build it.

In the Research Onion described by Mark Saunders and colleagues, "theory is not treated as an idea that can be declared."

It is something that emerges from specific research approaches designed for theory construction. Grounded theory, for example, is explicitly aimed at building theory from systematic data analysis.

By contrast, approaches such as phenomenology, ethnography or case study are designed to understand or describe phenomena. They may inform theory, but they are not themselves theory-building mechanisms.

As Jaccard and Jacoby show in their work on theory construction and model-building skills, diagrams or conceptual models only qualify as theory when constructs, relationships and explanatory logic are specified with enough precision for others to test, challenge, and potentially falsify them.

The Standards

Six standards separate a theory from a framework, and none of them is prestige.

This is what my professor was enforcing in that first semester. Methodological discipline.

1  ·  Falsifiability
"A theory must make claims that are, in principle, falsifiable." Empirical observation must be able to prove the theory wrong (Popper, 1959). "When failure can always be explained away by insufficient intention, awareness, or depth of practice, the claim is insulated from disconfirmation and falls outside the domain of theory."
2  ·  Clear and operationalisable constructs
Theories require explicitly defined constructs and relationships that can be examined, tested and replicated across studies (Merton, 1968; Whetten, 1989). Metaphorical or evocative language may support reflection, but without operational clarity there can be no cumulative research program.
3  ·  Scholarly contestation
Theory develops through sustained critique, revision and replacement over time (Kuhn, 1962). Mature theories attract critics and generate disagreement. Where an idea diffuses widely without producing serious scholarly contestation, it has not entered the theoretical arena.
4  ·  Category discipline
A framework may organise concepts or guide action, but it does not constitute theory unless it provides explanatory logic, specifies causal relationships and articulates boundary conditions (Whetten, 1989; Sutton & Staw, 1995). "Confusing intervention tools with explanatory theory is a category error."
5  ·  Evidence over authority
Scientific knowledge claims are evaluated on the basis of evidence and organised skepticism, not institutional prestige or authorial authority (Merton, 1968). "Reputation may accelerate diffusion, but it cannot substitute for peer challenge."
6  ·  Openness rather than credentialisation
"Theory advances through exposure to critique, not through affiliation, certification, or badges." When legitimacy is granted through participation rather than argument and refutation, "what emerges is a professional practice community, not a self-correcting body of theory" (Merton, 1973).

Conclusion

When you hear the word theory, pause before being impressed.

"Frameworks can be useful. Some travel widely. Some shape practice. That does not make them theory."

pause before being impressed. Not everything labelled as a theory has passed through the discipline required to become one.

How standing is actually earned
"In academia, ideas earn standing by surviving attack" and by being produced through recognised theory-building approaches. Neither condition can be met by announcement.
What the distinction is not
It is not a dismissal of frameworks. The author uses them, teaches them, and built a career on producing them. The objection is to the label, not to the work — and to what the label is used to sell.
Why it does not reverse
"That distinction, once learned, is irreversible." Some constructs are useful frameworks, some are practical methods, some are inspiring lenses. But not all of them, not even at the level of low-range theory, meet the standards that research requires.
