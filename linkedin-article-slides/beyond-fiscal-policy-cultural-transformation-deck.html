Beyond Fiscal Policy: Structural Barriers, Cultural Transformation, and Indonesia's Economic Future

A budget worth 17 percent of GDP is not primarily a tax problem. Tax reform, industrial policy and a sovereign wealth fund all address the symptom; the constraint underneath is a system in which political connection outranks merit, and beneath that a culture that prices short-term gain above long-term strategy. On the article's own estimate that takes two to three generations to move, which makes the cultural shift the leading item rather than the trailing one.

Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

Indonesia collects less of its economy than any of its neighbours, and everything downstream is rationed by it.

The piece begins from a conversation, not a dataset.

Situation

"A recent conversation with Prof. (Ret.) Djoko Suharto from ITB’s Mechanical Engineering Department about Indonesia’s budget ratio—hovering at around 17 percent of GDP—left me deep in thought."

Complication

"This figure is relatively small compared to other Southeast Asian developing nations" — the article gives Malaysia at 23 to 25 percent, Thailand 20 to 22, the Philippines 22 to 24 and Vietnam 28 to 30.

Question

"The limited fiscal capacity implied by this ratio constrains Indonesia’s ability to fund development programs, education, healthcare, infrastructure, and even the Free Nutritious Meals (MBG) project"

Answer

"A fiscal policy focused on deficit control and economic stability, combined with a narrow tax base and a low-deficit policy, has made the national budget less flexible."

The Constraint

Prudence and a narrow tax base close the same door from opposite sides.

"While the government strives to maintain a low deficit to avoid excessive debt risk, without increased state revenue, room for development expansion remains limited."

The escape route is blocked too. "industrialization, which could boost tax revenue, continues to progress slowly due to insufficient support for high-value-added manufacturing investments, limited research subsidies, and inadequate infrastructure."

"This challenge is further compounded by Indonesia’s narrow tax base and low compliance rate, which hinder the government’s ability to generate sufficient revenue for long-term development."

The scale of what the ratio has to cover is not abstract: the Free Nutritious Meals programme is "reportedly set to receive IDR 171 trillion in the 2025 state budget", sourced in the article to Kompas of 31 January 2025.

The Response

Four instruments are already in play, and the article calls them necessary but not sufficient.

"To address these fiscal constraints, the government has introduced a range of economic policies."

1  ·  Tax reform
Reforms "aim to broaden the tax base, improve compliance, and crack down on evasion, while digital and carbon taxes are being implemented to diversify revenue sources."
2  ·  Industrial policy
It "is being geared toward high-value-added manufacturing, yet progress remains slow due to regulatory uncertainty and a lack of institutional support for innovation."
3  ·  Off-balance-sheet financing
"Public-private partnerships (PPPs) and capital market instruments such as bonds and sukuk have been leveraged to finance infrastructure, reducing reliance on direct state spending."
4  ·  The sovereign wealth fund
Danantara "is positioned as a major fiscal innovation" and "seeks to mobilize capital, optimize state assets, and attract long-term investments into critical sectors such as infrastructure, energy, digital transformation, and industrial development." "However, its success is not guaranteed by financial strategy alone."
The models it is built against

Danantara is "Modeled after successful global funds such as Norway’s Government Pension Fund Global (GPFG) and Singapore’s Temasek Holdings". The article's appendix gives GPFG "assets exceeding $1.4 trillion" with "average annual returns of around 6% since inception", and Temasek "valued at $287 billion as of 2023" with "an annualized return of 14% since its inception in 1974".

Diagnosis

Three structural failures make any instrument underperform, and all three are the same failure.

"economic growth is fundamentally shaped by how institutions function and how incentives are structured."

BARRIER A
Merit is not the currency
"the elimination of meritocracy, where economic and political advancement is often determined by loyalty, personal networks, and political alignment rather than competence or performance." "Over time, talent is sidelined in favor of those who maintain the status quo, making economic transformation nearly impossible."
BARRIER B
Corruption is a mechanism, not a leak
It "is not just an economic inefficiency but a mechanism of power that preserves elite structures, sustains informal political deals, and enables rent-seeking behaviors that suffocate emerging industries." "economic decision-making is often dictated by who benefits politically rather than what benefits national long-term development."
BARRIER C
Policy protects incumbents
"state policies are shaped to protect entrenched business elites rather than encourage a competitive, high-growth economy." The result is "a political-industrial structure where influence determines opportunity rather than innovation or efficiency."
BARRIER D
And these are symptoms too
"No policy measure can succeed if Indonesia remains trapped in a system where political connections override meritocracy" — but "even these structural failures are symptoms, not the root cause. They stem from a deeper cultural challenge embedded in the national mindset."

Conclusion

On the article's own timescale the cultural shift takes two to three generations, which is why it cannot be the last step.

"At its core, economic development is a reflection of cultural values."

Culture, not policy, determines whether a nation thrives or stagnates.

— Beyond Fiscal Policy
What defeats good policy
Policies "will remain ineffective in a society where short-term gains are prioritized over long-term strategy, where innovation is met with resistance rather than encouragement, and where government intervention is seen as a substitute for individual economic responsibility."
The timescale
"History shows that deep cultural shifts require at least 50–75 years (2–3 generations) to fully materialize. Sustainable development is impossible without cultural transformation." The figure is asserted in the article without a source, and no source has been supplied here.
What reform is actually measured in
"For Indonesia to transcend its fiscal limitations, it must first transcend its cultural constraints. Real reform is not measured in financial figures or legislative changes alone." And the constitutional frame: "If Indonesia seeks to truly fulfill the mandate of the 1945 Constitution to achieve the people’s welfare, then technical reforms must go hand in hand with long-term cultural transformation."
