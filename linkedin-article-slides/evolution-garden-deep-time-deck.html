What the Evolution Garden Taught Me About Geology, Energy and Deep Time

A garden that refuses to separate rocks from plants makes one claim that a career in oil and gas never had to face: life did not only adapt to Earth, life is what made Earth habitable. Coal is that same biological time, buried. In the field geology stays operational. In a garden it becomes existential — and the last sign turns admiration into a warning.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

I worked above deep time for years without ever studying it.

A regret that has, in the author's own words, slowly become an obsession.

Situation

Oil and gas people drill, log, test, produce and calculate reserves. They speak of formations, traps, source rocks, reservoirs, seals and migration. The vocabulary is complete and the work is competent.

Complication

"Oil and gas people live above time without always understanding time." Beneath the technical words lies something older and more humbling: Earth as a long biological and geological memory.

Question

What does a person who spent a career extracting from the subsurface learn when the same history is laid out above ground, as plants?

Answer

That the subsurface is not only a technical system. It is accumulated Earth history — and the energy economy is the use of ancient biological time.

The Frame

The garden refuses to separate the rocks from the plants growing on them.

The design decision that makes the Evolution Garden at Singapore Botanic Gardens an argument rather than a display.

The rocks are not merely decorative. The plants are not merely botanical specimens. Both are placed inside one continuous story, so that the visitor cannot read geology in one frame and biology in another.

The garden quietly says that "vegetation is geological history made visible." That single move is what allows everything downstream — coal, energy, extinction — to be read as one sequence instead of as separate disciplines.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Sequence

It begins not with life but with a planet that could not hold it.

The garden's own order of milestones, read as it is walked. The first lesson is not life. It is absence.

4,600 Ma
A Lifeless Earth
A young planet with water vapour, hydrogen cyanide, methane and ammonia. No oxygen to breathe. No ozone layer against ultraviolet rays. Oceans had formed, but life could not yet survive. Earth had to become habitable before life could become visible.
3,500 Ma
The Beginnings of Life
Primitive bacteria developed photosynthesis, producing complex molecules from light energy, with oxygen as a by-product. Stromatolites, formed by colonial organisms secreting calcium carbonate, became ancient fossils — the first rock record written by living things.
450 Ma
The Invasion of the Land
Land plants first appeared. Algae and liverworts grew near rivers; small stick-like plants without roots or leaves followed. By 380 million years ago, primitive ancestors of clubmosses, spike-mosses and horsetails had covered the land.
300 Ma
The Great Coal Swamps
Huge swamps dominated by giant lycopods laid down thick peat deposits, later fossilised as coal. Coal from that age became one of today's major energy sources. Then the climate warmed and the swamps disappeared.
245–65 Ma
The Age of Cycads
A warm Mesozoic without polar ice caps. Cycads and conifers dominated the plant world while dinosaurs dominated the animal one. The garden remembers the vegetation the popular imagination forgets: dinosaurs did not walk through empty scenery.
The First Flowers
Ancestry still uncertain
Flowering plants share ancestry with other seed plants, but their closest relatives remain uncertain. A magnolia-like plant was once considered primitive; more recent thinking proposed an ancestral waterlily. The sign says so rather than settling it.
Today
The Lowland Tropical Rain Forest
Described on the final sign as the most diverse plant community that has ever existed in billions of years of life on Earth. The walk ends not in admiration but in a warning about clearing it.
What the garden does not pretend

The uncertainty about flowering-plant ancestry is left standing on the sign. "It shows science as disciplined revision." Old explanations are tested, new evidence changes the argument, and even in a garden knowledge remains provisional.

The Reversal

Life did not only adapt to Earth; life is what made Earth breathable.

The claim at the centre of the walk, and the one that reverses the common assumption.

Photosynthesis was not merely a plant function. It changed the planet. Oxygen did not simply exist as a background condition against which organisms competed — life helped create it. Living organisms began to alter the atmosphere and to leave a rock record while doing it.

For someone who once worked in oil and gas, this is where the frame breaks. Geological formations are not dead material. They can be the residue of ancient life, ancient water, ancient climate and ancient chemistry. "A rock can be a memory of organisms."

The same reversal governs the land. Once plants entered it, they altered soil, water movement, erosion, atmosphere and habitat. Land was no longer bare geology. It became living geology.

The sentence that carries it

"Life did not only adapt to Earth. Life changed Earth." Everything the garden says afterwards about coal, energy and extinction depends on that order being correct.

What It Costs

Modern industry burns ancient biological time.

The lesson the author calls severe, and the one that lands hardest on a career spent in the subsurface.

Every tonne of coal carries a buried botanical past.

— What the Evolution Garden Taught Me About Geology, Energy and Deep Time
Coal is not an abstract commodity
"Coal is not just coal. It is ancient vegetation compressed into energy." It is swamp, peat, burial, heat, pressure and time — fossilised plant abundance, priced as a fuel.
The energy economy is partly built from dead forests
That is the author's own formulation of the consequence. The industry that measures reserves in barrels and tonnes is drawing down a stock that a hundred million years of vegetation laid down.
Extraction and history are the same object
Coal, oil, gas, vegetation, climate and extinction belong to one long story. Treating the subsurface as a purely technical system is what allows that story to be extracted without being read.

The Warning

The most diverse plant community in Earth history is the one now being cleared.

The final sign is where the garden stops being a museum. The sign does not end in admiration.

What the Walk Invites
✓Admiration for a tropical rain forest as ordinary greenery
✓Deep time as a sequence safely concluded
✓Plant life as background scenery to an animal story
✓Geology as a technical system for locating resources
What the Last Sign Actually Says
✗The rain forest is the highest achievement of plant diversity in Earth history
✗Widespread clearing threatens extinctions on a scale not seen since geological events
✗The comparison offered is the event that exterminated the dinosaurs
✗The sequence is still running, and human action is now inside it
Where the walk turns

The garden begins with a lifeless Earth and moves through bacteria, oxygen, stromatolites, land plants, coal swamps, ferns, cycads, flowers and rain forest — showing at every step that plant life helped make Earth breathable, habitable, fertile, energetic and diverse. Then it says what can be lost.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
What I Carry

In the field geology is operational; in a garden it becomes existential.

Why the delayed recognition may have had its own value.

The rocks hold time. The plants reveal time. The rain forest warns what time can lose.

— What the Evolution Garden Taught Me About Geology, Energy and Deep Time
The question changes
It is no longer only about where resources are found. It becomes about how Earth became alive, how life changed Earth, and how human action can now damage what took billions of years to form.
The regret is not resolved
Geology should have been studied more deeply, earlier. The piece does not withdraw that. It argues only that recognition arriving late in a botanic garden reaches somewhere the field never required it to go.
