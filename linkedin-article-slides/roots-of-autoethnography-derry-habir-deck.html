Roots of Autoethnography in My "I": Derry Habir, Clair de Lune, and the Unfolding Coincidences

A 2000 MBA course under a diplomat's son planted the seeds of a research method not yet named, and case-method discipline, three organizational-behavior frameworks, and a musical metaphor converge on autoethnography's essential quality: revealing meaning without over-explaining it.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A 2000 MBA course under Prof. Derry Habir planted the seeds of a method not yet named.

My baby step journey into autoethnography has roots in a pivotal experience during my MBA studies in 2000.

Prof. Derry Habir, a mentor with a rich background, introduced me to a course in Organizational Behavior. Prof. Derry's story is intertwined with Indonesia's diplomatic history; coming from a diplomat family, his family joined the foreign service shortly after independence, giving him a wealth of cultural insights from living abroad. These memories were more than anecdotes; they embodied how personal experiences are shaped by historical and cultural contexts — the beginning of understanding that narratives serve as conduits between the personal and the societal.

In the classroom, Prof. Habir applied the Harvard Business School case study method with rigor, emphasizing that analysis should be based solely on the information available in the text, without introducing assumptions. This lesson in disciplined analysis became a touchstone in my later work, reminding me that, whether analyzing case studies or my own memories, one must remain mindful of the boundaries between observation and interpretation.

Framework

Four case-study frameworks shaped an eye for unspoken influence long before autoethnography had a name.

Each framework, encountered through a specific Harvard case, later resurfaced as a tool for reading his own narrative.

1  ·  Schein's model of artifacts, values and assumptions
Opened my eyes to the layered nature of organizational culture — an approach to seeing beyond the surface that is essential in autoethnography's sensitivity to hidden assumptions.
2  ·  McCaskey's Workgroup Framework (via the "Slade Company" case)
Became a cornerstone of my analytical toolkit, revisited years later in Prof. Linda Hill's class at HBS, where it took on new dimensions.
3  ·  Gabarro's "assumptions, perceptions, and behavior" (via "Bob Knowlton")
A one-to-one resolution tool I have used many times to mediate conflicts among subordinates, with supervisors, and between departments.
4  ·  Gabarro and Harlan's "Process Observation"
Taught the importance of observing who participates, how often, and to what effect — identifying social leaders whose influence remains unspoken and unrecorded in organizational charts. This became crucial across a 16-year sales career, and resurfaced later as the anthropological method of participant observation.

Details

A 2023 doctoral setback in anthropology made the continuity between these frameworks explicit.

Gabarro and Harlan's emphasis on influence and participation mirrored the anthropological methods of closely observing cultural practices.

As I engaged with James P. Spradley's Participant Observation and The Ethnographic Interview, along with Charlotte Aull Davies' Reflexive Ethnography, I couldn't help but recall the foundations laid by Gabarro and Harlan's "Process Observation." This phase highlighted the continuity between my past and present learning, showing how foundational principles of observation carry through into more advanced methods of self-reflection and cultural analysis.

This convergence deepened at MIT from 2016 to 2018, where the Three Lenses Framework — political, cultural, and structural — reinforced the need to analyze group dynamics from multiple perspectives. I hadn't realized that the "Process Observation" approach had materialized at MIT as the Martin Trust Center's doctrine of Primary Market Research, which emphasizes immersive understanding of market needs.

Evidence

A musical metaphor captures the method's essential quality: revealing without over-explaining.

Autoethnography's essence is much like experiencing Claude Debussy's Clair de Lune — French for "moonlight."

The composition shifts one's perception, moving from the clarity of sunlight to the subtle, silvery touch of moonlight. Autoethnography captures this same quality, providing a way of seeing experiences through a softer, more introspective lens — inviting readers to see the subtleties of lived experience in a different light, revealing what might otherwise remain unseen.

This aligns with Ferdinand de Saussure's post-structuralist insight that meaning is not fixed but shaped by context. Rather than being constrained to a single truth, autoethnography opens up a space where multiple interpretations coexist, each reading revealing new layers of insight — much like listening to Clair de Lune, where each listen might uncover a different emotion or depth.

Implications

The piece closes with its own explicit "So What?" accounting, mapping five ways the personal story matters.

The narrative's own annotation makes the "So What?" visible rather than implicit.

By framing Prof. Habir's background in Indonesia's diplomatic history and his teaching methods, the piece suggests a deeper cultural resonance — how his life and teaching reflect broader cultural and historical dynamics in Indonesia. Drawing connections between the analytical frameworks learned through the Harvard case method and an autoethnographic approach shows how these early experiences shaped an understanding of analysis and reflexivity.

The narrative also implies that this personal story is a window into how global educational trends intersect with local traditions — a reflection of larger shifts in Indonesia's educational landscape, not just an individual journey. And in revisiting frameworks like "Process Observation" during doctoral studies, it highlights the continuity and evolution of research methods and their impact on personal and professional growth.

Conclusion

Every experience holds a lesson, and every reflection is an opportunity for growth.

My story is not unique, but it is mine, and through autoethnography, I hope to offer it as a contribution to the broader dialogue about culture, identity, and change.

This ongoing journey reminds us that autoethnography is never truly complete. It is a practice of continually revisiting our stories, finding new meanings, and sharing them in the hope that they will resonate with others — a testament to the belief that our stories matter, not just for what they say about us, but for what they reveal about the world we live in.
