When Academic Authority Preaches AI Without Evidence or Competence

The moral case for AI inclusion is valid and the message making it does not survive checking. Access is not capability: a country can have millions of users and no ability to inspect, govern or replace the systems they use. That the message came from a vice rector is not incidental — it is what students will copy.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A vice rector circulated a case for AI inclusion that its own evidence cannot carry.

A friend forwarded a long WhatsApp message written by a vice rector of a well-known university. Its subject was close to an argument I developed in 2023 after a class discussion with Prof. Dodi.

Situation

The message argues that artificial intelligence should not remain a privilege for people with strong education, reliable internet access, and enough money to use advanced systems. Fishermen should receive better weather information. Farmers should have access to market data. Children in remote regions should benefit from educational resources. People without university qualifications should also be able to enter advanced intellectual discussions.

Complication

"The argument has a clear moral appeal because unequal access to technology can reinforce existing inequality. Its weakness lies in treating wider access as evidence of empowerment while giving much less attention to the national capability required to understand, verify, govern, and control the systems being used." Its factual foundation also requires scrutiny: "Verified events, loose paraphrases, commercial forecasts, speculative scenarios, and unverified statistics are presented with nearly the same certainty."

Question

If the moral concern is right and the evidence is not, what exactly does the argument establish — and why does the author's position make the answer sharper?

Answer

It establishes that access is expanding. It does not establish national capability, which is a different variable that adoption does not move. And because a vice rector helps define an institution's intellectual standard, the manner of the argument teaches more than its content.

Gap

Access shows that people can reach an interface, not that a country can govern one.

A country may have millions of AI users while possessing little independent AI capability.

"Usage shows that people can access an interface. It does not establish that the country can shape the technology, direct it towards national priorities, or continue functioning when a foreign provider changes its prices, policies, technical standards, or access conditions."

Indonesia may become a large market for ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and other systems without developing comparable strength in research, computing infrastructure, data governance, model evaluation, cybersecurity, or domestic technological production. "Citizens may become competent users of foreign platforms while universities, government institutions, companies, and researchers remain unable to inspect the systems, test their assumptions, or replace them."

"A country does not need to be occupied physically before part of its decision-making independence is lost." Government agencies, universities, companies, schools, and researchers may depend increasingly on systems built and governed elsewhere, and foreign institutions can then influence how problems are framed, which data are prioritised, and which solutions appear reasonable.

Core Gap

"The institutions still issue the final decisions, but part of the intellectual work may already have been transferred to systems whose assumptions they cannot examine."

The Risk Is Not Only Wrong Answers

"Incorrect answers are only one part of the risk. Institutions may gradually lose the human capability required to recognise when an answer is wrong. Once that capability has weakened, dependence becomes harder to reverse."

Framework

National weakness appears in eight capabilities, and usage measures none of them.

National weakness in AI appears when a country cannot do these eight things. Every one of them can fail while adoption rises.

1  ·  Define its own problems
"National capability begins with the ability to define problems independently." A government agency that asks a foreign model to frame policy options may accept categories and assumptions developed outside Indonesia. A university that relies on AI to generate research questions may begin with machine-produced priorities before deciding whether those priorities matter locally.
2  ·  Produce original knowledge
Indonesia may have many AI users while employing too few researchers capable of developing models, maintaining too few laboratories with adequate computing resources, and allocating too little funding for long-term research. "Universities may gradually become consumers of foreign systems instead of producers of original methods, datasets, and theories."
3  ·  Govern its data
AI requires reliable, well-governed and contextually accurate information. A country with fragmented public records, inconsistent standards, poor interoperability and inadequate protection of sensitive information "may provide raw data to foreign platforms and receive processed intelligence in return while remaining unable to reproduce or audit the process that generated the result."
4  ·  Maintain critical infrastructure
Advanced models require specialised chips, cloud capacity, energy, software and technical maintenance. "When these remain under foreign control, access depends on external prices, export restrictions, geopolitical relations, and corporate decisions. A system available today may become more expensive, restricted, or unavailable tomorrow."
5  ·  Train competent professionals
"A population may know how to prompt AI without knowing how to test its output." Teachers may use generated material without sufficient subject knowledge to detect errors; civil servants may accept machine summaries because they lack the time or training to examine the underlying evidence. "The country may become efficient at operating the tool while remaining weak at judging what the tool produces."
6  ·  Regulate powerful systems
"Government cannot regulate AI effectively when regulators do not understand how models are trained, how data cross borders, how automated decisions affect citizens, or how responsibility should be assigned when systems fail." Rules written without technical understanding may leave practical control with model owners, cloud providers and foreign firms.
7  ·  Capture economic value
Indonesia may contribute users, language data, behavioural data and market growth "while the largest financial returns remain with foreign owners of models, infrastructure, and intellectual property. High adoption may strengthen foreign technology companies more than it strengthens domestic research, industry, or employment."
8  ·  Preserve independent judgement
A university cannot measure progress only by the number of students using AI. "It must also ask whether those students can locate original sources, recognise weak logic, distinguish evidence from prediction, and reject a fluent answer when the reasoning fails."

The Fisherman

The forecast helps the fisherman without making Indonesia able to build, inspect or replace it.

The fisherman in the WhatsApp message illustrates the immediate benefit of access. A weather application may help him determine whether conditions are safe enough to go to sea. This may protect income and save lives.

What the Example Establishes
✓A weather application can tell him whether it is safe to go to sea
✓That benefit is real, and it may protect income and save lives
✓Unequal access to it would reinforce existing inequality
What It Leaves Unanswered
✗Whether the data represent local coastal conditions accurately
✗Whether the forecast has been validated, and by whom
✗Who controls the platform and how his data are processed
✗Whether the service continues when the provider changes commercial policy
The Same Structure Appears in Education

An AI system may give a student a quick and convincing explanation, which helps where qualified teachers are scarce. "Repeated reliance on machine-generated answers can still weaken education when students have not learned how to define a problem, examine evidence, identify unsupported claims, and form an independent conclusion." Students can become skilled at requesting answers while remaining weak at judging them.

Evidence

Confirmed facts, corporate forecasts and untraceable numbers arrive with equal confidence.

Several claims in the message have genuine sources. The mathematical event happened. Gartner issued a related forecast. DeepMind published a substantial safety paper. The failure appears in how these materials are handled.

CHECKED A
The Liam Price case, simplified
Price prompted GPT-5.4 Pro on Erdős Problem #1196 and the output contributed to the resolution; the paper has eight authors including Price, Terence Tao and Jared Duker Lichtman. "Access created the opportunity, while mathematical competence and professional verification established the credibility of the result." The paper calls the method new; the message calls it a ninety-year-old method.
CHECKED B
The timeline, given false precision
Gartner forecast 40 percent of enterprise applications with integrated “task-specific” AI agents by 2026 — the message keeps the percentage and drops the qualifier — and separately predicted more than 40 percent of agentic AI projects cancelled by end-2027, which the message omits. "Some are commercial forecasts. Some are corporate targets. Some are safety scenarios. Others cannot be traced."
CHECKED C
The Indonesian statistics, unsupported
350 million monthly generative-AI users attributed to Statista with no dataset, date or definition; 27 percent of Indonesian internet users attributed to APJII with no table, page or survey question; a national AI-literacy score of 49.96 out of 100. "I could not find a validated national AI-literacy index reporting that figure." Each remains unverified pending the original source.
CHECKED D
The claim I am not making
"I would not describe every questionable statement as a hallucination because several can be traced to genuine sources. The more serious problem is that confirmed facts, loose paraphrases, commercial forecasts, personal interpretations, and unverified numbers are presented with almost equal confidence." This pattern commonly appears when chatbot output enters a draft without disciplined checking.

Implications

When a vice rector publishes this, students learn that fluent prose is enough.

A vice rector helps define the intellectual standard of an institution. Students observe how academic leaders use evidence, distinguish fact from illustration, and revise AI-assisted prose.

The message carries familiar chatbot traits. "It relies on dramatic short sentences to create emphasis. It moves through famous names without examining their arguments. It uses fishermen, farmers, and children in remote regions as moral symbols. It presents a future timeline with more certainty than the evidence allows. It connects distant subjects through polished transitions without establishing the relationships among them."

"Senior academics may use AI. Research and writing practices are already changing. Academic responsibility still requires every claim to be checked, every source to be understood, and every connection to survive scrutiny. The final text must reflect the author's own judgement."

"When a vice rector circulates writing that appears heavily shaped by chatbot habits and insufficiently revised, students receive a damaging lesson. They may conclude that fluent prose, moral confidence, famous names, and precise numbers are enough. They may mistake the appearance of research for research itself."

Neither Author Nor Institution Is Named

The article identifies the writer only as a vice rector of a well-known university and does not name the person or the institution. That restraint is part of the argument: the failure being examined is a practice, not a personality, and it is common enough that naming one holder of it would narrow the point.

Conclusion

Adopt in parallel, and build the capability to judge what the machine produces.

My 2023 proposal was parallel action: engage with available technology while developing the foundations required to remain intellectually and institutionally independent. Indonesia does not need to wait until every condition is perfect.

1
Strengthen reasoning before dependence forms
"Education must strengthen reading, writing, mathematics, reasoning, and the ability to question evidence before students become dependent on machine-generated answers."
2
Invest in research capacity, not platform subscriptions
"Universities must invest in research capacity instead of treating foreign platforms as substitutes for scholarship." A university's progress is not the count of students using AI.
3
Build data quality and regulatory competence together
"Government must improve data quality, technical competence, and regulatory understanding." Rules written without technical understanding leave practical control with model owners and cloud providers.
4
Develop local expertise rather than importing finished systems
"Industry must develop local expertise rather than importing finished systems without understanding their limits." Foreign relationships should contribute to domestic capability rather than replace it.
5
Treat local-language access as usability, not sovereignty
"A model may speak Indonesian, Javanese, Bugis, or another local language while remaining technically, commercially, and institutionally controlled abroad. Local-language access improves usability, but it does not create technological sovereignty."

Achieving that balance requires institutions capable of thinking before the machine answers, checking after it answers, and continuing to think when the machine is unavailable.

— The article's closing sentence
