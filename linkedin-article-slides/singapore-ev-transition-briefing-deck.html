Singapore's Electric Vehicle Transition: A Tactical Approach to Reducing Global Temperatures

This is the record of one evening's briefing, written before it became an argument. What it shows is a state that treats electrification as a tactic inside a land and demographic constraint — walking, cycling and rail first, vehicles second — and a writer who left the room with a private question the article does not answer.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

The briefing opened with 6.9 million people and 12 percent of the land under roads.

Notes from a fireside chat on 12 September 2024 with Jeremy Yap, Deputy CEO of the Singapore Land and Transport Authority.

The framing was demographic and spatial before it was environmental. Singapore's population is expected to reach 6.9 million by 2030, and one in four Singaporeans will be over 65 — a manpower problem as much as an infrastructural one, since an ageing population still requires qualified drivers while the country manages its reliance on foreign labour.

Twelve percent of Singapore's land is already dedicated to roads. As the population becomes more affluent, demand for better transport services rises, straining infrastructure that cannot be extended by simply adding more of it.

That is the constraint the electric vehicle programme sits inside. Nothing in the evening presented electrification as an answer to the climate; it was presented as one instrument for a country running out of room.

The Frame

Land transport is the third-largest emitter at 15 percent, which makes electrifying it a tactic.

Two strategies were named, and their order is the article's own.

Land transport is Singapore's third-largest carbon emitter, accounting for 15 percent of national emissions. Two strategies address it. The first is to encourage more people to walk, cycle and take public transport, by improving those networks and increasing accessibility. The second is a shift to cleaner-energy vehicles, particularly electric ones, which halve carbon emissions compared with internal combustion engines.

Halving the emissions of one sector that produces 15 percent of the total is a meaningful move and a bounded one. The article's own title calls it tactical, and the ordering of the two strategies — mode share first, vehicle technology second — says the same thing structurally.

Details

The plan puts walking, cycling and rail first, and cleaner vehicles second.

The Land Transport Master Plan 2040, as presented.

1  ·  20-Minute Towns and a 45-Minute City
Residents should travel within their town in twenty minutes and reach the city within forty-five, supported by a well-connected and fast transport network. The target is time, not vehicle type.
2  ·  Healthy Lives, Safer Journeys
Safer, healthier and more liveable environments with vibrant community spaces — the plan's second stated vision, alongside Transport for All, an inclusive ecosystem with supportive infrastructure for the elderly and disabled.
3  ·  Walk, Cycle, Ride as the primary mode
By 2030 the rail network expands to 360 kilometres and cycling paths to 1,300 kilometres; by 2040, 350 kilometres of sheltered walkways connect transport nodes, and eight in ten households sit within a ten-minute walk of a train station.
4  ·  The 2040 mode-share target
By 2030, public transport mode share during peak hours rises to 75 percent and half the public bus fleet is electrified. By 2040, 90 percent of journeys rely on walking, cycling or riding — which is where the largest share of the reduction is expected to come from.

The Sequence

Registrations moved from 0.2 percent electric in 2020 to 32 percent by mid-2024.

Cars and buses, year by year, as reported at the briefing.

2018
Sixty buses, bought to be tested
Bus electrification began with the procurement of 60 electric buses, designed to test operational efficiency in a tropical climate and to experiment with charging methods including pantograph charging.
2019
A fleet commitment, and the certifications to match
Singapore committed to a 100 percent cleaner-energy public bus fleet by 2040, and updated technical specialist certifications to accommodate electric bus operations.
2020
First buses on the road; 0.2 percent of vehicles electric
The first batch of electric buses entered service in the year when only 0.2 percent of the country's vehicles were electric.
2021
3.8 percent
Electric vehicles climbed to 3.8 percent, the first year in which the share stopped being a rounding error.
2022
12 percent of vehicle registrations
The share of new registrations reached double figures — the point at which charging infrastructure, rather than vehicle supply, becomes the operative question.
2023
18 percent, 360 more buses, and a battery unit
Registrations reached 18 percent. A further 360 electric buses were procured, and an in-house EV battery unit was established to address safety and standards issues.
Mid-2024
32 percent of car registrations, and 44 percent of light goods vehicles
The proportion of electric cars in registrations reached 32 percent — a 150-fold increase since 2020 — with 44 percent of newly registered light goods vehicles electric, and a battery-swapping sandbox introduced for motorcycles.

Details

The binding constraint is overnight charging and the grid, not the vehicles.

Where the plan concentrates its effort.

REPORTED A
Overnight residential charging is the design case
Shared residential slow charging overnight is the primary mode for most drivers, with a full charge needed only every five to seven days on a 48-kilometre daily drive — deliberately reducing stress on the electricity grid.
REPORTED B
Fast charging is the exception, not the default
Fast charging complements the overnight case, particularly for fleet and taxi vehicles with high usage, with deployment planned alongside smart charging to reduce infrastructure strain.
REPORTED C
Density already achieved
As of 2023 Singapore had 6,500 publicly accessible charging points, the highest density in Southeast Asia — one charger for every three electric vehicles — against a target of up to 40,000 public chargers by 2030.
REPORTED D
The readiness programme behind the numbers
By 2025 all HDB towns will be EV-ready. A Centre of Excellence oversees government charger deployment, engineering studies determine the electrical upgrades required, the EV Common Charger Grant co-funds private installations, and the threshold for deployment in private condominiums has been lowered to 50 percent.

The Ecosystem

Coordination is what the National Electric Vehicle Centre exists to do.

The transition is organised as a stakeholder problem rather than a technology one.

The National Electric Vehicle Centre coordinates carpark owners — HDB, URA and private developers — with regulators including the Land Transport Authority, the Energy Market Authority and the Singapore Civil Defence Force, and with supporting agencies such as the Economic Development Board, Enterprise Singapore, A*STAR, NUS and NTU. Its second remit is capability building: working with research institutes and universities to develop the technical expertise that drives both innovation and regulation.

The wider ecosystem is named in the same register: charger installers including ComfortDelGro, HAIKIM and Schneider Electric; operators such as EVCO; equipment from ABB and Delta; batteries from Envision and ABB; insurers including Singlife and Budget Direct; and fleet owners such as Grab and ComfortDelGro converting their fleets.

The workforce is treated as infrastructure too. The National EV Safety Specialist certification equips workshop technicians to work safely on high-voltage systems, and a separate course prepares electrical workers for charger certification and inspection. Engagement runs outward as well — bilateral exchanges with global partners, and work with Malaysia on cross-border EV drives.

Where the incentives sit

The cost gap is closed by policy: an Early Adoption Incentive discounting the Additional Registration Fee, a Vehicle Emission Scheme paying rebates on less polluting cars and taxis, a revised Category A power threshold, and combined road tax bands. These are the instruments the author later carried into his Jakarta Post column on what Indonesia might adapt.

The New Risk

A new kind of fire arrived with the batteries and needed its own taskforce.

The part of the transition that had no precedent to borrow.

Electric vehicle fires behave differently from petrol and diesel ones: they burn longer, require more water to extinguish, and can reignite. Singapore's response was an interagency EV Battery Safety Taskforce with the Civil Defence Force, working on incident response frameworks, infrastructure readiness and public awareness, alongside a dedicated battery unit and controlled-environment sandboxes for demonstrating new technologies.

The regulatory layer arrived with it. The Electric Vehicle Charging Act, taking effect in December 2023, permits only approved chargers, licenses charging operators to guarantee service quality, and requires new developments to include charging infrastructure. Technical Reference 25, developed by a public-private working group led by the Land Transport Authority, covers electrical safety and general charging requirements and is updated every three years, with the next revision due in 2026 and possibly covering mobile and wireless charging.

One detail from the evening is worth keeping for its method rather than its content: the overnight residential charging masterplan came from observing drivers overseas, who charge predominantly at home even where public chargers are available at offices and in public spaces. The plan was built from behaviour, not from capacity.

The Tension

Six days later I told an audience that all this buys one-tenth of a degree.

The same author, the same week, two different scales of claim — set side by side here, since the article itself does not make the comparison.

What the briefing establishes
•Land transport is 15 percent of Singapore's national emissions
•Electric vehicles halve emissions against internal combustion engines
•The transition is dated, funded and sequenced to 2040
•Annual transport emissions to fall by up to 2 million tonnes from the 2016 peak of 7.7 million
What the simulator says at global scale
•Electrifying all transport worldwide lowers temperature by about 0.1°C
•Subsidising every renewable globally adds roughly another 0.1°C
•Single levers behave this way because of feedback loops and delay
•Which is why the article's own title calls this approach tactical

Closing

The question I left the evening with was whether I could buy the Mercedes.

The article ends on a personal note and does not resolve it.

— The article's closing personal note, 13 September 2024
Two models, listed without comment
The EQE 300 SUV and the EQB 250+ are named at the end of the piece, with no argument for or against either, and no answer to the question the note poses.
Why the note is not a throwaway
An evening of state planning — targets, chargers, legislation, a fire taskforce — lands on a private purchasing decision. The gap between the two scales is the piece, and it is left open.
