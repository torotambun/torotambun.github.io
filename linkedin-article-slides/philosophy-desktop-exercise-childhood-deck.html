(3 of 5 Part 2) Philosophy "desktop" exercise: "I wish I could return to our childhood?"

Where the previous instalment surveyed the frameworks, this one runs them. The same sentence is put through eight traditions in turn, and the ninth is deliberately held back: theology reasons from revelation, philosophy from questioning, and putting them in the same column would flatten the difference. What the exercise finally establishes is not an answer but a rule — that a philosophical question detached from the fear that produced it becomes a geometry problem.

Opening

The frameworks have been surveyed; now the sentence goes through them one at a time.

"Let’s begin the desktop exercise using the question:"

Situation

"Can we return to our childhood?"

Complication

"Let's walk it step by step through all nine minus one frameworks."

Question

Which of the eight can take the sentence seriously without either mocking it or granting it?

Answer

Each returns a different question rather than a different answer, and the differences are the finding.

The Exercise

Eight traditions each convert the wish into a question only that tradition can ask.

The article works each framework with its own key question and its own thinkers.

1  ·  Ontology
"This is not just a question of time. It’s a question of being." Heidegger: "childhood" "is not a place we were — it’s a way we once were in being-in-the-world." Bergson: "You may not return to “childhood” as a point on a timeline, but you might access it as a quality of inner experience".
2  ·  Epistemology
"How do I know my childhood — and can I trust that knowledge?" Ricoeur: "Memory is fragile, always subject to distortion, but essential for identity." Merleau-Ponty: "Childhood is not "stored" as facts, but as felt traces in the body and sensation." Hume: "It’s not a stable entity — it’s a remembered cluster, ever-changing."
3  ·  Axiology
"Why do I want to return — and what does this say about what I value?" Scheler on layered value hierarchies; MacIntyre: "The modern world has lost coherent moral narratives." Weil: "The absence of rootedness is the deepest poverty."
4  ·  Continental philosophy
The question "becomes not only a personal inquiry, but a cultural, linguistic, and historical one." Derrida deconstructs the binaries the question already assumes: "Is “childhood” a pure origin?" "Or is it already a construct, shaped by present needs?" "Does the very act of naming “childhood” already distort it?"
5  ·  Analytic philosophy
Committed to "Breaking complex concepts into simple components", "Clarifying definitions", "Testing logical coherence". Its challenge: "Is your question well-formed? Are you saying something meaningful — or just emotionally evocative?" — and first, "What do you mean by “return”? Physically? Psychologically? Temporally?"
6  ·  Virtue ethics
"A tradition not primarily concerned with logic or language, but with the formation of character". The wish "is heard as a moral signal — a longing that reveals something about the kind of person you are, or hope to become." Its question: "What kind of soul desires to return — and what virtues (or vices) does that reveal?"
7  ·  Existentialism
Here the question is "not analyzed for logical structure or moral coherence, but felt as a cry from within the human condition." "Am I trying to escape the anguish of responsibility by imagining a return?" "Do I want to hide from freedom behind the comfort of being cared for?" Kierkegaard sees the self as "a dynamic relation — not a fixed identity, but a becoming".
8  ·  Pragmatism
"a uniquely action-driven tradition that asks not, “Is this true in the abstract?”" but "What difference does this idea make in how we live, act, and decide?"

The Division

Two of the traditions cannot be run the same way, and the article says so rather than blending them.

The exercise is explicit about where its own instrument changes.

Continental — comfortable with ambiguity
•Treats the question as "a cultural, linguistic, and historical one"
•Asks what "childhood" means "in the historical and cultural language I’ve inherited"
•Asks whether the longing is "a product of modern alienation, capitalist burnout, or loss of communal rituals"
•Deconstructs the pairs the question smuggles in — childhood against adulthood, past against present, return against progress
•Suspects the naming itself of distorting what it names
Analytic — committed to precision
•"Unlike Continental philosophy, which is comfortable with ambiguity", it tests the sentence for form before content
•"Avoiding metaphysical excess unless rigorously justified"
•Demands the terms be fixed before the question is answerable
•Does not dismiss the longing; refuses to proceed while it is ambiguous
•The article's closing note is careful that this means the broad tradition, not logical positivism: "We can do analytic philosophy with or without the assumptions of positivism."

What It Establishes

A question cut loose from the fear that produced it stops being philosophy.

The article's own reason for insisting on the motif.

ARGUMENT A
Philosophy has always been situated
"Plato’s Republic was born from political crisis in Athens. Nietzsche’s Genealogy of Morals came from cultural disillusionment. Kierkegaard wrote from the depths of religious hypocrisy and personal anguish."
ARGUMENT B
So the admission is not a digression
"So when you say, “I wish I could return to childhood,” and then quietly add, “Because I’m afraid of what’s happening around me…” That’s not a distraction. That’s the origin of the question."
ARGUMENT C
The motif selects the framework
"If my fear is about political decay → the question leans into axiology, existentialism, and theology"; "If my fear feels like loss of structure or logic → it pulls me into epistemology and analytic clarity"; "If I feel your language no longer fits your reality → I would have already been walking with Derrida".
ARGUMENT D
And it guards against detachment
"Otherwise, we risk asking, “Can we return to childhood?” — as if it were a geometry problem. When in truth, what we’re really asking is: “Can I find again what I’ve lost — before the world collapses too far to remember it?”"
The fear, named

The article lists it as four items: "the collapse of currency", "the rise of spectacle over truth", "the loss of moral compass", "the return of authoritarian logic". It does not attach them to any person, party or government, and this deck does not either.

The Withholding

Theology is held back on purpose, because it does not reason the way the other eight do.

Eight frameworks are run here; the ninth is handed to the next instalment.

Knowing your motivation keeps your inquiry grounded, urgent, and morally awake.

— (3 of 5 Part 2) Philosophy "desktop" exercise
Where existentialism leaves it
The longing becomes "Not weakness" but "an encounter with my finitude" — "A threshold moment — to either flee into memory or step forward into radical meaning-making" "the task is not to regress — it is to choose authenticity now, to confront life without illusion."
Why the count is nine minus one
The exercise names its own arithmetic in its opening line and never restores the missing term. Theology's separation is argued in the following instalment, on the ground that it proceeds from revelation rather than from questioning.
What the article claims for the fear
"And in your case — your fear in response to what’s happening in Indonesia — is not just relevant. It is philosophically illuminating."
