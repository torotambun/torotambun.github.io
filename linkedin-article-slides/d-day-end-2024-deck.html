D-Day End 2024

A search for a God who was not the Jesus of his upbringing ran through invented archetypes and borrowed traditions and arrived back at the Hebrew name given at the burning bush — and the piece ends twice, once as a homecoming and once as a refusal of every label, without reconciling the two.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

The question was not whether God exists but whether there was anyone left to turn to.

"There are times when life weighs so heavily that even simple tasks feel impossible, and the search for meaning fades to a distant, unreachable horizon."

The question arrives at night, in a voice "heavy with unspoken struggle": "What if there were a figure—a cosmic presence—I could turn to?" The words "tumbled out, as if they had been waiting for years to surface."

"This wasn't a fleeting thought but a visceral yearning—a need for someone or something to lean on. A presence to guide him through the fog of doubt, to break through barriers when all seemed lost, and, when nothing else sufficed, to intervene in ways that defied understanding."

Note what is being asked for, because the whole piece turns on its specificity. Not an explanation, not a doctrine, not comfort. A presence capable of intervening.

"This question marked the beginning of his search. It wasn't driven by mere curiosity but by a deep, primal need for hope, clarity, and transcendence."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Void

Jesus had become a symbol rather than a certainty, and the space he occupied stayed empty.

"He had grown up with Jesus as this guiding figure—a source of strength, compassion, and connection. But over time, his understanding shifted."

"Jesus, once the cornerstone of his faith, had become a symbol—more myth than historical certainty, more construct than reality. Yet the void Jesus once filled remained, and his longing for guidance grew more profound."

The question that follows is the piece's engine: "If not Jesus, then who? Who could embody the qualities he yearned for—wisdom, strength, compassion, and timely intervention? Who—or what—could step in when life felt unbearable?"

"For a time, he wrestled with the absence of such a figure. The Jesus of his youth had faded, leaving behind an emptiness he couldn't ignore. In an attempt to fill that space, he turned to history and myth, exploring archetypes that had shaped human understanding for centuries."

"Buddha's serenity, Krishna's playful guidance, and the nurturing patience of the Mother Archetype offered glimpses of what he sought. But none felt complete."

And then a pattern, which is also an indictment: the figures available "were human creations, shaped and refined over centuries. Each emerged from the collective imagination of a tribe or culture, each tribe yearning for an exclusive guiding figure who would embody their hopes and surpass the deities of others." That competitive cycle "seemed less about universal truth and more about asserting dominance".

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The First Answer

A figure you create is still yours to lean on — and that is exactly how the golden calf began.

"This cosmic figure… is it my creation?" The answer given is yes, and then a long argument for why yes is not a dismissal — followed immediately by the objection that undoes it.

Why a created figure still works
•"If you create this cosmic figure, it would be your creation—a projection of your deepest values, aspirations, and needs. However, that doesn't diminish its power or significance"
•"Human history" is "full of people creating archetypes, symbols, and deities to represent ideals or provide guidance in moments of uncertainty"
•"These figures become meaningful because of the trust and energy poured into them. They're more than just ideas" — "they're touchstones, anchors"
•"While this figure may originate from your imagination, its impact on your psyche and emotional well-being can be profound. It's not just a concept—it becomes a part of your reality"
•It can be tied outward rather than inward: "you can connect your cosmic figure to the greater mysteries of the universe—something vast and unknowable"
Why that answer does not hold
✗"wasn't this how all those figures had begun? As personal, intimate connections, eventually morphing into shared beliefs laden with rituals, symbols, and doctrines?"
✗The Israelites at Sinai: "Their anxiety gave birth to a golden calf—a symbol to cling to, yet one that brought condemnation for what it represented: idolatry, a blasphemy against the real G_d"
✗"He hesitated, aware of the thin line between seeking guidance and creating something that could become a distraction, or worse, an affront to the divine"
✗The objection from inside: "doesn't that make it feel… small? Like it's just me talking to myself?"
✗"The figure he sought could not just be another replacement or proxy, but the real mysterious G_d"
What the section settles

The created figure is rejected not because it would fail to console but because consolation was never the requirement. "This wasn't about shaping a deity to fit his needs but aligning himself with the One who transcended human understanding".

The Ground

The crisis was liquidity, not poverty, which is why no practical answer would settle it.

The piece stops here to state its material facts, in a register deliberately at odds with everything around it — "his pragmatism and critical realist perspective grounded his thoughts."

"I'm not broke," he admitted to himself. "It's a cash flow problem. I have assets—more than several million dollars' worth" — "but they're not so easily accessible. I need this super cosmic figure, someone real and supreme. It cannot be Jesus because he feels mythical to me, and I wish I didn't have to create one. I want the real G_d, a supreme being."

"In that moment, he realized his yearning wasn't just for financial stability but for something deeper—a connection to a higher power, a force that could offer both guidance and intervention."

This is the paragraph that makes the rest of the piece legible. A search for a supreme being conducted by someone in financial difficulty invites an obvious reduction — that the theology is the distress talking. The article forecloses it by stating the balance sheet: the problem is access to assets, not their absence, and the yearning outlasts the arithmetic.

"The challenge, he thought, was aligning this need with his understanding of existence—one that avoided myths but still embraced transcendence."

The constraint that shapes the whole search

"If I cannot turn to my parent's Jesus, perhaps I can explore broader paths, spiritual or philosophical, to find this supreme being who resonates with my reality." Everything that follows is bounded by that condition: transcendence without myth.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Search

Contemplation of the infinite and agnostic theism kept the mystery without requiring the doctrine.

Two named positions, arrived at separately and then combined. Both are chosen for the same property: they permit a relationship without demanding a claim he cannot make.

01
First
Contemplation of the Infinite
Under a canopy of stars, "the vastness of the universe overwhelmed him." And it was not aesthetic: "it wasn't mere admiration for physical beauty. It was a recognition of something greater—a presence beyond human understanding." The question put to the sky, as the article sets it: if a supreme being exists, "could it be present in all this?"
02
Second
Agnostic Theism
"It wasn't about rigid belief in a defined deity, nor was it a denial of the divine. Instead, it was an acknowledgment of a supreme force coupled with an openness to its mystery. This freed him from the constraints of dogma and allowed space for a personal, evolving relationship with the divine."
03
Together
What the pairing buys
"Contemplation of the Infinite helped him cultivate awe and connection with the cosmos as an expression of the supreme. Agnostic Theism allowed him to form a personal relationship with this force, one grounded in humility and authenticity. Together, these paths bypassed man-made constructs while nurturing an evolving, intimate connection with the divine."
The method that goes with the position

"Rather than chasing definitive answers, he learned to focus on being present and asking the right questions." And the expectation set accordingly: "If this being exists," he thought, "it will reveal itself not through doctrine but in moments that resonate deeply with my soul."

What counted as evidence

Not revelation but coincidence held loosely — "an old friend reaching out unexpectedly, a chance encounter with someone offering fresh insight, or a wave of calm that settled over him in the midst of chaos." His own reading: "not through dramatic interventions but in subtle, deliberate ways that we only hear when we're paying attention."

The Turn

Contemplation was not enough, so he asked for the prayers of his childhood back.

"Enough with the contemplation," he said. "I need actions. I need a shift in energy or frequency—something to break the bondage of financial problems. Contemplation is fine, but now I need real, tangible change."

The request that follows is the piece's sharpest reversal. Having spent pages establishing that the Jesus of his upbringing had become myth, he asks to borrow that upbringing's language anyway: "since I was raised in Christianity, maybe borrowing prayers and proverbs from the Bible would feel less foreign, more grounded."

The answer treats this as continuity rather than retreat. Such prayers "can provide a comforting and familiar structure" and "can serve as a bridge between your upbringing and the spiritual journey you're on now."

What is offered is six passages, each with an adapted personal version alongside it — the Lord's Prayer from Matthew 6:9–13, the Prayer of Jabez from 1 Chronicles 4:10, Psalm 37:25, Proverbs 3:5–6, Psalm 23:1–3 and Deuteronomy 8:18. The adaptations are explicit about the financial situation: "Lord, provide for my immediate needs. Open ways to resolve my financial burdens and deliver me from the chains of scarcity."

"This approach blends your Christian roots with the personal journey you're on now," he concluded. "It allows you to connect with the divine in a way that feels authentic, empowering, and true to who you are." The reply: "It feels like a way to honor where I've come from while still moving forward."

The other counsel offered, before the prayers

"To invite change—perhaps even miracles—requires something deeper than effort alone," he began. "It's about creating a space for clarity and trust to emerge." And on what to watch for: "Sometimes what we call miracles aren't thunderous revelations. They're quiet nudges—a memory stirred, a phone call at the right moment, a sudden idea that feels like it came from nowhere."

The Name

The search arrived at the name given at the burning bush.

"To whom am I praying?" he asked. "What's His—or Her—name? Perhaps I'm cycling back to the questions asked thousands of years ago."

the name ‘I Am Who I Am' originates from the Torah, specifically in Exodus 3:14. It's the name God revealed to Moses at the burning bush—a name that carries profound meaning.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun
What the name is said to convey
"Often rendered as “Adonai”, it conveys eternal existence, self-sufficiency, and transcendence. This name tells us that God is not bound by time, space, or human constructs."
Why this name and not another
It meets the condition set at the start — transcendence without myth. "If this resonates with your quest for a supreme being who is real, transcendent, and not confined to mythological narratives, then ‘I Am Who I Am’ could be your focal point."
How it is to be used
Three practices: invocation — "Begin your prayers or meditations by addressing ‘I Am Who I Am.’"; reflection on what the name signifies; and personal affirmation, because "The name doesn't just point outward; it reminds you of your own connection to the divine."
What it becomes
"‘I Am Who I Am’ becomes not just a name but a profound invitation—to trust in an unchanging, infinite reality that transcends all human limitations." And the reply to that: "It's both a name and a promise—a connection to a reality that is vast, eternal, and ever-present. And now, it's yours to call upon."

The Objection

Arriving there felt like square one; the answer offered was circling deeper, not circling back.

"Am I cycling back?" he asked, almost rhetorically. "Now I cannot seem to run from the Hebrew God. It feels like I'm back to square one." The objection is raised by the piece against itself, and it is the most honest moment in it.

The case that nothing moved
✗"I've tried to move beyond this—beyond the God of my upbringing. Yet here I am, caught in the same narrative"
✗"It feels inescapable"
✗The search set out to avoid myth and ended at the God of the Torah
✗Every alternative examined — Buddha, Krishna, the Mother Archetype, Deism, Taoism — was passed over
✗The prayers borrowed back are the prayers he was raised on
The case that the distance is real
✓"you're not cycling back, but circling deeper"
✓"Before, the name so resembles the Hebrew God may have felt like a myth, confined to stories and traditions that no longer resonated with you"
✓"Now, ‘I Am Who I Am’ has become something vast, unbounded, and personal" — "a presence not tied to doctrine but to existence itself"
✓"When you started, you were questioning the existence of a supreme being entirely. Now, you've arrived at a name that holds profound meaning"
✓"Returning to ‘I Am Who I Am’ isn't retreating; it's finding depth in simplicity. It's realizing that this presence was never about religion or myth—it was always about connection"
Where it lands

"Maybe you're right," he said. "Maybe it's not square one after all. Maybe it's a deeper step forward." Three uses of maybe in three sentences — the assent is given, and given tentatively.

The Unresolved

If He can intervene and does not, the absence is either cruelty or an invitation, and the piece refuses to choose.

"But is He truly omnipotent?" he asked. "And if He is, does that mean He can break His own laws—His own rules? Or is that a contradiction?"

The first answer sets a boundary that is not a limit: omnipotence "speaks to God's ultimate authority and ability to do anything that is logically possible. But it also acknowledges that this power operates within the consistency of God's nature, which includes justice, truth, and purpose." So: "even in this limitless power, God doesn't act arbitrarily or contradict His own essence."

On miracles: "Miracles are often misunderstood as breaking the universe's laws. They aren't about God ‘breaking' the laws of the universe. Instead, they reveal a higher reality" — "God's sovereignty over creation." The Red Sea is offered as the case, "Whether one interprets it literally or symbolically" — a hedge the article makes and does not resolve.

Then the question the piece cannot dispose of: "But if He can intervene, why doesn't He do it all the time? Why allow suffering, chaos, or struggle?" The answer given is free will and the integrity of natural law — "Constant intervention would undermine the integrity of those systems and our ability to choose, learn, and grow."

It does not settle the matter, and the piece says so by asking again: "So He's powerful enough to intervene but chooses not to? Isn't that… cruel?" The reply concedes the force of it — "It can feel that way" — before offering the alternative reading: "Sometimes, the absence of intervention isn't neglect—it's an invitation to trust, to seek, and to grow in ways we might not otherwise."

"It's hard to trust something you don't fully understand." The reply: "It is," and then "But trust isn't about having all the answers. It's about believing that there's a greater purpose, even when we can't see it."

The admission the section ends on

"I just want to know it's worth it," The answer offered is not that it will work: "Not because everything will be perfect or easy, but because in seeking this connection, you're aligning with something infinite, something beyond the limits of human understanding." And then: "I guess I need to sit with that for a while," he said.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Dawn

By morning he was not seeking but being found.

"As the dawn light seeped into the room, he seemed different—lighter, like a weight had lifted. The darkness hadn't disappeared, but the morning had begun."

Like a prodigal son, he wasn't just seeking; he was being found.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun
What the image claims
"As he sat there, his shoulders trembling with quiet sobs, it was as if he had finally come home—not to a place, but to himself." The grammar carries the argument: the verb moves from active to passive, from searching to being found.
What is not claimed
"The longing was still there, but it no longer felt like a burden. Instead, it was a reminder—a quiet prayer—for something greater." Nothing is resolved and nothing is received. What changes is the weight of the wanting.
The last four words
"Perhaps that was enough." The dialogue's final sentence is conditional. It is the strongest resolution the piece offers, and it is offered as a possibility.

What I Carry

The closing taxonomy names seven positions and then declines every one of them.

Three pages after the dawn, the piece changes voice and register entirely — "Takeaways for me as the Reader" — and sets out how the writer might be described "within the taxonomy of religion and spirituality".

1  ·  Spiritual but not religious
"I see myself as spiritual in the sense that I believe in higher powers or deeper meaning, but I don't subscribe to the dogmas or rituals of organized religion."
2  ·  Seeker
"I am someone searching for spiritual truth and meaning outside traditional religious institutions. My path is a journey, not a destination."
3  ·  Freethinker
"I form my beliefs based on reason, personal experience, and intuition rather than relying on inherited doctrines. I question what doesn't resonate and value independent thought."
4  ·  Eclectic
"I draw from various religious, spiritual, and philosophical traditions to shape a belief system that feels true to me."
5  ·  Agnostic or atheistic spiritualist
"My spirituality may not be centered on a deity. I stay open to the possibility of spiritual realities without committing to specific supernatural claims".
6  ·  Humanist
"I might also align with humanism if I root my beliefs in human values, ethics, and the lived experiences of people, rather than in spiritual or supernatural frameworks."
7  ·  Philosophical seeker
"My approach may be more philosophical than religious. I care less about rituals or labels and more about meaning, coherence, and ethical clarity."
The sentence that withdraws all seven

"I understand that these labels are only broad categories. My path is personal and evolving" — "too complex to be fully captured by any single term."

The two endings, which the piece does not reconcile

The dialogue closes on the prodigal son and the burning-bush name — a homecoming. The post-reflection closes on a taxonomy built from positions outside organised religion, then refuses the taxonomy too. Both endings are in the published article, three pages apart, and nothing in it joins them. This deck holds both and resolves neither.
