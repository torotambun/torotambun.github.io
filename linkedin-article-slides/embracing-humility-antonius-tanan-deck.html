Embracing Humility: Lessons from Dr. Antonius Tanan on Balancing Intelligence and Likability

Intelligence and likability are treated as a trade-off by people who have plenty of the first. Schopenhauer is the standing case that they need not be, and his own mother wrote the indictment. Research on social networks finds people prefer a lovable fool to a competent jerk — so the fourth cell, high on both, is the only stable place to stand. Humility costs an intelligent person nothing, which is what makes its absence a choice rather than a temperament.

Opening

Two men exchanged books, and one of them made me feel safe enough to learn.

A meeting on 10 June 2024 with Dr. Antonius Tanan of Universitas Ciputra Surabaya, and his son Johan Tanan.

The description is unusually direct about all three registers at once: "a renowned scholar, successful businessman and a principled fear of God person. He is a very likable man and, at the same time, a highly intelligent person." The conjunction is the subject of the article.

Books changed hands both ways — the author's own “Disciplined Storytelling?” for Tanan's "Landing Smoothly in the 2nd career", described as "an insight that is especially pertinent in today's rapidly changing world."

What the meeting produced is stated as a condition rather than a feeling: "In the presence of Dr. Tanan and his son, Johan Tanan, I felt safe and encouraged to learn." And the reading drawn from it — Tanan "embodies the balance between intellectual prowess and interpersonal likability, demonstrating that true wisdom involves humility and the ability to create a supportive atmosphere for learning and growth."

Why it registered as remarkable

"His exceptional quality piqued my interest because I am surrounded by many individuals with long academic titles who often emphasize their intellectual achievements over interpersonal connections." The compliment has a comparison inside it, and the article does not disguise that.

The Counter-Example

Schopenhauer is the standing case, and his own mother wrote the indictment.

The article's illustration of the failure mode is a philosopher who described the mechanism and then fell into it.

"Schopenhauer's intellectual arrogance often overshadowed his admirable qualities. He had a strong desire to be seen as highly knowledgeable and superior, which frequently led to strained social interactions and isolation."

The most damaging quotation in the piece is from Johanna Schopenhauer, his mother: "All of your good qualities become obscured by your super-cleverness and are made useless to the world merely because of your rage at wanting to know everything better than others; of wanting to improve and master what you cannot command. With this, you embitter the people around you". Made useless to the world is the phrase that carries.

What makes the case sharp is that Schopenhauer understood the dynamic exactly. He observed that when a man perceives his interlocutor to be "intellectually very much his superior… this is a method of reasoning… which rouses the bitterest feelings of sullen and rancorous hatred." And he saw no escape from it: "A man may be as humble as possible in his demeanor, yet hardly ever get people to overlook his crime in standing intellectually above them."

His account of why people arrange themselves as they do is quoted at the head of the piece: "It is a real recommendation to be stupid. For just as warmth is agreeable to the body, so it does the mind good to feel its superiority; and a man will seek company likely to give him this feeling, as instinctively as he will approach the fireplace or walk in the sun if he wants to get warm".

Evidence

People would rather work with a lovable fool than a competent jerk.

The article moves from a philosopher's complaint to a finding, and the finding is not flattering to competence.

What competence alone produces
✗Competent Jerk — "High competence, low likability"
✗Incompetent Jerk — "Low competence, low likability"
✗"people prefer to work with those who are likable, even if they are less competent, over those who are highly competent but unpleasant"
✗"People generally dislike feeling inferior and are more comfortable and accepted in environments that are not intimidatingly superior in terms of intelligence"
What likability buys
✓Lovable Fool — "Low competence, high likability"
✓Lovable Star — "High competence, high likability"
✓"people often prefer to work with lovable fools over competent jerks because they value positive interactions and team harmony"
✓Gracián's advice, quoted from maxim 240: "To be well liked, one must dress in the skin of the simplest animals."
Schopenhauer's own generalisation of the finding

"These remarks are applicable not only to advancement in political life but to all competition for places of honor and dignity, and even for reputation in the world of science, literature, and art. In learned societies, for example, mediocrity – that very acceptable quality – is always to the fore, whilst merit meets with tardy recognition, or with none at all. So it is in everything."

Diagnosis

Five ways an intelligent person sabotages himself, and four of them are about ego.

The article imports a list of pitfalls and applies it to the group it has been describing.

1  ·  Undermining team dynamics
"Focusing solely on their intelligence and dismissing the contributions of others." The cost falls on the team before it falls on the person.
2  ·  Overemphasis on intelligence
"Letting their ego get hurt if their intelligence is not respected." The vulnerability is created by the thing being defended.
3  ·  Overthinking
"Getting bogged down in details and losing sight of the bigger picture." The only one of the five that is a failure of capability rather than of character.
4  ·  Ego-driven decisions
"Believing they are the smartest in the room and ignoring valuable input." The belief may even be true; the article's point is that acting on it is still a mistake.
5  ·  Ignoring warning signs
"Pursuing personal visions without heeding red flags". The list is imported from Harvard Business Review and applied to the group the article has been describing.

What It Revealed

Dr. Tanan is the fourth cell — he sits, listens, observes, and does not offer his opinion quickly.

The observation is behavioural and repeated, which is what makes it evidence rather than courtesy.

"In my various encounters with Dr. Tanan, I've consistently observed his “lovable star” quality. Despite his impressive intellect, he always engages with humility and kindness, creating an environment where learning and collaboration thrive."

Then the specific behaviour, which is the most useful sentence in the article: "He doesn't quickly offer his opinion; he sits, listens, and observes—another quality of a highly open-minded person. No wonder he is likable and not threatening." Not threatening is doing real work there — it names what the competent jerk cannot help being.

The article records, in a closing note, a phrase from Tanan's own book: "Socially active, Intellectually active, Physically active Spiritually active" — four registers held at once, which is the same shape as the argument being made about him.

Conclusion

Humility costs an intelligent person nothing, which is why refusing it is a choice.

The argument closes on a claim about cost, and it is the claim that makes the piece more than an appreciation.

Our intelligence or existence won't change just because we appear humble and open to learning.

— Embracing Humility: Lessons from Dr. Antonius Tanan, 14 June 2024
The condition, from Aristotle
"Why don't we listen to Aristotle? He wisely stated, “It is the mark of an educated mind to be able to entertain a thought without accepting it.”" The same line closes the Harvardians lunch deck of six weeks later — it is a standing test for him, not a decoration.
What is actually gained
"Embracing a humble attitude and being open-minded does not diminish our intellectual capabilities; rather, it enhances our social interactions and makes us more approachable and likable." Nothing is surrendered; something is added.
Where the line is drawn
"while intelligence is a valuable trait, its emphasis should not come at the cost of social harmony." The target is not intelligence but its emphasis — a distinction the Schopenhauer case exists to make.
