I Hate Doing Marathons. Yet This Is My Tenth.

The marathons are endured for the uninterrupted attention they allow, not for competition. The tenth showed that solitude does not guarantee noticing one’s own assumptions: a stranger’s pace was followed for six kilometres without asking why, and at kilometre 36 the habit of competing, carried from a corporate career, was consciously set down.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

Ten marathons run for solitude rather than competition still left room for an unexamined race against a stranger.

"What 42.195 kilometres revealed about solitude, attention and the habit of competing."

Situation

"I hate marathons. Yet I have completed ten of them", entirely in Singapore and without entering organised competitions. The tenth was run on 29 October 2026.

Complication

"Despite deliberately avoiding competitive running, I found myself following another runner’s pace."

Question

Why accelerate when there is no race, no rival and no finishing-time target?

Answer

"For six kilometres, I concentrated on running faster without questioning why I wanted to do so."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Scene

The distance is endured for hours free of the iPhone, in which to observe, contemplate and converse with the Creator.

"Physical endurance, however, cannot fully explain why I return."

Daily runs of 3.5 to 5 kilometres grew to 10, 18 and 29, then the full distance. Chunking helps: "fourteen repetitions of a familiar 3-kilometre exercise seem more manageable." But "The final 3 kilometres feel nothing like the first."

"During a marathon, I am free from my iPhone, internet distractions and the compulsion to check what is happening elsewhere." Singapore supplies trees, sea breeze and people going about their lives. On the tenth, the National Environment Agency recorded one-hour PM₂.₅ of 36 µg/m³ in the east at 9 a.m., Band 1 (Normal). Earlier runs bracketed the thesis defence: time to contemplate what lay ahead, then to reflect on it.

1 Timothy 4:8

"I do not interpret it as suggesting that exercise is worthless." But "completing a marathon does not make someone a better person, and I find little satisfaction in using physical achievement to establish superiority over others."

The Turns

At kilometre 21 the body refused, from kilometre 30 a stranger set the pace, and at kilometre 36 the mind chose to slow.

Three turns in one run, each settled differently.

Km 21–24
The body refused
The wish to accelerate met a body that refused. "Around kilometre 24, my mind finally accepted what my body had been communicating."
Km 30–36
A stranger set the pace
After a coconut, an older runner passed. "Almost instinctively, I began following his pace." Those six kilometres ran faster than the first three.
Km 36
The mind chose to slow
"At kilometre 36, I deliberately reduced my pace." This time the body could go faster; the decision to slow was conscious.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
What It Revealed

Bazerman’s noticing exposed the gap: close attention to how he was running had displaced the question of why he ran faster.

"I had noticed the faster runner. I had noticed my breathing and improved pace. What I had initially failed to examine was why I wanted to accelerate."

Bazerman (2014) examines how people overlook information relevant to their decisions because their attention is directed elsewhere. "The other runner had done nothing to challenge me. There was no competition, and I had no finishing-time target." His pace became a reference point "simply because I had allowed it to influence my behaviour."

"This exposed a limitation in the very solitude I valued." Freedom from the iPhone sharpened attention to the surroundings, "but it did not automatically make me attentive to the assumptions behind my own behaviour."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Field Notes

Read as age 60 on a career that began at 24, kilometre 36 recast the acceleration as a habit outliving its purpose.

Kilometre zero as age 24, joining Schlumberger in 1994; each kilometre another year. "This was only my personal accounting of a professional journey, not a prediction about how long anyone will live."

Decades in intensely competitive corporate environments. "Did I succeed? Other people may have their opinions. I have little interest in settling that question." Competition once served practical purposes, "But habits acquired early in a career can continue influencing behaviour long after the circumstances that produced them have changed."

"I still had more than six kilometres to complete. But what purpose would be served by allowing another person’s pace to determine how I covered them?"

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Burden

Hebrews 12:1 separated burdens carried by choice, like the spare shirts, from habits kept after their purpose had gone.

The passage concerns endurance in faith and "does not offer advice about running equipment. It prompted me to consider what constitutes an unnecessary burden."

Carried by choice
✓"three T-shirts, medicines, a second pair of socks, spare hats and small towels."
✓"Having these provisions available was more important to me than minimising my finishing time."
✓"Some responsibilities remain worthwhile despite their demands."
Carried by habit
✗"The impulse to match another runner’s pace had arisen without the same consideration."
✗Some burdens "may have been acquired through competition and retained long after their original purpose disappeared."
✗"The difficulty lies in recognising the difference."
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
What I Carry

The remaining kilometres kept their purpose once another runner’s pace no longer set it.

"There was no shortcut through the final distance." What changed was awareness "of how easily another person’s pace could divert that attention."

At kilometre 36, I realised I could be faster. But why should I sacrifice the very reason I undertake marathons: to meditate, contemplate, and converse with the earth and the Creator, rather than compete with others?

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, I Hate Doing Marathons. Yet This Is My Tenth.
