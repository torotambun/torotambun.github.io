LinkedIn Article  ·  Innovation Ecosystems · Reading a Country as a Multinational Does  ·  19 June 2019

Each Region Has Its Own Unique Cluster Ecosystems. How to Find One ? Don't Emulate Silicon Valley!

The article says it is easy to criticise multinationals for not building local manufacturing, and applies the MIT framework for assessing innovation-driven entrepreneurship ecosystems (Murray and Budden, 2017) to see how a company reads a country: ten foundational-institution measures, then innovation capacity and entrepreneurial capacity across five areas each. It expects Indonesia to score unfavourably on the foundational measures, gives no scores, and answers with a long-term plan across five stakeholders, the checklist as a proxy and calm trust in the process. The integrated 2019-08-11 piece adds that each region's ecosystem is its own and that a country should stop emulating Silicon Valley and focus on what it has now.

Introduction

Criticising multinationals for not manufacturing locally is easy, and reading the country as they do is the better question.

Situation

Much social-media talk blames advanced manufacturing companies for being unwilling to build factories and to use local content. Those that have operated in the host country are also criticised as unfair and exploitative, since they are perceived as not using most of the local content, as lacking skill transfer and as lacking investment in the ecosystem.

Complication

The article says it is easy to criticise the multinationals for hesitating to bring a full manufacturing ecosystem, and just as easy to criticise the local business community for keeping rent-seeking businesses for years. What is needed is to learn objectively how multinationals evaluate their decisions, and how the government must align to make the country unique in their portfolio of countries for foreign direct investment.

Question

How does a multinational read a host country, and what does that reading say about what to do?

Answer

The author applies the MIT framework for assessing innovation-driven entrepreneurship ecosystems, which he finds more handy and more thorough than the country-analysis frameworks he met at the Harvard Business School GMP and in his 2000 MBA. Its baseline shows where a country stands. Each region's ecosystem is its own, so the answer is to build that ecosystem and not to emulate Silicon Valley.

Framework

The MIT framework reads a country at three levels, beginning with foundational institutions.

The framework comes from Murray and Budden's September 2017 MIT working paper and is normally used to measure the readiness of an entrepreneurial and innovation ecosystem. The article says it establishes the baseline, and that multinationals such as Boeing or Airbus could adopt it beside the traditional security, political, GDP and macroeconomic checklist. The two upper levels, it says, answer critics who see multinationals as not keen to transfer skills and knowledge.

01
Level I
Foundational institutions
Ten measures of the rules a country sets, from ease of doing business to the corruption perceptions index. The article calls this the very base of the pyramid: a company that finds the base unfavourable has no reason to go further up.
02
Level II
Innovation capacity
Five areas: human capital, funding, infrastructure, demand, and culture and incentives. This level tests innovation readiness.
03
Level III
Entrepreneurial capacity
The same five areas, read for entrepreneurship readiness: human capital, funding, infrastructure, demand, and culture and incentives.
From the 2019-08-11 companion piece

That piece credits the same MIT class for the line that each centre has its own chemistry, which shapes the DNA of its entrepreneurial and innovation ecosystems. The line is set in quotation marks and described as inspired by Murray and Budden, so it is paraphrased here and not given as their words.

Evidence note

The article says the framework can be adopted by multinationals such as Boeing or Airbus. It offers this as an option, and gives no source showing that either company uses it.

Details

The base of the pyramid is ten foundational-institution measures, and a base that fails gives a company no reason to go higher.

The article lists ten measures under Measuring Foundational Institutions. The same ten appear in the author's 2019-10-09 piece on unicorns and free cash flow.

1  ·  Ease of doing business
Composite country ranking from the World Bank across 10 topics relevant to ease of operating private-sector firms.
2  ·  Starting a business
Ranking of the simplicity of starting a new business, incorporating and registering a new firm.
3  ·  Paying taxes
Ranking of tax rates and the administrative burden of paying tax for typical medium-size firms.
4  ·  Resolving insolvency
Ranking of the weaknesses in insolvency law and the main bottlenecks in the process.
5  ·  Enforcing contracts
Ranking of the time and cost of resolving a commercial dispute, including the degree of good practice in the court system.
6  ·  Property rights
Score across the strength of laws that let individuals accumulate five types of property rights, including IPRs.
7  ·  Government integrity
Score capturing levels of trust, transparency and absence of corruption.
8  ·  Labor freedom
Score capturing the flexibility and efficiency of the labor market, including hindrance to hiring.
9  ·  Trade freedom
Score capturing tariff and non-tariff barriers to imports and exports.
10  ·  Corruption Perceptions Index
Overall ranking of countries by composite level of perceived corruption. A high ranking implies high corruption.

Gap

The criticism meets the base of the pyramid: the author expects unfavourable scores on measures such as contracts, corruption, insolvency and labor.

The article says that if each index were cross-checked to find where the host country belongs, a lot of homework would remain for Indonesia before a rational multinational goes ahead. It gives no scores, and names four measures as examples.

The criticism
•Multinationals are hesitant to bring a full manufacturing ecosystem
•They use little local content and transfer little skill
•They invest too little in the local ecosystem
•They are seen as unfair and exploitative
What the author expects the base to show
✗Enforcing contracts: unfavourable scores expected
✗Corruption index: unfavourable scores expected
✗Resolving insolvency: unfavourable scores expected
✗Labor freedom: unfavourable scores expected
✗China is named as an exception, where geopolitical motives are exceptional
Evidence note

The article gives no scores for Indonesia or any other country. The expectation is the author's, and the China exception carries no source.

Details

Innovation capacity is read across five areas, from STEM education to the culture that attracts talent.

Each area carries indicators, given in the article with the question each one answers.

1  ·  Human capital
Quality of STEM education, STEM graduates per capita, new PhD graduates per capita, availability of scientists and engineers, and researchers and professionals engaged in R&D per million population.
2  ·  Funding
R&D expenditure as a percentage of GDP and in absolute terms, the public share of R&D expenditure and the business share, the last two from UNESCO.
3  ·  Infrastructure
ICT access, internet bandwidth, the sophistication of production processes and the availability of the latest technologies.
4  ·  Demand
Government procurement of advanced technology, university and industry research collaboration, and trade, competition and market scale.
5  ·  Culture and incentives
The quality of scientific research institutions, which can attract talent from the country and abroad, and the share of graduates in science and engineering, which shows how alluring that degree is.

Details

Entrepreneurial capacity is read across the same five areas, from schooling to attitudes toward risk.

The article closes the framework with a condition: if the host country is found not ready entrepreneurially and in innovation, then what happened in Indonesia happened.

1  ·  Human capital
The share of school graduates in tertiary education, and the share of the adult population who believe they have the skills and knowledge to start a business.
2  ·  Funding
Ease of access to loans and to credit, venture capital availability, venture capital investment and the number of venture capital deals per year.
3  ·  Infrastructure
Electricity and telephony infrastructure, the number of internet users, and logistics performance, including customs clearance, trade and transportation infrastructure and timely delivery.
4  ·  Demand
Buyer sophistication, meaning whether buyers decide on price or on performance, and the scale of the domestic market.
5  ·  Culture and incentives
Entrepreneurial intention within three years, attitudes toward entrepreneurial risk overall and for men and women, fear of failure, entrepreneurship as a good career choice, the status given to successful entrepreneurs, and business freedom.
Evidence note

Two funding items, venture capital availability and venture capital investment, carry identical descriptions in the article. The article gives no data on either level for Indonesia.

Implications

Without a long-term plan the future is bleak, so the answer is a five-party plan, the checklist as a proxy and calm trust in the process.

The article asks how, and says the answer is not a panadol: it takes a long time and needs commitment from all parties. Without a long-term plan that stakeholders commit to, it calls the future rather bleak, and it warns of emulation after emulation as a business model, in businesses that fill a void, which it says creates negative reinforcing feedback in the country's business culture.

1
Build a long-term plan for the ecosystem
A long-term plan to build a proper ecosystem across five stakeholders: government, large corporations, risk capital players, R&D and universities, and entrepreneur cultivation.
2
Use the checklist as a proxy
Use the checklist above as a proxy for where the country stands.
3
Keep calm and trust the process
The article ends its list on this line and gives no steps or timetable for it.
Evidence note

The article gives no timetable, cost or sequence for the plan. The sentence on emulation is cut across a page break in the source, so its reading here follows the text as printed.

Conclusion

Each region has its own ecosystem, so a country stops emulating Silicon Valley and focuses on what it has now.

The 2019-08-11 companion piece, integrated here, supplies the principle behind the title of the 2019-06-19 article.

I wish a country would stop dreaming of becoming and emulating Silicon Valley.

— Each city / region has its own unique innovation ecosystem, 11 August 2019
One of each
The piece says there is only one Silicon Valley, Kendall, Bangalore, Singapore, London and Dubai, and that innovation should arise from and tailor to a place's own ecosystem.
What a country may find
Each country should investigate its own ecosystem, cultivate and nurture it, and accept that it may be agriculture, fisheries, new food tech and tourism instead of advanced robotics and high-tech manufacturing. Not every country has the ability to be an early adopter of electric cars or solar energy.
The cost of not exploring
The piece warns of the sad story of a country abundant in resources yet terribly declining because it fails to explore, and advises focusing on what the country has now.
