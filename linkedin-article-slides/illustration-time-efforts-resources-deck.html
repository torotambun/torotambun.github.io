LinkedIn Article  ·  Deliberate Practice · Eight Years of Vocal Training  ·  18 March 2024

An Illustration for Time, Efforts and Resources: Reminiscence of my regiment singing lessons

The article is not a memoir of singing but an audit of what mastery costs, using the only case the author can document from the inside. Two named maestros, eight years, a weekly bicycle ride, nearly a year spent on a single three-note passage before performance was permitted, and a curriculum of named exercise books — these are produced as the itemised bill behind a competence that would otherwise look like talent. Dunning-Kruger supplies the shape of the climb; Michelangelo supplies the reason nobody sees it.

Opening

Michelangelo said mastery would not seem wonderful if people knew the work.

The article states its purpose in its first sentences, and the purpose is not nostalgia.

"The path to excellence is arduous and often unseen, a truth captured by Michelangelo’s words, “If people knew how hard I had to work to gain my mastery, it wouldn’t seem wonderful at all.”"

The claim is immediately generalised beyond one discipline: "This sentiment rings true across disciplines, uniting Nobel Prize winners, opera singers, craftsmen, super chefs, and professors in a common narrative of relentless dedication."

The author's own case begins in play rather than in training. Singing started “amidst the vibrant echo of school performances and the harmonious corners of church choirs since the age of five”. “These early years were marked by a sense of natural exploration, where the joy of music was heartfelt, nurtured by the melodic tunes of my parents' singing, radios, TV, and cassettes of various genres.”

Even the untrained period was not idle: "Without formal training or teachers, I embarked on a journey of self-discovery", emphasising "foundational vocal techniques such as breath control, vocal resonance, and pitch accuracy", with "classical, gospel, and old songs" the preferred ground and Queen the stated exception.

The Scene

Two maestros, eight years, and a bicycle to Merican Baru every Friday at four.

The pivot the article marks is from exploration to structure, and it is dated.

Age 5 onward
Singing as exploration
School performances and church choirs, self-taught, no teacher. "Exploring a variety of musical styles and genres broadened my musical vocabulary and allowed me to discover my strengths and preferences."
1986–1988
Pranawengrum Katamsi, Kompleks Triloka, Pancoran
"As I reached sixteen, a pivotal transformation occurred: the shift from innate exploration to structured learning, a step into the world of disciplined vocal training." Tuesdays at 10:00 for a half-hour lesson. Her authority is given with its provenance — "her seven-time championship at the Bintang Radio Televisi - BRTV". The regimen was disrupted first by a national researcher competition in late 1987, then by exchange study in Japan and the Singapore Youth Science Fortnight.
1989–1994
Daelamy Hassan, Merican Baru
“At 16:00, with punctuality that mirrored the discipline of my craft, I would pedal my bicycle from Bulaksumur to Merican Baru.” Then: “Arriving by 16:30, I was greeted by the steadfast presence of Pak Daelamy Hassan. During Ramadhan, clad in his traditional sarung, he embodied the commitment that my vocal training demanded.” An alumnus of Tokyo University of the Arts and “a shaping force behind the Yogyakarta Institute of Art”.
Nearly a year, within that period
One passage, before permission to perform
"Under his mentorship, my focus was unwavering, devoted to perfecting the challenging tenor arc of F F# G. This singular focus on vocal technique, persisting for nearly a year, was a rite of passage before I could grace the stage with performance."

Field Notes

The curriculum has names, and the names are the resource cost.

The article reproduces both teachers' syllabi in full. What follows is the structure, because the specificity is the argument.

1  ·  Katamsi — two thick black books, and one exception
"She uses her own collection of Indonesian Seriosa scores, found in two thick black books. Most of these scores were printed by Radio Republic Indonesia and written by her pianist, a wonderful gentleman named Sunarto Sunaryo. There's only one exception, a piece called “Caro Mio Ben,” which she adds at the end of the year."
2  ·  Katamsi — technique, then interpretation, then the stage
"Breath control, vocal resonance, range development, articulation, and diction formed the cornerstone of my vocal development." Beyond technique came "pitch accuracy, rhythm, timing, and expressive phrasing", then "performance skills and stage presence", then repertoire selection, score study and "mastering languages for operatic performances".
3  ·  Hassan — foundational techniques
Named by book and by function: Vaccai's Metodo Pratico for breath and Italian diction, Brown's Discover Your Voice for voice mechanics, Marchesi's 20 Vocalises Op. 15 for healthy production, Concone's Fifty Lessons Op. 9 for pitch and ear training, Panofka's 24 Vocalises Op. 81 for resonance and tone, and Lamperti's The Technics of Bel Canto.
4  ·  Hassan — intermediate and advanced
Lütgen's The Art of Vocal Velocity for flexibility across registers and the Estelle Liebling Vocal Course for range extension, then coloratura and agility, "Extended Techniques and Range Expansion", acting and stagecraft, "Endurance and Performance Stamina", and interpretation and role preparation.
5  ·  What the curriculum was for
“This comprehensive curriculum is designed to provide a detailed roadmap for vocal development across all stages of a singer's growth, from foundational techniques to advanced artistry.” The article's own gloss: “Each selected resource brings its unique strengths to the table, offering exercises, insights, and repertoire that cater to specific areas of vocal technique, musical expression, and performance readiness.”
Why the list is reproduced rather than summarised

Time, effort and resources are the article's three named quantities, and the third one is the hardest to see. A syllabus of a dozen named collections, most of them nineteenth-century Italian, is what "resources" actually looked like for a student in Yogyakarta before the internet.

What It Revealed

A year on one three-note passage is what time, efforts and resources actually means.

The article turns from documentation to what the documentation is evidence of.

The years are refused the status of biography: "These sequential years of discipline and learning were not mere appointments in a schedule; they were the building blocks of my musical foundation, imbued with the rigors and rhythms that would shape my voice and character."

The formation is described in physical terms rather than sentimental ones. Learning from Katamsi, "This synaptic neuron process undeniably shapes the future neuron pathways for me in my mental model or system 1 thinking, as well as potentially influencing my musical abilities (if any) and my throat muscles."

And the transfer is claimed explicitly, to two domains at once: "the discipline honed during those countless evenings practicing under the skies of Lapangan Pancasila, or aboard the seismic vessels of Schlumberger, mirrors the entrepreneur's resolve in the face of uncertainty and disruption."

"These early lessons in perseverance and focus have parallels in the entrepreneurial journey or mastering storyteller journey, where one must commit to a path of 'Unconscious Incompetence', striving towards 'Conscious Competence' through a rigorous and often humbling process".

Framework

Dunning-Kruger names the valley the practice is paying for.

The article closes its argument on the curve, which gives the eight years a shape rather than a duration.

1  ·  Low knowledge, high confidence
"In the initial phase, an individual may have very little knowledge but a disproportionately high level of confidence. This is where the Dunning-Kruger effect is most pronounced. People in this phase might not know enough to recognize their own lack of skill, leading to an inflated self-assessment."
2  ·  Awareness of deficiency
"As a person gains some experience and knowledge, they begin to realize the extent of their own lack of skill, which results in a rapid decrease in confidence."
3  ·  Valley of despair
"At this stage, individuals are acutely aware of their shortcomings, which can lead to a lack of confidence or even impostor syndrome, where they might doubt their capabilities despite having adequate skills."
4  ·  Ascending the slope of enlightenment
"With further learning and practice, individuals gain more expertise and start to build a more accurate understanding of their own level of competence, leading to gradually increasing confidence."
5  ·  Plateau of sustainability
"Eventually, as people acquire a significant amount of knowledge and expertise, their confidence stabilizes at a level that more accurately reflects their true ability."
What the curve explains about the year on F F# G

"The graph is a visual metaphor for this psychological phenomenon, showing that with increased knowledge, people's confidence tends to decrease after an initial peak, only to gradually increase again as they develop true expertise." A teacher who withholds the stage for a year is holding a student inside the valley on purpose.

What I Carry

Discipline is doing what you hate to do, but do it like you love it.

The line the article gives to Mike Tyson's trainer, and the reason it is the closing thought.

Discipline is doing what you hate to do, but do it like you love it

— Cus d'Amato, as captioned in “An Illustration for Time, Efforts and Resources”, 18 March 2024
Why the article reaches for a boxing trainer
"D'Amato was known for his profound impact on Tyson's career and his psychological approach to training. This quote reflects the essence of discipline in the context of rigorous training and preparation, emphasizing the importance of putting in the work and dedication required to achieve excellence, regardless of one's personal feelings towards the task at hand."
What the article does not claim
It does not say the training made him a singer. The performances are offered as links for anyone interested, the musical ability is parenthesised as "(if any)", and the eight years are presented as an illustration of a cost — which is what the title says they are.
A note on the wording
This article captions the line as “Discipline is doing what you hate to do, but do it like you love it”. The author's companion article of the same day prints a slightly different wording and flags its own version as verbatim. Each deck follows the form printed in its own source; neither is corrected against the other.

The article carries no formal reference list. The vocal method books are named in the body by composer, title and opus and are listed below as named there, without editions, since none is given.
