When Collectivism Gave Comfort: During Grief and Faced with Loneliness

A night of communal mourning in a Muslim-majority village, for a friend's mother-in-law who was the only Christian in her neighborhood, complicates the author's own standing critique that collectivism stifles innovation — showing that at its purest, collectivism is a form of contribution, not conformity, and offers a comfort individuality and innovation alone could not.

Dr. Toronata Tambun

Opening

A late-night drive to a village at the foot of Mount Slamet, for a friend's mother-in-law's wake, becomes a test of a standing critique.

The journey itself is deliberate, not rushed.

A very dear friend's mother-in-law passed away on a Monday morning. The news came suddenly, prompting his family to reschedule their commitments and set everything else aside. They made the decision to drive rather than take a train or plane, allowing for the flexibility to process the moment as a family. They traveled deliberately, resting every two hours, and kept their speed below 80 km/h even on the toll road. The journey was long, heavy with grief and reflection.

The Scene

Villagers who are not family keep vigil through the night, and a neighbor's cooked meal turns waiting into solidarity.

By the time the family arrived, the wake had already been underway for hours.

The wake had already been underway for hours, with villagers seated on plastic chairs in quiet solidarity — neighbors who had known the family for decades, bound not by blood but by shared history and community. She had lived in the village since 1965, the only Christian in a community of 30 Muslim families within the RT.

The family had traveled for hours from Jakarta — exhausted, hungry, and unclean from the road — and were met with the steadfast presence of villagers. One of the neighbors, Slamet, quietly noticed their exhaustion and left without a word. An hour later, Slamet returned, carrying four oversized plates of Nasi Mawut, each piled high with a hearty mix of rice, noodles, eggs, and chili. Cooked in his own kitchen, it wasn't just food — it was a gesture of solidarity.

Seated on the plastic chairs, they ate. The chili's heat mingled with the bitterness of black coffee, and the men passed around six boxes of Kretek, finishing them within a few hours. It wasn't just a meal — it was a communal act, a tangible reminder that in grief, community endures.

Field Notes

The next morning's coexistence rests on decades of quiet reciprocity the deceased herself built.

There was no tension the next morning, and the piece traces why.

Neighbors, predominantly Muslim, sat quietly outside as the Christian family conducted the burial rites inside. Hymns floated through the morning air, carried by the voices of the congregation. There was no tension, no sense of division — only shared reverence.

Asked why so many had come, one villager answered simply: "The family was always kind." Every Christmas, she prepared gifts for the neighbors. During Lebaran, the neighbors brought ketupat, and she always sent a token of thanks in return. When misfortune struck any family in the RT, she discreetly offered financial help. She had lived there since 1965, perhaps longer than most of them, and they did not seem to see her being Christian as threatening.

One of the senior women added a verse from her own tradition: "La kum dinukum waliya din", "For you is your religion, and for me is my religion" (Surah Al-Kafirun 109:6) — a verse, she explained, about mutual respect and religious tolerance.

What It Revealed

'Wong Urip Urup' reframes collectivism's supposed enemy of innovation as active contribution, not passive conformity.

As someone who often critiques collectivism for stifling innovation, the author found a different perspective in this instance.

"Wong Urip Urup" fits harmoniously within collectivism's emphasis on contributing to the well-being of the group, but adds a dimension collectivism alone lacks: active contribution rather than passive conformity. While collectivism can hinder innovation by preserving group norms, "Wong Urip Urup" encourages individuals to be proactive in serving the community — a bridge between the relational aspects of collectivism and the need for contribution and positive impact.

This practice of communal mourning is echoed worldwide — the Philippines' Bayanihan spirit, Mexico's velorios, and traditions across Africa and South Asia — showing that grief becomes lighter when shared, and that the instinct to comfort transcends religion or geography.

Innovation is often associated with individuality and breaking from tradition, yet the solidarity shown that night and morning revealed another path — one rooted in shared purpose and connection, challenging the notion that progress must always be individualistic or disruptive.

What I Carry

Collectivism, at its purest, is about humanity — a comfort innovation and individuality alone could not offer.

The piece closes on what stayed with the author rather than on a resolved argument about collectivism.

What lingers
For now, the debates about collectivism can wait. What lingers is the memory of people who, though not family, became companions in grief — Wong Urip Urup, to live is to bring light, lived rather than merely spoken.
