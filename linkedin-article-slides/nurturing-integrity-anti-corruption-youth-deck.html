LinkedIn Article  ·  Anti-Corruption · Starting With Children, Grounded in Pancasila  ·  9 August 2023

Nurturing Integrity: Instilling Anti-Corruption Values in Indonesian Youth

The article argues that anti-corruption education has to begin in childhood. It sets out seven harms corruption does to a citizen, then fifteen harms at societal, corporate and individual levels, each paired with a behavioural-economics bias, and concludes that addressing corruption means addressing those biases. It finds Pancasila already supplies aligned values, proposes six concepts taught through ten activity formats, and gives eight steps for a child whose father is involved in corruption. An appendix adds the limit of structural reform: where corruption is already cultural, decentralised government can reinforce it, which the article ties to imitating institutions without laying a foundation of shared values.

Introduction

Corruption damages a citizen's income, services, trust, security, cohesion and rights, so prevention has to start with children.

The article gives its reason for starting early in one sentence: "it's imperative to cultivate anti-corruption values from a young age". It then lists seven ways corruption endangers individual citizens.

1  ·  Economic impact
Inefficient use of resources and economic stagnation, which affect job opportunities and income.
2  ·  Access to public services
Funds meant for healthcare and education are diverted, so essential services are harder to reach.
3  ·  Inequality
A privileged few benefit at the expense of the majority.
4  ·  Loss of trust
Widespread corruption erodes trust in government and public institutions.
5  ·  Security concerns
Law enforcement and the judiciary are weakened, leaving a sense of vulnerability.
6  ·  Social cohesion
A sense of unfairness and injustice can break down cohesion in the community.
7  ·  Diminished rights and freedoms
Corrupt officials may abuse their power, which hinders citizens from exercising their rights.

Framework

Biases operate at societal, corporate and individual levels, so addressing corruption means addressing them.

The article's conclusion states the requirement: "Addressing corruption requires understanding and addressing these biases while promoting ethical behavior and transparency at all levels of society, corporations, and individuals".

01
Five harms, five biases
Societal level
Erosion of trust with confirmation bias; impaired development with status quo bias; inequality with in-group bias; a weakened rule of law with anchoring bias; political instability with loss aversion.
02
Five harms, five biases
Corporate level
Reduced fair competition with prospect theory; decreased investor confidence with herding behavior; ethical erosion with moral licensing; financial losses with overconfidence bias; reputation damage with negativity bias.
03
Five harms, five biases
Individual level
Loss of opportunities with self-serving bias; compromised integrity with cognitive dissonance; legal consequences with present bias; psychological impact with regret aversion; the cycle of corruption with intergenerational equity bias.
How the pairings are worded

The article words every pairing as a possibility and offers no data for any of them. Two run against corruption: regret aversion "might make individuals avoid engaging in corrupt activities to prevent future emotional distress", and the intergenerational equity bias is said to encourage individuals to break the cycle.

Details

Pancasila does not name corruption, and its five principles still align with the fight against it.

The article makes the alignment with a stated limit: Pancasila has no explicit anti-corruption principle, but "the values it promotes align with anti-corruption efforts".

01
Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa
Belief in the One and Only God
Encourages ethical behavior, honesty and integrity, which are "essential elements in the fight against corruption".
02
Kemanusiaan yang Adil dan Beradab
Just and Civilized Humanity
Emphasises justice, fairness and respect for others, all of which run counter to corrupt practices.
03
Persatuan Indonesia
Unity of Indonesia
Corruption can undermine national unity by eroding trust in institutions, so unity requires transparent and accountable governance.
04
Kerakyatan yang Dipimpin oleh Hikmat Kebijaksanaan
Democracy guided by inner wisdom
A democratic society built on informed decision-making and representation is said to be less prone to corruption.
05
Keadilan Sosial bagi Seluruh Rakyat Indonesia
Social justice for all
Corruption can worsen social inequalities, and the principle stresses equitable treatment of all citizens.

Implications

The proposal teaches six concepts through ten activity formats.

The proposal is an education framework for children, set out as concepts to teach and activities through which to teach them.

01
What is taught
Six concepts
Integrity and honesty; accountability; transparency; fairness and equality; resisting peer pressure; and empathy and compassion.
02
How it is taught
Ten activity formats
Ethical dilemma scenarios, role-playing, storytelling, debate, values collage, case studies, community projects, art and creativity, interactive quizzes and guest speakers.
Evidence note

The article proposes this framework and reports no pilot, class or result from using it.

Details

A child confronting a father's involvement is advised to seek a trusted adult first and to protect personal safety.

The article works one example through in eight steps, offering "a simplified approach" for a child who faces the ethical dilemma of a father's involvement in corruption.

1  ·  Talk to a trusted adult
Choose an adult outside the immediate family, such as a teacher, school counselor or community leader.
2  ·  Ask questions
Try calmly and respectfully to understand why certain things are happening.
3  ·  Express feelings
Share emotions with the father, using "I" statements.
4  ·  Suggest positive actions together
For example community service or supporting charitable initiatives, which can encourage more ethical choices.
5  ·  Find positive role models
Look for stories of people who made a positive impact by choosing ethical paths.
6  ·  Stay kind and respectful
Focus on actions rather than criticising the person.
7  ·  Keep learning
Understanding of right and wrong is a continuous process that deepens with age.
8  ·  Seek professional help if needed
If the child feels unsafe or needs more support, safety and well-being come first.

Conclusion

Where corruption is already cultural, decentralised government reinforces it, so a foundation of shared values has to come first.

The appendix, headed as a note on shared values in dealing with cancer-like corruption, is where the article states the limit of structural reform.

What happen is (bitterly true) it has reenforced corruption instead of balancing them

— Nurturing Integrity, appendix
The comparison it starts from
The article notes that some countries with a centralized government have "a relatively lower corruption index than that of very democratic and extreme autonomy countries". No source is given for the claim.
The reversal
Its example is "country B, let's say", a country with a decentralized government whose culture, from all strata, has inherited corruption. Decentralisation there is said to have reinforced corruption rather than balanced it.
The hedged form
The paragraph that follows states it conditionally: "Decentralized government in a context where corruption is deeply ingrained in the culture can potentially lead to reinforcement rather than mitigation of corruption". Six mechanisms are listed: cultural norms, limited oversight, networks of patronage, lack of awareness, resource allocation and misuse of authority.
How the main text closes
Before the appendix, the conclusion says that by instilling anti-corruption values from an early age, "we can contribute to a just, prosperous, and corruption-free Indonesia, in alignment with the ideals of Pancasila".
