Indonesia's Energy Dilemma: The Gas Price Crisis Facing Java's Manufacturers

Java's manufacturers pay roughly four times what Vietnam's do for gas despite a domestic electricity surplus, and three reinforcing feedback loops explain why — export contracts and oligopoly power keep supply locked up while the balancing loops meant to fix it are delayed by infrastructure lead times measured in years.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Java's manufacturers pay up to four times what Vietnam's do for gas — despite a domestic electricity surplus.

As of 2023, industrial users in Java are paying between $11.89 and $12.52 per MMBTU, versus roughly $3 per MMBTU in Thailand and Vietnam.

This price disparity severely undermines the competitiveness of Indonesian manufacturers, despite the country having a surplus of electricity. The Java-Bali grid has an installed generation capacity of around 35 GW, with a current overcapacity of about 4 GW — meaning demand is lower than supply, and planned additions of 10.252 GW would only widen that gap.

This article explores why this is happening through the lens of system dynamics, a method that helps explain how interconnected factors — supply, demand, infrastructure, and government policy — keep prices high even as electricity sits in surplus.

Framework

Three reinforcing loops lock in high prices; two balancing loops meant to fix it are delayed for years.

The Causal Loop Diagram identifies five loops governing the market.

LOOP A
R1 — Gas prices and competitiveness
Higher gas prices raise energy and operational costs, reducing manufacturing competitiveness and output. Falling demand should in theory lower gas prices — but supply constraints mean it doesn't.
LOOP B
R2 / R3 — Export commitments and domestic scarcity
Long-term export contracts, particularly with East Asian buyers, tie up a significant share of gas production. Higher prices make exports even more lucrative, reinforcing the very commitments that keep domestic supply constrained.
LOOP C
B1 — Infrastructure investment (delayed)
Rising demand should pressure infrastructure investment, which expands pipeline capacity and raises domestic supply. But pipelines, regasification units and storage facilities take years to plan, approve and build — the balancing effect arrives too late to matter in the short term.
LOOP D
B2 — Pipeline capacity vs. export constraints
Even as pipeline capacity grows, export constraints continue to limit how much of that capacity actually serves domestic users — a balancing loop that is itself throttled by the reinforcing loops above it.

Details

Temporary fixes like floating storage units perpetuate the very cycle they're meant to relieve.

FSRUs provide a short-term supply boost, but their high operational costs feed straight back into energy costs.

FSRUs (Floating Storage and Regasification Units) temporarily increase domestic gas supply to address immediate shortages. But the short-term nature of FSRUs adds to operational costs due to their high maintenance and operational expenses — and those higher operational costs raise overall energy costs, which in turn affects gas prices. Although FSRUs provide a temporary fix, they do not resolve the systemic problem of supply constraints and high prices; instead, they perpetuate the reinforcing loops by adding to operational costs.

A small number of players — state-owned enterprises like PGN and a few upstream producers — control most of Indonesia's gas market. This oligopolistic structure gives these entities significant pricing power, allowing them to maintain high prices even when domestic demand falls, preventing the market from adjusting the way a competitive market would.

Gap

The state-controlled system built to shield citizens from market volatility now behaves like a quasi-market that keeps prices high.

Indonesia's constitution emphasizes the state's responsibility to control natural resources for the welfare of the people — the current mechanism is not functioning as intended.

The government's inability to provide sufficient subsidies and regulate prices across all sectors means industries are left exposed to high gas costs, despite the constitutional mandate to prioritize the public's welfare. The state's reliance on export revenues, combined with limited domestic infrastructure capacity, has led to a situation where global markets effectively dictate domestic gas prices — the opposite of what was envisioned in the constitution.

This reveals a deeper disconnect between the constitutional intent — to use natural resources for the welfare of the people — and the reality of policy execution. The gas market is neither fully free nor fully controlled in a way that benefits domestic users; it is stuck in a middle ground where the inefficiencies of both systems combine to create a dysfunctional outcome.

Implications

PLN and PGN each manage their piece of the system linearly, missing the loops that connect them.

PLN's electricity surplus does not translate into cheap power because the gas feeding it remains expensive; PGN's short-term fixes delay the infrastructure investment that could actually help.

PLN, which manages Indonesia's electricity grid, oversees a power mix that includes coal, renewables, and natural gas. Even with an electricity surplus, gas-powered electricity remains expensive due to the high price of natural gas — a reinforcing loop where high gas prices increase electricity costs for manufacturers, reducing their output, while gas supply remains constrained and prices stay high.

PGN controls gas distribution and has raised prices for industrial users, citing upstream supply shortages and the high costs of FSRUs. But this approach overlooks the balancing loop that could emerge from long-term infrastructure development — investing in expanded pipelines and better storage facilities could eventually lower costs, yet PGN's reliance on short-term fixes delays these critical infrastructure projects, keeping prices elevated for longer.

Conclusion

There is no silver bullet — only systemic, adaptive policy that accounts for delay.

Interventions must be adaptive and iterative, recognizing that changes in one part of the system will have ripple effects elsewhere.

Rather than accelerating infrastructure in isolation, the government must evaluate how infrastructure development will interact with export policies, domestic demand, and pricing regulations. Reconsidering export contracts could make more gas available for domestic use, but this alone will not resolve the issue — changes in export policy must be accompanied by parallel efforts to improve infrastructure, adjust subsidy allocations, and address the oligopoly structure of the gas market.

Indonesia's energy crisis requires systemic thinking — recognizing the feedback loops, delays, and complex interactions at play. By embracing a long-term, adaptive approach, Indonesia can begin to untangle the intricate web of policies and constraints that keep gas prices elevated, ultimately fulfilling the constitutional mandate to use natural resources for the welfare of its people.
