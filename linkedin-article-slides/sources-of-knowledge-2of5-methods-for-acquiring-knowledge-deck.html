(2 of 5) Methods for Acquiring Knowledge: Science is Only One of So Many Others

Five methods beyond science each map onto a component of Ruth Benedict's own theory of culture, and science itself is barely four centuries old as a formal method — balancing science with these other methods, not subordinating them to it, is what a mature epistemology requires.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Framework

Five methods beyond science each offer a distinct, legitimate path to knowledge.

Science is only one method within a broader epistemological spectrum.

01
01
Philosophical reflection
Logical reasoning, ethical intuition, and metaphysical exploration — deriving insight through thought and contemplation rather than empirical testing.
02
02
Historical analysis
Interpreting records, documents, and artifacts to reconstruct the past, contingent on interpretation rather than experimentation.
03
03
Artistic interpretation
Knowledge from subjective experience and creative expression — literature, painting, music, and film convey insight that cannot be easily measured.
04
04
Religious or spiritual experience
Knowledge based on faith, revelation, and sacred texts, addressing questions of existence and morality that empirical methods are not equipped to provide.
05
05
Cultural practices
Rituals, festivals, oral histories, and social norms, passed down and acquired informally through participation rather than scientific study.

Details

Each method maps onto a component of Ruth Benedict's own theory of culture.

Benedict's Patterns of Culture (1934) describes culture as an integrated pattern of values, customs, language, and institutions — and the five methods align with it directly.

1  ·  Philosophical reflection = Values and beliefs
Abstract reasoning about ethics and existence mirrors the cultural component of values and beliefs — the core ideas about what is right, wrong, or real.
2  ·  Historical analysis = Shared history and experiences
Studying the past corresponds to a society's collective memory, allowing deeper understanding of how prior generations shaped the present.
3  ·  Artistic interpretation = Art and creative expression
Literature, music, and visual arts convey knowledge, emotions, and narratives that help societies reflect on their collective identity.
4  ·  Religious experience = Religious and spiritual beliefs
Faith and spirituality provide knowledge about existence, morality, and the divine, shaping a culture's worldview and ethics.
5  ·  Cultural practices = Customs, traditions, norms, institutions
Rituals, festivals, and social roles pass down knowledge and values informally, shaping how individuals behave within their culture.
6  ·  Science = Technology and material culture
Scientific methods produce practical knowledge and tools, shaping a society's technological identity and capability.

Evidence

Science, now the most valued method, is relatively young compared to the others it is measured against.

The appendix traces science's own formalization, from ancient observation to a structured method.

384–322 BCE
Aristotle
Emphasized observation and classification in the study of the natural world, developing inductive reasoning — an essential element of scientific inquiry today.
c. 460–370 BCE
Hippocrates
Introduced a rational, evidence-based approach to medicine, using observation and diagnosis grounded in clinical experience rather than supernatural explanation.
1620
Francis Bacon
Formalized the empirical method in Novum Organum, emphasizing observation, experimentation, and inductive reasoning — laying the groundwork for systematic collection and testing of evidence.
1596–1650
René Descartes
Contributed deductive reasoning, focusing on logic and mathematics, advocating doubt and questioning as part of gaining knowledge.
1564–1727
Galileo Galilei and Isaac Newton
Advanced the method further by applying rigorous mathematical frameworks and controlled experiments, shifting the focus from tradition and authority to evidence and experimentation.

Conclusion

Balancing science with other methods is what a mature epistemology requires.

Recognizing multiple methods does not diminish science; it locates it.

Integrating diverse methods helps create a holistic view of human experience, blending empirical evidence with emotional, spiritual, and cultural insights. This balance is crucial for expanding the boundaries of what we consider valuable knowledge and ensuring we appreciate the multifaceted nature of understanding the world.

Science remains a powerful tool for understanding the natural world through empirical evidence and systematic inquiry. However, recognizing and embracing other approaches prevents us from narrowing our perspective and allows for a more inclusive, dynamic approach to knowledge.
