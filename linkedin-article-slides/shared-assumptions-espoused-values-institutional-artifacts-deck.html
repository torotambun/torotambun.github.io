Shared Assumptions, Espoused Values, and Institutional Artifacts in Education Policy: A Response to A (former) Minister's Keynote Speech

A policy speech on university transformation stays rhetorical because Indonesia's institutional artifacts — its KPIs, hierarchies and incentive structures — were never derived from a shift in the country's own shared cultural assumptions. South Korea, Japan and Finland each attempted the same performance-to-innovation transition, and their divergent outcomes confirm the same rule: policy sticks only once assumptions move first, not after.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A university reform speech spoke only in espoused values, never in shared assumptions.

On 7 February 2025, a senior government official who has since resigned delivered a policy address at a prominent Indonesian university, outlining a vision for universities to be more engaged with society, innovative and accountable.

Situation

The speech emphasized social responsibility, real-world impact and inclusive education — aspirations that sound normative because they never touch the institutional artifacts that actually govern university behavior.

Complication

Instead, "universities function as performance engines, where success is measured primarily through Scopus-indexed publications, accreditation standards, and bureaucratic KPIs, rather than through their ability to solve real-world problems or drive social transformation."

Question

Can a policy vision produce cultural transformation if the artifacts it leaves in place were never derived from a shift in shared assumptions?

Answer

No. More importantly, "the artifacts governing higher education were never derived from deeply held shared assumptions." Hierarchical thinking, risk aversion, corruption as a shared assumption, shortcut mentality and exclusive collectivism remain untouched.

Gap

Three ingrained cultural constraints go unnamed in the speech itself.

Espoused values alone cannot drive reform unless they are backed by shifts in deep-seated cultural norms.

Corruption as a shared assumption — integrity and transparency were mentioned, but the system's embedded informal practices still hinder merit-based governance.

Shortcut mentality — the speech calls for long-term impact, yet the system still incentivizes short-term outputs for quick policy wins.

Exclusive collectivism — universities are encouraged to be inclusive, yet decision-making remains insular, favoring entrenched networks.

Framework

A performance engine cannot become an innovation engine by adding a mission statement.

Drawing on Vijay Govindarajan's framework (Govindarajan & Trimble, 2010).

Universities in Indonesia operate primarily as performance engines, focused on efficiency, predictability and measurable outputs. A parallel innovation engine — one built for agility, experimentation and real-world engagement — is required alongside it.

"The performance engine is designed for efficiency and predictability, whereas the innovation engine must be built for flexibility and experimentation" (Govindarajan & Trimble, 2010, p. 25).

But the very need for an innovation engine becomes its own obstacle: hierarchical decision-making, exclusive collectivism, shortcut mentality, a lack of competitiveness and an unstable reform culture create structural resistance before the engine ever starts.

Evidence

South Korea shifted assumptions first; Japan tried programs without them; Finland proves the sequence.

Three countries attempted the same performance-to-innovation transition, with different outcomes.

CASE A
South Korea — assumptions shifted, and incentives followed
The government incentivized university-industry collaboration on applied research rather than just Scopus-indexed output, and funding shifted from meeting quotas to technology commercialization. Companies like Samsung, LG and Hyundai pushed for problem-solving graduates over credentialism. "Korea's success came from aligning incentives with deeper shifts in academic and industrial culture."
CASE B
Japan — programs launched, assumptions untouched
The Super Global University Program (2014) aimed to increase university-industry collaboration, but senior professors and administrators maintained top-down control, discouraging risk-taking by younger researchers. Japan's case proves that "implementing programs alone does not work" — cultural inertia blocks transformation unless shared assumptions shift first.
CASE C
Finland — the sequence, not just the content
Universities were given autonomy to focus on education quality rather than government targets; research culture moved from individual KPIs to cross-disciplinary collaboration. Finland's case proves that "real transformation happens when shared assumptions shift first, not after. Policies followed cultural change, not the other way around."

Implications

Four recommendations follow directly from what the case studies show.

Bridging the gap between espoused values and institutional artifacts.

1  ·  Redefine university KPIs to align with societal impact
Introduce alternative performance metrics that reward community engagement and applied research, and prioritize problem-solving over publication counts.
2  ·  Institutionalize an innovation engine
Create independent, agile teams within universities to run experimental programs and industry partnerships, and reduce the bureaucratic barriers that block external collaboration.
3  ·  Cultural and governance reforms
Address corruption explicitly through transparent governance and accountability frameworks, shift from a shortcuts-to-compliance mindset toward long-term capability building, and encourage inclusive collectivism that keeps meritocracy intact.
4  ·  Policy-level adjustments
The relevant governing body must adjust national performance assessments to emphasize transformation over bureaucratic fulfillment, and funding models should reward demonstrated real-world impact rather than administrative achievement.

Conclusion

True cultural transformation takes fifty to seventy-five years — two to three generations.

The paper's closing measure of the timescale involved.

Transformation is not just about changing policies—it is about shifting how success is defined, how behaviors are incentivized, and how deeply rooted cultural assumptions are reexamined.

— Closing conclusion of the white paper

"The policy address sets an ambitious vision, but without structural realignment, it risks remaining rhetorical."
