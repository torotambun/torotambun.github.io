Autoethnographic Lessons from a Memorable Meeting with fellow Harvardians in Jakarta (Part 1 of 2)

A lunch among elite alumni is treated as a field site rather than as a network event. What separated the room from the country around it was not wealth or credentials but reading: eight books were referenced across one meal. The observer's own method — arrive, notice, stay silent — is the argument, and the discipline he admires in the wealthiest person present is the refusal of shortcuts.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

An invitation arrived with the wrong month in it, and I took two days to answer.

The article begins with an exchange of messages and reads the timing of the replies as data.

The invitation is reproduced. A Harvard classmate was visiting Jakarta and "asked me if I knew some Harvard folks who might be interested in lunch and/or pre-dinner drinks." The date given was wrong; two hours later the correction arrived in two words — "Oops, yes July."

The reply times are recorded precisely: eleven minutes from him, two days to confirm attendance. And they are read rather than reported. "Reflecting on this interaction, I realized how the fast-paced nature of professional life often clashes with the need for thoughtful engagement. The quick response time required in such exchanges highlights the immediacy and pressure of maintaining social and professional networks, particularly among elite educational circles."

The protagonist is anonymised

The article names the visiting classmate as "Ling Wei (an anonymized name)". Part 2 of this series states that all names and identifying details have been changed. The deck uses that name and adds no identification.

The Scene

I arrived late in my morning clothes, with a waterproof field note and an astronaut pen.

The self-observation begins before the meeting does, and the detail it fixes on is what he is wearing.

"Normally, I would wear my running attire for informal gatherings, feeling more comfortable and authentic." In the hurry he did not change out of the trousers and t-shirt from his morning meetings, and the effect is recorded: "This minor oversight left me feeling slightly out of place, a theme that would resonate throughout the meeting." The instruments came even when the clothes did not — "I did manage to bring my waterproof field note and an astronaut pen to take notes".

The reading he draws from it is about the room rather than about himself. The episode "highlighted the tension between maintaining authenticity and conforming to expected norms, a common theme in professional and social interactions within elite circles."

The arrival is described in the vocabulary of instrumentation. Expecting a small gathering and finding a table set for six, "As an introvert, walking into a larger group heightened my alertness, akin to a radio tower antenna and radar measuring the energy around me." The staff merged two tables to make room.

Field Notes

A turquoise linen shirt, a 42mm iWatch with the battery saver off, and no makeup.

The identification of the guest of honour is made by observation, at a level of detail that is the method rather than the decoration.

OBSERVED A
How she was identified
"I identified Ling Wei by her turquoise linen shirt, two buttons open, a few necklaces, and a 42mm iWatch without battery saver mode, as I could see the measurements on the watch face." The battery-saver detail is the tell that the observation is real: it can only be known from the face being lit.
OBSERVED B
The questions the observation raised
"What makes a billionaire wear simple yet elegant attire? The watch is not fancy; why doesn't she wear luxurious watches as Asian rich normally do?" The article puts these as questions and leaves them as questions in this part.
OBSERVED C
What the plainness is read as
Her "simplicity and composure suggested a confidence that transcends material displays of wealth, challenging the conventional markers of status often seen in Asian societies." The article frames this as "a shift in values, where intellectual and social capital can be as significant as financial wealth."
OBSERVED D
His own error, recorded
Greeting a PhD candidate in anthropology from East Asia, he records saying Kamsamida instead of Annyeonghaseyo — thanking her rather than greeting her. He keeps the mistake in, as evidence of "the potential for small missteps to impact perceptions."

The Contrast

A Hermes shirt and a gold Audemars Piguet sat at the same table.

The two ways of holding wealth were both in the room, and the article declines to make the comparison a verdict on either person.

What was worn
•A turquoise linen shirt, a few necklaces, a basic 42mm smartwatch, no makeup
•"a white Hermes long-sleeve t-shirt and a gold Audemars Piguet watch with a leather strap on his left wrist"
•A sunburn "typical of a golfer"
•The observer himself, still in the trousers and t-shirt from his morning meetings
What was done
✓"He had supported my publications and posts for years" — known until then only by name and by WhatsApp
✓He moved his chair closer for almost twenty minutes of private conversation, "asking a few questions about my well-being and whereabouts"
✓"What I can write here is that he shows genuine interest in people"
✓"Being an affluent person, he also has a foundation that serves refugees in Indonesia for years"
The judgement he does make

"One of the few HBS alumni in Indonesia who genuinely extend support." It is a compliment with an implicit count inside it, and the article does not soften the implication.

The Turns

I stayed silent, which is how you learn: be the stupidest in the room.

The method of the piece is stated as a choice made at the table, and the table's subject matter is what makes the choice pay.

"I remained mostly silent, absorbing the intellectual exchange." What he absorbed was a conversation on Malay as a lingua franca since the fifteenth century, the Sumpah Pemuda of 1928 and Bahasa Indonesia as a unifying second language, and a debate on education under Dutch and Japanese colonial rule in which the participants agreed "that the Dutch showed little interest in educating the grassroots." A story about a parrot retrained from Dutch to Indonesian is recorded as "symbolizing the broader cultural shifts and adaptations within Indonesian society."

The reason for the silence is given without modesty and without false modesty: "It was a reminder of the value of listening, of being the “stupidest” in the room to learn from others." The article treats this as a requirement rather than a temperament — "the humility required to truly learn and the importance of creating spaces where diverse voices can be heard."

There was also a discipline at the table about what he ate. Mindful of Proverbs 23:1-2, he took one piece of meat for politeness; observing that nobody drank alcohol, he ordered sparkling water. "From time to time, I was asked politely to start eating, which I kept delaying, lost in the rich exchange of ideas."

The Discipline

She was assigned the lowest job three times before she demanded fair treatment.

The business account is short, and what the article admires in it is a refusal rather than an achievement.

The brands are named and the route is specific: successful in China, and "For her to bring the brands to other countries, the route must be through Hong Kong and Singapore." The article's judgement on that is comparative — "I admired that discipline of scaling, very different from many startups that provide nonsensical scaling plans."

The career behind it is the part that carries. "Despite being the daughter of a tycoon and having a degree from Harvard University," she started at the bottom of the corporate pyramid. "After being assigned three times to the lowest corporate positions, she demanded fair treatment." Only then was she given a company to run, which produced a successful high-end fashion brand — a journey that "began almost two decades ago."

The sentence that generalises it: "There is no reminiscence of shortcuts and premature victory laps in her story." Against that, the article's diagnosis of the founders he usually meets — many "lack a clear sense-making of how to scale or even a coherent go-to-market strategy." The journey, he writes, "truly begins only after securing a set of paying customers, validating the next ten customers, and proving resilience in a competitive market."

A second thing she suggested

That a researcher may wish to become a Key Opinion Leader — "or as commonly referred to now, an “influencer.”" The conversation also turned on raison d'être, and on "cherishing the research process, the writing process, the publication process, and the dissemination process" — finding happiness in the pursuit rather than pursuing happiness.

What It Revealed

Eight books were referenced over one lunch, and that is the gap.

The count is the finding of the piece, and the conclusion drawn from it is the least comfortable sentence in the article.

Ling Wei named books she had read or listened to across the conversation, and he noted the moment she said she had listened to one as an audiobook. "Her extensive reading list underscored her intellectual rigor."

Then the count, and the inference: "To realize eight books were referred to during the conversation was a reminder of what makes most of the population of this nation lag behind: they don't read as much. They prefer shallow knowledge through easy means rather than in-depth reading."

It is stated as an observation about a nation on the evidence of one table, and the article does not qualify it. What it does add is a mechanism: reflecting on "the theories of synaptic plasticity and how exposure to diverse intellectual stimuli can shape one’s mental models," the discussion is read as contributing "to the continuous development of our cognitive frameworks."

The list itself is not in the record

The eight titles appear in the source as an image and do not survive text extraction. The deck reports the count the article gives and names no book.

What I Carry

Reading is travel, and most travellers come home unchanged.

The piece closes on two borrowed lines and one of its own, and the one of its own is the sharpest.

Travel is the fatal enemy of prejudice, as Mark Twain famously said.

— Autoethnographic Lessons from a Memorable Meeting with fellow Harvardians in Jakarta, 30 July 2024
The correction he makes to it
"Contrary to what many people believe, traveling is not just a physical act; it should also expand the mind. Unfortunately, many travelers return unchanged, seeing no enrichment or revelation in their experiences." Reading is offered as the substitute that works: it "allows people to travel to other parts of the world, encounter different views, opinions, and contrasting beliefs."
The condition for it to work
"As Aristotle wisely noted, “It is the mark of an educated mind to be able to entertain a thought without accepting it.”" The article adds that this ability "to welcome new ideas without necessarily having to change one’s own beliefs is crucial for intellectual growth."
