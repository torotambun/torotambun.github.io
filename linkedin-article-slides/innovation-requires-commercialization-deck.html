Innovation Requires Commercialization: Schumpeter, Roberts, and Indonesian Law Are Unambiguous

A dictionary defines innovation as novelty, which is why it is the wrong instrument for policy or strategy. Schumpeter separated invention from innovation and made economic realisation the condition; Roberts and Aulet expressed it as a multiplication, where a zero on either term yields nothing; and Indonesian law codifies the same distinction. Innovation without commercialization is not innovation. It is unutilized possibility.

Introduction

A general dictionary is the wrong instrument for defining innovation.

"Relying on a general dictionary like Merriam-Webster to define innovation in economic or management contexts is misleading."

Situation

"Dictionaries are descriptive—they reflect how words are used in everyday language, not how concepts are defined within theoretical or operational frameworks."

Complication

Popular definitions such as Merriam-Webster's — innovation as "a new idea, method, or device" — "prioritize surface-level novelty and omit the requirement of market realization or economic impact".

Question

If the everyday definition is inadequate for academic, policy or strategic contexts, what is the definition that those contexts actually require?

Answer

"In contrast, Schumpeter's work, and later Roberts' empirical investigations, provide a formal, grounded, and tested definition with clear implications for innovation ecosystems, industrial policy, and entrepreneurship strategy."

Schumpeter

Schumpeter separated invention from innovation and required economic realization.

"Joseph Schumpeter is widely recognized as the intellectual father of innovation theory within economics."

His work "laid the foundational understanding of innovation not as a synonym for invention, but as a distinct economic function that transforms novel ideas into market value". In The Theory of Economic Development (1934) he identified five categories: "introducing a new product, employing a new method of production, opening a new market, accessing new sources of raw material, and restructuring an industry".

"Each of these innovations, according to Schumpeter, requires not only novelty but also economic realization" — concrete application that changes market behaviour and generates value.

In Business Cycles (1939) "he clarified that innovation is the market introduction of a novelty, not the novelty itself". "He rejected the idea that invention alone constitutes innovation, arguing instead that innovation can occur without any invention, and that invention may fail to result in innovation." He reaffirmed it in 1942, "where he defined innovation as the implementation of a new production function—explicitly distinguishing it from invention or discovery".

Roberts and Aulet

Roberts and Aulet made it a multiplication, not an addition.

"This framework directly supports the formulation used by Aulet and Roberts: Innovation = Invention × Commercialization."

"Aulet (2013), in his book Disciplined Entrepreneurship, adopts this formula as a foundational principle for building innovation-driven ventures. He attributes this formulation to the earlier empirical work of Edward B. Roberts."

Roberts, in Entrepreneurs in High Technology (1991) and more explicitly in Managing Invention and Innovation (2007), "argued that innovation requires both invention and exploitation—where exploitation refers to the economic and commercial realization of ideas. Without this step, Roberts contends, invention alone does not constitute innovation."

The form of the expression is doing work. "The multiplication logic employed by Aulet and Roberts is not rhetorical; it reflects Schumpeter's position that without commercialization, innovation does not occur." A multiplication by zero yields zero — which is why "commercialization is not an optional element—it is a necessary condition for innovation to take place".

The Law

Indonesian law codifies the same distinction in Sisnas IPTEK.

"This position is not only supported by economic theory and entrepreneurship research—it is also codified in Indonesian national law."

Undang-Undang Republik Indonesia Nomor 12 Tahun 2019 tentang Sistem Nasional Ilmu Pengetahuan dan Teknologi, Article 1, defines innovation as "hasil invensi yang telah diterapkan dan memberikan manfaat ekonomi dan/atau sosial" — the article's own translation: "the result of an invention that has been applied and provides economic and/or social benefit".

"This statutory definition confirms that Indonesia's legal framework distinguishes between invention (an idea or discovery) and innovation (its application and utility)."

"In the eyes of the law, innovation without applied benefit—commercial, societal, or both—is not innovation. This legal position harmonizes precisely with Schumpeter's original distinction and Roberts' empirical demonstration."

What the statute adds to the theory

The law admits social benefit alongside economic benefit — so the Indonesian definition is broader than a purely commercial reading of Schumpeter, while holding the same structural requirement that something must have been applied.

Conclusion

Innovation without commercialization is unutilized possibility.

"Schumpeter, Roberts, Aulet, and Indonesian law all converge on the same conclusion: novelty alone is not enough."

Innovation without commercialization is not innovation. It is unutilized possibility.

— Innovation Requires Commercialization: Schumpeter, Roberts, and Indonesian Law Are Unambiguous
What the requirement actually is
"Innovation must be transformative, which means it must be applied and useful in economic or societal terms." Not admired, not funded, not announced — applied.
What treating it as optional costs
"Treating commercialization as optional—or worse, ignoring it—confuses ideation with execution, creativity with consequence, and potential with actualization." Three distinct category errors, each of which lets an organisation believe it has innovated when it has not.
Why this is not a matter of preference
"Preferring Schumpeter's framework—and by extension, definitions that include commercialization—is not a matter of preference or style. It is a matter of disciplinary accuracy, legal coherence, and strategic relevance."
