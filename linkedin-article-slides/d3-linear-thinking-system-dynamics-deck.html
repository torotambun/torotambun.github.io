D-3 Linear Thinking Still? See System Dynamics for Complex, Interconnected, Delayed, Feedback and Policy Resistance Wicked Problems

I published fourteen minutes of someone else's speech at the end of the year because it names the reason well-intentioned fixes keep failing — and because the discipline it asks for, refusing to lower the goal when the gap is exhausting, is the one a new year actually requires.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

I published someone else's fourteen minutes because it names why our fixes keep failing.

This article is almost entirely a transcript of Professor John Sterman's speech at the 2024 MIT Sustainability Summit. What follows first is the reason for carrying it.

The framing given in the companion introduction post: it is a fourteen-minute masterclass "on how well-intentioned policies often fall victim to policy resistance, where the very structure of systems undermines interventions and exacerbates problems."

"These examples highlight the limitations of conventional approaches in a world where nothing is truly linear—feedback, delays, interconnections, complexity, and policy resistance shape every system."

And the reason it belongs at the end of a year rather than anywhere else: "Let this serve as a call to action as we embrace the new year with renewed purpose and resolve."

What the remaining sections set out is Sterman's argument, attributed to him throughout. The claim being made by publishing it is the one above — that conventional problem-solving is failing in a way that has a name, and that the name is worth carrying into January.

Why system dynamics rather than better analysis

"Unlike conventional, linear thinking—which often focuses on symptoms and leads to short-sighted interventions—system dynamics examines feedback loops, delays, and the interconnected nature of systems. It provides actionable insights by addressing root causes and redesigning systems for lasting, meaningful change."

The Mechanism

Policy resistance is his term for a system's structure undermining the intervention made in good faith.

Sterman opens on the pattern rather than the theory: problems we have tried to solve "over and over again", with policies "we think are best, but they're not working."

"This is a phenomenon that Jay Forrester, the founder of system dynamics, and the late Donella Meadows, my mentor, called policy resistance."

"Complex systems resist and work to undermine many of the interventions that people in good faith try to use to solve pressing problems."

The scope he gives it is deliberately wide — "whether it's climate change, getting green buildings going, cement (as you were just hearing), food, or our businesses struggling" — and it includes the private case: "our personal lives might not be what we need or want them to be, and we've been trying to solve that repeatedly."

The definition does real work because of what it excludes. The intervention is not failing through incompetence, bad faith or insufficient effort. It is failing because the system responds to it.

The Examples

His three examples all show the intervention producing the opposite of its intent.

Sterman offers three cases, each one a well-understood remedy that made its own problem worse.

CASE A
Traffic congestion
"Let's build more roads!" — and then: "Well, we all know what happens" — "you get more traffic." The capacity added is consumed by the demand it invites.
CASE B
Agricultural chemicals
Pesticides and herbicides "have been miraculous in increasing crop yields around the world. But now there are resistant weeds and pests. We're stuck in a vicious cycle: we apply more and more chemicals, and the weeds and pests become increasingly resistant." He names the mechanism: "What we've created is unnatural selection, where those pests and pathogens evolve faster and faster".
CASE C
Growth and well-being in the United States
"real GDP per capita—so average income per person—since 1970 has more than doubled." Over the same period "the average level of happiness people report—measured in many different instruments—is going down. There's been a statistically significant drop in reported well-being." His verdict: "For an affluent society like the United States, that's a great example of policy resistance."
The distinction he insists on

The third case is not an argument against growth everywhere. "In the developing world, however, where people can't meet their basic needs, growth is essential." The claim applies to the audience in front of him: "for everyone in this room, who is privileged—and that's every one of us" — "we have more than enough. Pursuing more is making us worse off."

The Trap

Seeing the complexity tempts you into helplessness, and helplessness returns you to the simple answer.

"There's a deeper problem. When people begin to look at the complexity of the world, it's easy to become overwhelmed and discouraged."

"It's way beyond any one person's ability to fully understand. Nobody can comprehend even the smallest system in which we're embedded." The response he describes is a specific one, and he quotes it: "I'm embedded in all these systems I didn't create, but they shape my behavior. They determine what I end up doing and whether what I do makes a difference or not. I now feel more helpless than I did before."

He puts the question to the room — "Anybody ever have that feeling?" — and reports that about three-quarters of the hands go up, his own among them: "Yeah, I have that feeling too."

The trap closes in two steps. First the sense of being "a leaf being blown around by gales I didn't create and can't understand." Then the escape from that feeling: "All that systems stuff must be wrong. Climate change isn't that big of a problem. We'll solve it with fusion power or modular nuclear reactors".

"In other words, we fall back on a simplistic, non-systems-thinking mental model: “We have a problem. Somebody else will solve it for me.” That doesn't work."

Why this section matters to the argument

Seeing the system correctly produces the despair, and the despair produces the retreat to the simple model. Complexity does not fail by being wrong. It fails by being unbearable.

The Slinky

His Slinky drops differently each time, which locates the behaviour in the structure rather than the event.

The first of two physical demonstrations, run in three parts.

He holds it, lets go, and asks why it happened. "I let go of the Slinky, right? That's the number one answer you always get."

Then he does it again. "The behavior of the system is completely different, even though I took my hand away both times and gravity is still here. Why?" The mental model reaches for the trigger: "What people's mental models often lead them to is the event: “What was the precipitating event?” The event was that I took my hand away."

That answer implies a remedy, and the remedy is absurd. If the hand caused it, "you might think, “Make sure we never let the hand go away.” That can't possibly be right because when I take my hand away the second time, you get a totally different behavior."

"If you want to change the behavior of the system, you've got to recognize that this behavior is coming from the internal structure of the system. You're going to need to change that if you want to get a better outcome."

The third part removes the event entirely. He holds it in equilibrium and wiggles his hand by an inch — "There's always some noise and variability in the world" — and it oscillates. "The system is generating the behavior it was designed to create." His conclusion: "You want to solve that problem—stabilize your economy, or prevent similar dynamics" — "you'd better redesign the system."

The Inner Tube

His stretched inner tube shows what happens when the arms tire: the cheapest release is to lower the goal.

The second demonstration, credited to Peter Senge, with a volunteer holding a bicycle inner tube stretched between both hands.

One end "represents the world we desire" — and he is precise about what he does not mean: "I don't mean a world where our company's sales are growing, or we hit profit targets. I mean a world we truly desire: one where we solve the climate crisis, create a safer, healthier, more equitable, and resilient world—not just for us but for everybody."

"The other end is the world as it is today, nowhere close to those aspirations." Then the question to the volunteer, and to the room: "How are your arms doing? Getting tired?"

"So, what happens when our arms—our mental arms—get tired? When we face the huge gap between the world we want and the world as it is?" The answer he gives: "we're going to gradually relax the tension."

And which end gives way is not arbitrary: "The easiest, fastest way to do that, since fixing the world is hard, is to erode our goals. That feels a lot easier. But if we do that, we will never succeed."

"What I'm asking you to do is to strengthen your muscles—your emotional, moral, and intellectual muscles—so you can face what's really going on and work as hard as possible from a systems point of view."

The headwind and the rocks

On climate he adds an image for the opposition: at Heartbreak Hill in the Boston Marathon, "That headwind is the fossil fuel industry denying that climate change matters, or that it's real, or that we can do anything about it. And they've put rocks in our backpacks."

The form goal erosion takes in argument

"It's super easy to say, “Let's erode our goals. Be realistic. We're going to need fossil fuels for decades.”" And the pressure that enforces it: "If you don't understand that, you're naive, and you'll condemn billions of people in Africa, South America, and the Indochina region, Southeast Asia, to energy poverty."

What I Carry Into the Year

Refusing to erode the aspiration is the discipline I am carrying into the year.

Sterman's close, and the reason this speech was the piece chosen for the third-to-last day of 2024.

Never let anyone tell you that your aspirations are naive or idealistic. Build the capabilities you need to be an effective systems thinker.

— John Sterman, 2024 MIT Sustainability Summit
What he says the obstacle actually is
"To achieve systems change, we need to recognize this opposition while speeding up feedback loops—learning, scale, R&D, and public acceptance for wind, solar, storage, and the technologies we already have. The problem isn't the lack of technology; it's deploying them at scale and overcoming political opposition."
The last instruction
"Close the collaboration gap. This is going to be hard, but hold fast to your aspirations, and you will be able to make a difference."
Why it was published here
The introduction post gives the reason directly: as the post puts it, "His insights inspire a shift from reactive problem-solving to designing systems that enable meaningful and lasting change." A year-end piece that asks the reader not to lower the goal is a different kind of resolution from the usual sort.
