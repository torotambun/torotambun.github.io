Questioning, Listening, and Responding when facilitating Case Study

A case facilitator's instrument is the question, and the question is chosen methodologically rather than by instinct: an opening question is derived either deductively from the objective or inductively from the room, follow-ups do five distinct jobs, and reflective and action-oriented questions are timed against where engagement sits on the clock rather than where the syllabus does.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

My students were professors learning to teach the case method, and I was learning alongside them.

The article is a preparation record, written from inside the apprenticeship rather than from the far side of it.

The framing sentence is a single line and it sets the whole exercise: "As an instructor using this Mary Scott case study I have 90 minutes to teach this short case." The audience is stated in the same breath — "My students are professors who learn how to teach case study" — which puts the facilitator and the participants on the same side of the problem.

That doubling matters for how the rest reads. Everything that follows is simultaneously a plan for teaching a case and a demonstration of the craft being taught. The participants are meant to watch the questioning as much as answer it.

The apprenticeship

The article's opening photograph is captioned "With Dr. Leo Aldianto in HBS". The setting is a case-teaching programme at Harvard Business School, and the piece is the author's own working material from it.

The Case

The case hands the facilitator two problems the plan cannot anticipate.

Session duration 90 minutes. Audience: professors learning to teach case studies. The case is fictional and its protagonist teaches finance.

The setup is compact: "Professor Emma Scott is teaching a finance course to a diverse group of students with varying levels of expertise." Two things then go wrong at once. First, "a particular student has received low feedback on class participation and might push back on this assessment." Second, "another student unexpectedly raises a sensitive issue related to social inequalities, which was not part of the planned discussion."

Neither is a content problem. Both are problems of what to say next in real time — which is precisely why a teaching plan cannot solve them and a repertoire of questions can. The session objectives name the tension directly, asking participants to explore "the balance between maintaining control of the classroom and allowing flexibility for organic discussion."

The article works through five role-play scenarios built on that shape: the unexpected sensitive issue, the quieter student, the pushback on feedback, the tangential discussion, and the dominant student who leaves no room for others.

Framework

An opening question is chosen methodologically — deductively from the objective, or inductively from the room.

The article's one explicit theoretical move, stated as a pair of directions of travel: inside out and outside in.

Deductive — outside in
•"Deductive reasoning starts with a general principle or theory and then moves to specific instances or questions."
•Identify the teaching objective, break it into skills, formulate a question that leads to those skills
•Fits complex, multi-layered cases and less experienced participants, where the discussion must stay focused
•"The deductive process ensures that the question aligns closely with the intended learning outcomes"
Inductive — inside out
✓"Inductive reasoning begins with specific observations or experiences and then moves toward broader generalizations or principles."
✓Start from a common challenge, invite participants to share, then draw the connections upward
✓Fits knowledgeable and experienced participants, and simpler or open-ended cases
✓"This approach can be more exploratory and open-ended, encouraging participants to discover insights through reflection"
What decides

The article names three factors and no hierarchy among them: the audience, the nature of the case, and the learning objectives. If the goal is a specific outcome, deductive; if the goal is critical thinking and exploration, inductive.

Details

Five kinds of follow-up do five different jobs.

Follow-up questions are the part of facilitation that cannot be written in advance, so the article classifies them by purpose rather than by wording.

1  ·  Clarifying
Purpose: "To ensure that participants fully understand the point being discussed or to clear up any confusion." Its examples ask a speaker to restate — what did you mean, how does that relate to the main issue, could you give an example.
2  ·  Probing
Purpose: "To delve deeper into the reasoning behind a participant's response or to explore the implications of an idea." It goes after the why beneath a stated position, and after the downside the speaker has not costed.
3  ·  Challenging
Purpose: "To push participants to consider alternative viewpoints or to critically evaluate their own assumptions." This is the only one of the five that puts the speaker's own reasoning at risk rather than the content.
4  ·  Connecting
Purpose: "To help participants link their responses to the broader case objectives or to integrate different parts of the discussion." It does the work of holding a ninety-minute discussion together as one argument.
5  ·  Redirecting
Purpose: "To steer the discussion back on track if it begins to wander or if participants are missing the main points." It is the instrument the case's two unplanned problems actually call for.

Details

Reflective questions look back; action-oriented questions commit the participant to a change.

The distinction the article treats as the pay-off of a case session, because one of the two produces something the participant has to do on Monday.

Reflective — backward-facing
•"Reflective questions prompt participants to think critically about their experiences, decisions, and the implications of the concepts being discussed."
•"How did this discussion challenge or reinforce your previous understanding of classroom management?"
•"How might you have approached the situation differently, knowing what you know now?"
•Purpose: "To encourage deeper thinking and self-awareness"
Action-oriented — forward-facing
✓"Action-oriented questions are designed to translate insights from the case discussion into practical steps or strategies that participants can apply in their professional practice."
✓"What specific strategies will you implement in your classroom to manage discussions more effectively?"
✓"How will you prepare for handling unexpected sensitive issues in future class discussions?"
✓Purpose: "To facilitate the transfer of knowledge from the classroom to real-world situations"
The sequence

The article does not treat the seven kinds as alternatives. It sets an order: clarify or probe the initial response, then move to a reflective question, then close on an action-oriented one — so that a discussion ends with a commitment rather than with a summary.

The Craft

And sometimes the best follow-up question is silence.

Five strategies for crafting follow-ups, of which the fourth is the one that is hardest for a facilitator under time pressure.

The strategies are to listen actively, because effective "follow-up questions often arise directly from the details of a participant's response"; to stay focused on the objectives even when working inductively; to encourage depth by pushing past surface-level answers; to balance control and flexibility so the discussion can flow but not drift.

And to be patient. "Sometimes the best follow-up question is silence. Give participants time to think and respond fully before jumping in with a follow-up question." It is the only instruction in the piece that asks the facilitator to do nothing, and it sits inside a ninety-minute plan built minute by minute — which is exactly where it is hardest to obey.

The article's own summary of the whole undertaking is that choosing "the right follow-up questions is both an art and a science." The science is the taxonomy and the deductive-inductive derivation; the art is the timing.

Closing

Which question to ask is a matter of where engagement sits on the clock, not where the syllabus does.

The article ends abruptly, on a two-axis sketch and a single sentence. It is the only place the piece states a claim in the author's own compressed voice, and it reframes everything above it.

Expect variety of energy vs time graph. We need to be creative to ensure right type of questions given at th right time (e.g. reflective when the engagement is low).

The taxonomy is not a menu
Everything before this sentence reads as a catalogue of question types a facilitator may draw on. The closing line makes it a scheduling problem instead: the right question is a function of the engagement curve at that moment, not of which type has not yet been used.
The worked example runs against instinct
The one instance given is reflective questions when engagement is low. The instinct under a flagging room is to raise the energy with a challenge; the note says to turn the room inward instead. The article offers this as an example, not as a rule, and gives no others.
The graph is an image and the piece stops there
The energy-versus-time curve is a picture in the source with axes labelled Engagement and Time, and it does not survive text extraction. No data, no shape and no session is described. The article ends on the sentence above.
