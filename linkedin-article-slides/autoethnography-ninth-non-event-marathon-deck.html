An Autoethnography of My 9th Non-Event Marathon in Singapore

For thirty kilometres I argued with the people who had called me overqualified, and with God. The rain and six trail signs did not answer the argument. They replaced it with attention — and the agility I had been trying to prove turned out to look like the willingness to stop proving it.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

By kilometre 30, thinking had become confrontation.

The ninth non-event marathon. No race, no clock, no arch — and, for the first thirty kilometres, no silence either.

Situation

During the previous eight non-event marathons, distance had opened a private room inside my head. I used the hours to examine work, teaching, research, leadership, and whatever question had followed me onto the route. The first thirty kilometres of this one gave me the same space.

Complication

The heat had taken much of my physical reserve. My body was still moving, but my mind had turned against the questions that had occupied me for months. People had called me overqualified. Others had looked at my age and assumed that experience had made me less flexible. In that reasoning, age had become evidence.

Question

I had a reasonable answer to every accusation. So why did having the answers change nothing — and what was left to do with the remaining twelve kilometres?

Answer

Nothing resolved the argument. The rain arrived, and a series of signs on a nature trail asked for something the argument could not supply: attention rather than proof.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Argument

Every answer I had to "overqualified" was reasonable, and every one was exhausting.

Overqualified for what? The question was not rhetorical. I had built the answer over eight years.

Before completing my doctorate, I had been told that the work I wanted to do required a PhD. I completed it. During the preceding eight years, I also built capabilities that my industrial career had not developed. I expanded my social capital. I entered academic life. I worked with students more than thirty years younger than me. I learned how they communicated, thought, hesitated, experimented, and built. I was also developing an artificial intelligence company with some of those students.

A career accumulated over decades was being treated as a constraint rather than a resource. So as I continued, I argued with those judgments — and the arguing was not free. It ran on the same reserve as the running did.

What Part of That Was Evidence of Inflexibility?

"I had answers for every accusation. The answers were reasonable. They were also exhausting." Having the argument won did not shorten the distance, and did not quiet the question.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Protest

I argued with God, and I was speaking without listening.

Between kilometres 30 and 33, the argument moved beyond recruiters and institutions.

Isaiah 40:31 came into my mind. I knew the verse. I had probably read it many times. On that afternoon, however, it did not arrive as comfort. It became a question: where was that renewed strength?

I had waited. I had worked. I had prepared myself for the next chapter of my life. Yet the doors that should have recognised the preparation often seemed to close around the same words: age, seniority, qualification, fit. I cried out. I challenged God. I spoke loudly enough that the prayer was closer to protest.

Isaiah 40:31

"But those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength. They will soar on wings like eagles; they will run and not grow weary, they will walk and not be faint."

The Posture

"I was speaking, but I was not listening." From kilometre 33 toward kilometre 36 the marathon became harder to continue. The questions did not disappear. They remained inside the heat, attached to every step.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Rupture

The body does not negotiate with a curriculum vitae.

The physical honesty of hour five stripped away the authority of my explanations.

"It does not care about executive experience, academic credentials, alumni status, publications, or plans for another company. It reports only what remains available: breath, balance, strength, pain, temperature, and the willingness to continue."

My confidence in the arguments did not make the distance shorter. Whatever was going to carry me the remaining kilometres, it was not going to be the case for my own qualifications.

The Rain

The rain changed the temperature, and then it changed what held my attention.

It did not begin as a gentle change in weather. It poured.

The ground darkened. Water struck the leaves and the path. The air cooled. My clothes became wet. The heat that had pressed against me for hours began to release its grip. I had prepared for rain without knowing when it would come: my phone was protected inside a Pelican cover, and the backpack I had selected was fully water-resistant, designed for fishermen. I did not need to search for shelter or protect my belongings. I could remain on the route.

"The rain changed more than the temperature. It changed what occupied my attention." Around kilometre 36, I entered the Nature Immersion Trail at Saraca Stream in the Singapore Botanic Gardens, and continued the marathon at a much slower pace, almost halted.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Signs

Six signs on the Saraca Stream trail asked me to notice, not to prove.

Earlier, I had treated my senses as instruments of endurance. My eyes monitored the route. My feet assessed the surface. My body calculated whether it could maintain the pace. The signs asked for something else.

1  ·  Slow down and engage your senses
It asked me to notice without converting every sensation into a problem. The rain had made that possible. I began to hear it falling through different layers of the garden — heavy on broad leaves, finer on smaller ones, uneven where drops reached the ground. The garden was not silent. I had simply been too occupied to hear it.
2  ·  Inhale the goodness
I breathed more deliberately. The wet air carried the smell of soil, bark, leaves, and rainwater. "The air did not ask whether I was qualified. The trees did not assess my age. The path did not demand evidence of agility." It received the next step.
3  ·  Notice the hidden hues
Under the rain, green was no longer one colour. Some leaves became dark and polished. Others remained pale. New growth appeared almost translucent. I had been trying to defend the range of my professional life; the garden quietly presented another range that I had failed to see.
4  ·  Notice the play of light
The sky remained grey, yet light moved through the wet canopy. It gathered on leaves, disappeared under branches, returned on the path, and broke across the water. The scene did not become bright. It became visible in another way.
5  ·  What tiny life caught your eye?
That question changed the scale of my attention. For more than thirty-six kilometres, distance had been measured in large numbers — the watch counted kilometres, my mind counted years, qualifications, achievements, and unanswered applications. A small movement among the leaves became sufficient. "The question did not ask for significance. It asked for notice."
6  ·  Endings and beginnings
By then, I had stopped constructing rebuttals. The rain continued. The path continued. My body continued. Between kilometres 36 and 39, the marathon ceased to be a place for analysis. It became an exercise in attention.

What It Revealed

The signs did not answer my questions; they replaced them with better ones.

Nothing explained why organisations ask for experience and then become suspicious of it. Nothing resolved the contradiction between demanding qualifications and later treating those qualifications as excessive.

What I Carry

The strongest evidence of agility may have been the willingness to stop defending myself.

At kilometre 39 the route became familiar again, from Nassim Hill toward Dhoby Ghaut. The watch reached 42.195 kilometres at Istana Park, where I had planned to finish.

I had spent the earlier kilometres trying to prove that I was still agile. Yet the strongest evidence of agility may have been the capacity to stop defending myself and attend to what the environment was asking.

— The article, written afterwards
Nothing marked the finish
"There was no organised race. No finishing arch waited for me. No official clock recorded the time. No announcer called my name." I asked a bystander to take a photograph. In the image, the ninth non-event marathon had ended.
Gratitude did not close the hollow
The body had carried me through the heat, continued while the mind argued, and recovered enough to reach the place I had chosen. "Yet the completion did not remove the hollow I had carried into the marathon."
The promise stayed unfinished
Isaiah 40:31 remained with me, not as a verse I had proved through endurance, but as a promise whose fulfilment I was still waiting to understand. "Perhaps renewed strength does not always arrive as power." On that day it arrived first as rain, then as attention, and finally as enough strength to continue toward the Istana.
The Closing Lines

"The marathon was complete. The waiting was not."
