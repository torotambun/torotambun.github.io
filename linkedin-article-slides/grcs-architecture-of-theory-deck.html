GRCS and the Architecture of Theory

There is no single grand theory of GRCS. It is an applied field built from theories at three levels, and a doctoral study places its research problem at the correct level, keeps frameworks and tools below theory, and chooses a theory from the phenomenon and not from a favourite.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

There is no single grand theory of GRCS, so the task is to place a research problem at the correct theoretical level.

"What theoretical architecture sits underneath GRCS?"

Situation

GRCS is often taught through frameworks: agency structures and boards, ISO 31000, COSO ERM, risk registers and heat maps, regulation and controls, ESG standards and environmental indicators.

Complication

"Those are necessary." "But they are not enough for a PhD class." A doctoral student must tell apart a theory that explains a phenomenon, a framework that organizes practice, a model that represents a mechanism, and a tool that performs analysis.

Question

Which theories sit underneath GRCS, and at what level does each one work?

Answer

"There is no single grand theory of GRCS." It is an applied field built from economics, psychology, sociology, organization studies, systems science, safety science, regulation and finance, and "The challenge is to locate a research problem at the correct theoretical level."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

Theories sit at three levels, and the hierarchy widens as it moves downward from few grand theories to many low-range mechanisms.

"The purpose of the hierarchy is analytical." It asks how broad the phenomenon being explained is.

01
Few
Grand theory
Broad assumptions about organizations, uncertainty, rationality, systems and human behaviour. "A theory that applies only to insurance underwriting or road safety would normally not qualify as a grand theory."
02
More
Middle-range theory
Explains recurring organizational mechanisms across settings, such as why organizations ignore warnings, why people remain silent, and why abnormal behaviour gradually becomes accepted as normal.
03
Many
Low-range theory or model
Explains a specific phenomenon, actor, risk, mechanism or context, such as why insurance coverage changes the behaviour of an insured party or why dependence on one donor affects an NGO's decisions.
Levels are not fixed

"This classification is not absolute." Resource Dependence Theory can be middle-range, and propositions derived from it about dependence on a dominant donor in NGOs may operate at the low-range level.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Four grand theories supply the foundation for risk: risk versus uncertainty, a rational benchmark, bounded rationality and systems.

"Grand theories should be few."

01
Knight, 1921
Risk and uncertainty
"Risk exists when possible outcomes and their probabilities can, at least in principle, be estimated." "Uncertainty exists when those probabilities cannot reliably be known." Risk can often be priced, modeled or transferred, while uncertainty may need judgment, experimentation and adaptation.
02
von Neumann and Morgenstern, 1944
Expected utility
A normative account of how a rational decision maker should choose when outcomes are uncertain. "Expected Utility Theory establishes a rational benchmark." Later theories show how real people depart from it.
03
Simon, 1947 and 1955
Bounded rationality
Managers lack unlimited information, cognitive capacity and time, so organizations frequently satisfice. The question moves to "What can a real organization reasonably know and process?"
04
von Bertalanffy, 1968
General Systems Theory
Attention shifts from components to relationships among them. "This becomes important because risk is often systemic rather than isolated."
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Nine middle-range theories each explain a recurring organizational mechanism in risk.

Their purpose "is to explain identifiable recurring mechanisms."

1  ·  Agency Theory (Jensen and Meckling, 1976)
Delegation with differing interests and uneven information. It asks "Who makes the decision, who receives the benefit, and who carries the risk?"
2  ·  Resource Dependence Theory (Pfeffer and Salancik, 1978)
Dependence on external resources creates power. "The risk lies in dependence itself."
3  ·  Prospect Theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979)
People judge outcomes against reference points, and losses weigh more than equivalent gains. "Risk appetite is therefore partly psychological."
4  ·  Threat-Rigidity Theory (Staw, Sandelands and Dutton, 1981)
Threat can restrict information processing and centralize authority. "Threat can therefore reduce the capacity to respond intelligently to risk."
5  ·  Cultural Theory of Risk (Douglas and Wildavsky, 1982)
Risk perception is partly shaped by culture and social organization. "Risk is also socially selected."
6  ·  Normal Accident Theory (Perrow, 1984)
In complex, tightly coupled systems the structure itself can make some failures hard to anticipate. "Adding another rule does not necessarily make such systems safe."
7  ·  Managerial risk taking (March and Shapira, 1987)
Managers think about downside, survival, targets, aspirations and controllability, not variance around expected returns, so a risk committee and an operating manager may hold different conceptions of risk.
8  ·  High Reliability Organization research (Roberts and colleagues, from about 1990)
Asks how some organizations in hazardous settings avoid catastrophic failure repeatedly, where Perrow asks why complex systems fail.
9  ·  Organizational sensemaking (Weick, 1995)
"Sensemaking therefore precedes many formal risk-management processes." A risk register records a risk after someone has decided that a signal means something.

Details

Low-range theories multiply near specific phenomena, and the specific theory changes because the mechanism changes.

"Low-range does not mean inferior." It means narrower in explanatory scope, and the level mixes genuine theories, models and specific causal mechanisms.

01
Insurance
Protection and information
Moral hazard (Arrow, 1963; Pauly, 1968), credibility theory (Bühlmann, 1967), adverse selection (Akerlof, 1970) and signaling theory (Spence, 1973).
02
Transport and safety
Behaviour under safety pressure
Protection motivation (Rogers, 1975), risk compensation (Peltzman, 1975), risk homeostasis (Wilde, 1982), the Swiss Cheese Model (Reason, 1990), planned behavior (Ajzen, 1991) and migration toward boundaries (Rasmussen, 1997).
03
Compliance and misconduct
Why people comply
Economic deterrence (Becker, 1968), procedural justice (Tyler, 1990), responsive regulation (Ayres and Braithwaite, 1992) and normalization of deviance (Vaughan, 1996). "The absence of catastrophe becomes mistaken for evidence of safety."
04
Education and NGOs
Stress, voice and demands
Conservation of Resources (Hobfoll, 1989), Psychological Safety (Edmondson, 1999) and the Job Demands-Resources Model (Demerouti and colleagues, 2001).

Limits

Frameworks and tools organize practice, but they do not explain why organizations behave as they do.

"Only after the theoretical architecture is clear should frameworks enter the discussion." The distinction is fundamental in doctoral work.

What frameworks and tools do
•COSO Enterprise Risk Management (2004), ISO 31000 (2009) and the IIA Three Lines Model (2020) "organize practice"
•They help determine responsibilities, processes and reporting structures
•Risk registers, risk matrices, FMEA, bow-tie analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, stress testing, key risk indicators and scenario analysis are operational methods below frameworks
•"A risk matrix helps classify risks."
What still needs a theory
✗ISO 31000 may require communication and consultation but does not explain why employees remain silent, which Psychological Safety may help explain
✗COSO may require risk identification but does not explain why management notices one signal and misses another, which Sensemaking may help explain
✗A compliance framework may specify controls but does not explain why repeated violations become accepted, which Normalization of Deviance may help explain
✗"But none becomes a theory simply because it appears frequently in corporate risk management."
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Implications

One transport case with repeated warning signals supports five theoretical lenses, and the explanatory question chooses among them.

"Theory selection should not begin with a favourite theory. It should begin with the phenomenon."

1  ·  Failure to interpret warnings
Sensemaking may be appropriate.
2  ·  Complex system interaction
Normal Accident Theory may be more relevant.
3  ·  Danger recognized but silence kept
Psychological Safety may give a better explanation.
4  ·  Production pressure pushing operations toward unsafe limits
Rasmussen may be more appropriate.
5  ·  Repeated violations accepted as practice
Normalization of Deviance may explain the process.
Note in the article

The proposed architecture carries the author's note that "it will be different if we approach G first then C then R then S". The deck does not develop the alternative orderings.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Theory selection should begin with the phenomenon, and frameworks and tools sit below theory.

"The same empirical case can therefore support different theoretical lenses."

A risk matrix helps classify risks. It does not explain organizational behaviour.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, GRCS and the Architecture of Theory
Grand theories
Knight (1921), von Neumann and Morgenstern (1944), Simon (1947, 1955) and von Bertalanffy (1968).
Middle-range theories
Nine, from Jensen and Meckling (1976) to Weick (1995). Below them sit many low-range theories, models and mechanisms, "and many others."
Below theory
Frameworks (COSO ERM, ISO 31000, the Three Lines Model), then methods and tools (risk register, risk matrix, FMEA, bow-tie analysis, Monte Carlo simulation, stress testing, key risk indicators, scenario analysis).
