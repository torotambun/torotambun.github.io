The Value of Academic Writing: Insights from Larry McEnerney

Academic writing fails when it is built to display knowledge to an evaluator rather than to create value for a reader. Value is not a property of the text; it is conferred by a specific community that has its own problems, its own lexicon and its own sense of what counts as an instability worth addressing. The consequence is a change of target: away from filling knowledge gaps, which are infinite and which nobody is waiting for, and towards resolving a problem the readers already feel.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

Academic writing is not a call to action; it is an attempt to change what a reader understands.

The article opens by fixing the purpose, because everything else follows from getting that right.

Situation

"Academic writing serves a fundamental purpose: to deepen the reader's understanding of topics within their scope of interest." It "is not a call to action, but an endeavor to broaden and enrich knowledge."

Complication

"The value of academic work is contingent upon its relevance to the reader, underscoring the importance of addressing issues that the community finds significant." Value therefore sits outside the writer: "A better understanding, therefore, is not a universal measure but one that varies with each academic discipline and community."

Question

If value is conferred by the reader's community rather than earned by the writer's effort, what does a writer have to do differently?

Answer

"Effective communication hinges on the writer's insight into specific academic communities and their unique interests." And the text must do something to the reader: "Academic texts shine when they reveal inadequacies in the reader's knowledge, positioning themselves as a constructively critical lens through which to view a topic."

Gap

Filling a knowledge gap is infinite, and nobody is waiting for it.

The article names the substitution most academic writers make, and why it fails.

The reframe is the pivot of the whole piece: "The writer must identify and articulate a problem that resonates with the reader's interests, defining for whom it is a problem and outlining its implications."

And the target changes accordingly — "This focus shifts the narrative from filling knowledge gaps—common and infinite in academia—to resolving pertinent problems that impact the reader." Gaps are unlimited in supply; problems are not.

Not every instability qualifies. "It's not just any instability that warrants attention but one that poses a significant issue to the readers. Academic texts must, therefore, constructively respond to these problems, presenting solutions that enrich the community's discourse."

The failure is diagnosed as a habit carried over from being a student: the shift required is "moving beyond self-centric writing—where the primary objective has been to demonstrate personal knowledge to an evaluator, typically a teacher—to embrace a more functional perspective that considers the reader's needs."

Why novelty is not the test

"The writer's challenge is to address topics that not only capture personal interest but also align with the community's intellectual pursuits, avoiding the assumption that a topic's novelty equates to its relevance." New and valuable are independent properties.

Framework

Value is made inside a community, and that community has a lexicon.

What the writer must actually do, in the article's own order.

1  ·  Understand the audience before the topic
"Understanding your audience is half the battle in academic research. Writers must engage readers on topics that resonate with established community dialogues and values, offering insight into problems the community cares about."
2  ·  Use the community's own words
"The judicious use of language is critical in this endeavor, signaling alignment with the community's values and ongoing conversations. By choosing words that reflect the community's lexicon, writers can indicate the value of their work and its contribution to broader discussions." And the codes are the proof: "mastery of this vocabulary showcases relevance and value, inviting engagement and facilitating understanding."
3  ·  Argue rather than explain
"To further the community's knowledge, one must assert the audience's intelligence while demonstrating how their work can add to or amend existing understandings. This process transcends mere explanation and enters the realm of argumentation, requiring writers to predict and address potential doubts".
4  ·  Establish relevance immediately
"In crafting academic content, immediate relevance is paramount. Writers must articulate why readers should care from the onset, creating value through language and aligning with the goals and interests of the field."
5  ·  Keep your own position while meeting the norms
"Writers must navigate ethical considerations, balancing the need to contribute to community norms with maintaining their unique perspective."

Details

Interdisciplinary work is harder because the problem must be a problem in more than one place.

The article isolates the case where the community itself is not singular.

"In interdisciplinary work, understanding the reader community becomes especially complex. Writers must address a convergence of perspectives, aligning their arguments with varied definitions of problems across disciplines."

And the stakes of getting that alignment right are stated plainly: "This recognition is crucial, as academic success often depends on the convergence of the writer's subject matter with the audience's intellectual pursuits."

The demand is cumulative rather than additive. A problem must be recognised as a problem by each community addressed, in each community's own terms — which is why the lexicon requirement bites hardest here.

Behind all of it sits a rejection of how writing is usually taught: "academic writing should break away from conventional educational models that emphasize static knowledge bases. Instead, it should engage readers in the dynamic pursuit of understanding, making its utility apparent and its relevance indisputable."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

The shift is from showing what you know to changing what the reader thinks.

The closing section states the transformation the article has been describing throughout.

The texts suggest a transformative shift in academic writing from a self-serving exhibition of what one knows to creating a dialogue that adds substantial value for the reader.

— The Value of Academic Writing: Insights from Larry McEnerney, 10 March 2024
What the writing is for
"This paradigmatic shift is not confined to sharing ideas or tools; it transcends to offering valuable insight and understanding that resonates with the reader."
What an argument owes the reader
"The argumentative thrust of the paper is not self-justifying; instead, it aims to probe deeper, answering pertinent questions and resolving queries that are meaningful to the reader."
What first-rate writing is
"This shift from formality to functionality defines first-rate academic writing, marking the difference between information that is merely presented and knowledge that is truly engaged with."
