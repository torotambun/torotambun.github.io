Introduction to Ethnography

Class notes that end in a commitment. The piece works outward from the author's own participant-observation mind map, whose tree metaphor makes data the trunk and ethnography the fruit, and whose third branch insists that the ontological stance be settled before fieldwork begins. Having laid that out, the article does not stop at survey: it names autoethnography as the author's most likely method of choice, designs a triangulation around it, and closes on the ethical problem that method creates, which is that reading another person's journal turns consent from a formality into the load-bearing part of the design.

Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

Ethnography is not one method but six, and the choice of lens decides what can be seen.

The class notes open by refusing a single definition. Each type is presented as a different lens on culture and community, and the article is explicit that journaling as a method points towards two of them in particular.

1  ·  Traditional ethnography
The researcher immerses in the community or culture to observe and interact with participants over an extended period.
2  ·  Autoethnography
The researcher is also the subject, using self-reflection to explore personal experience and connect the autobiographical story to wider cultural, political and social meanings.
3  ·  Critical ethnography
Aims to critique and challenge power structures within the society being studied, often with the goal of advocating social change.
4  ·  Virtual ethnography, or netnography
Focuses on online communities and cultures, using the internet as the source of data.
5  ·  Phenomenological ethnography
Emphasises the lived experience of individuals within a culture, seeking to describe what it is like to be a member of it.
6  ·  Ethnography of communication
Focuses on language use and communication practices within a community.
What all six are for

"Ethnographies can vary widely in their approach and style, but they generally aim to provide a comprehensive, nuanced, and empathetic account of the study subjects that is accessible both to academic audiences and to the public."

Framework

If anthropology were a tree, data is the trunk and ethnography is the fruit.

The article's organising image comes from the author's own participant-observation mind map, whose four branches structure everything that follows.

01
First branch
Factual data
Empirical data collected through participation and observation, treated as the starting point for making sense of a community and for all analytical work that follows.
02
Second branch
The trunk
"If anthropology were a tree, the data is the trunk" — just as a trunk is central to a tree's structure and support, data is central to the discipline, and every other element is built on it.
03
Third branch
The ontological method
The nature of being and reality must be considered before the data can be interpreted, so the researcher's method has to align with the ontological position actually held.
04
Fourth branch
The fruit
"Ethnography is the fruits" — the descriptive study of a specific human culture that grows from the data, and the tangible outcome of the whole research process.
What the metaphor commits the researcher to

An order of operations. Data collection through active participation is the groundwork; ontology and method govern its interpretation; and the written ethnography is what the process yields rather than what it begins with.

Details

Being there is not watching, and the data has to be lived before it can be read.

The first branch is developed into a claim about what counts as evidence. Participation goes past observation: the researcher joins daily activities, rituals and social functions, so the data is not only seen but experienced, which supplies context for behaviour that might otherwise be misread or missed entirely.

The article is careful about the word objective. It means data verifiable and observable by others rather than data free of the researcher, and it concedes directly that the material is filtered through the anthropologist's own presence and participation. The requirement is to collect information as untainted by bias as possible, not to pretend the filter is absent.

The phrase the notes return to is being there. It marks knowledge gained through physical presence in the field, and the article reads the past tense in I was there as the move from collection into analysis, indicating a depth of understanding available only through direct engagement.

Immersion is then set against its alternative, and the alternative is named as a failure mode rather than a lesser option. What immersion produces is a grasp of the complexities, emotions and subtleties of social life that distance does not reach.

The distinction, stated

"Watching from a distance can create a detachment from the subject of study, which may result in a superficial understanding."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Map

A theory is the map, and entering the field without one means getting lost in the data.

The third branch turns philosophical, and the article gives it an image rather than an argument. Theory guides the research process the way a map guides a traveller through unfamiliar territory, supplying structure and direction before the territory is entered.

The consequence of going without one is specific. The researcher becomes lost in the vast amount of data, unable to find a path to meaningful insight, which makes the absence of theory a practical problem rather than a philosophical looseness.

The sequencing is what matters. The article's position is that the ontological stance and its corresponding methods have to be selected before entering the field, because they are what will do the sense-making once the data arrives.

It also names what the discipline is protecting against. Theories developed this way are grounded in the lived realities of the people studied rather than imposed constructs, which is the same requirement the first branch made of the data.

What theory is for, in the article's words

"Just as a map helps a traveler navigate unfamiliar territory, theoretical frameworks provide structure and direction for anthropologists…" as they enter what the notes call the complex jungles of human societies and cultures.

Ontology

The ontology comes first: interpretivism, non-positivism and mostly grounded theory.

Three sub-branches name the position the author actually takes, and they are consistent with one another rather than offered as options.

01
Sub-branch one
Interpretivism
Reality is multiple and subjective, constructed by individuals from their experience and interaction. The researcher seeks understanding from inside the culture, looking at how people interpret their own lives, which the notes gloss as explaining about ourselves.
02
Sub-branch two
Non-positivism
Set against the assumption that reality is objective and describable without the observer's interference. It accepts that aspects of human experience cannot be captured by quantitative measure or objective observation alone, and that values, beliefs and ascribed meaning are integral to understanding behaviour.
03
Sub-branch three
Mostly grounded theory
Theory developed through systematic gathering and analysis of data, emerging from the data itself rather than imposed beforehand. In anthropology this means letting the data speak and forming theory from what fieldwork produces.
Why the three belong together

Each rules out the same thing. If reality is constructed, if quantitative measure cannot reach the meanings involved, and if theory must emerge from data, then a method importing a finished framework into the field is inconsistent with all three at once.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Choice

Autoethnography is my most likely method of choice.

This is where the notes stop being notes. Having surveyed six types and settled an ontology, the article names the method the author expects to use, under its own heading, and the rest of the piece is a design rather than a summary.

The definition given is precise about what the method combines. Autoethnography brings together autobiography and ethnography, with researchers using personal experience to understand broader cultural, social or political phenomena, and the notes place it in anthropology, sociology and psychology among other fields.

The seven steps set out are a working procedure rather than an outline: identifying a focus and formulating questions that connect a personal story to larger concepts; gathering personal narratives from journals, memories, letters and photographs; engaging with the literature to situate the experience; analysing the narratives for themes, patterns and contradictions; writing in a narrative style that carries analytical insight; handling the ethics; and reviewing and revising.

One step in that list is doing more work than the others. The analysis stage requires acknowledging subjectivity, which means being aware of one's own biases and perspectives and considering how they shape the interpretation, and that requirement is what connects the method back to the interpretivist position above.

The definition, as given

"Auto ethnography is a qualitative research method that combines elements of autobiography and ethnography."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Method

The researcher is then part of the evidence, so subjectivity is declared rather than removed.

The article designs a triangulation around the method, combining three sources so that the researcher's own account is checked rather than trusted.

1  ·  Personal reflection and journaling
The researcher's own autoethnographic reflection, diary entries and autobiographical narrative, examined critically against the research context. The article's stated purpose for this is to surface the researcher's own biases and to understand how their presence and identity influence the setting, and the setting them.
2  ·  Analysing observants' journals
Reading and analysing the journals or diaries of subjects, which gives access to their personal experience, thoughts and perspectives, and supplies depth and context to lived experience inside the group being studied.
3  ·  Interviews and conversations
Traditional ethnographic technique, conducted with group leaders, professors and parents, offering external perspectives on how social, cultural and educational factors bear on the individuals whose journals are being read.
4  ·  Reading the three against each other
Comparing the researcher's reflections with the themes in the observants' journals to surface similarities and differences within one setting; using interview material to contextualise both; and looking for themes and patterns that emerge across all three.
The line the method has to stay on

The article warns that in autoethnography "there is a fine line between self-indulgence" and critical self-reflection, and sets the test as whether the personal narrative connects to broader cultural or social insight.

Conclusion

Reading someone's journal requires consent, and consent is where the method's ethics actually bite.

The article closes on the problem its own design creates, and does not resolve it.

It's important to consider the ethical implications of reading personal journals, even with consent.

— Introduction to Ethnography, 7 December 2023
What consent covers
Informed consent is required from individuals whose journals are read and from those interviewed, and the article notes that where a story involves other people, their agreement is needed for details that might identify them.
Why consent is not sufficient
The article's phrasing makes this explicit: the implications persist even with consent. Confidentiality and the respectful portrayal of subjects' private thoughts are named as paramount, which is a standard that a signature does not discharge.
The interpretive problem underneath
A decision is still required about how to read such texts: whether to take them at face value, analyse them for deeper meanings, or treat them as part of a broader narrative. The article poses this and leaves it open.
What the triangulation is finally for
Situating personal narratives within the broader social and cultural context supplied by leaders, educators and family, so that a journal entry is read in company rather than alone.
