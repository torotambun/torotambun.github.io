(3 of 7) What Biases Involved When Founders Commit Fraud?

Founders rarely inflate revenue through a single rational calculation; eleven overlapping biases do the rationalizing for them, and the biases founders can consciously counteract were themselves shaped, years earlier, by how thoroughly a stress-honesty tradeoff got wired into the brain during childhood pruning and myelination.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Founders rarely inflate revenue through one clean, rational choice.

This piece opens a three-block argument: the biases behind fraud, the countermeasures founders can build, and the childhood roots of whichever capacity wins out under pressure.

Situation

When founders inflate revenues in financial statements, the decision is treated in most commentary as either deliberate deception or a moment of desperation.

Complication

Neither framing accounts for what is actually happening moment to moment: a cluster of ordinary cognitive and behavioral biases distorting judgment and supplying the founder with justification before the act, not only after it.

Question

Which biases are doing that work, can a founder learn to counteract them, and why do some founders resist the pressure while others do not?

Answer

Eleven biases recur across fraud cases, nine practical habits can blunt them, and the difference in who holds the line traces back to early neural wiring around ethics and delayed gratification.

Gap

Eleven biases recur, and each one supplies its own alibi.

Section A of the argument: the cognitive and behavioral biases that distort a founder's judgment once fraud becomes a live option.

1  ·  Overconfidence
Founders overestimate their ability to steer the company back to success, treating fraud as a temporary fix they expect to outrun.
2  ·  Optimism bias
Belief in the vision is strong enough that the odds of being caught feel minimal, so the deception looks justified in advance.
3  ·  Sunk cost fallacy
Having already invested years and resources, founders rationalize fraud as protecting that prior investment rather than starting a new harm.
4  ·  Confirmation bias
Founders seek out examples confirming the belief that "other startups did this and got away with it", reinforcing the decision and screening out contrary evidence.
5  ·  Anchoring bias
Fixation on an arbitrary revenue milestone or valuation target makes manipulating the numbers feel like closing a gap rather than committing fraud.
6  ·  Loss aversion
The fear of losing funding or reputation outweighs the fear of being caught, so inflating revenue reads as avoiding a loss, not causing one.
7  ·  Social proof
A competitive ecosystem where "everyone inflates their metrics to survive" normalizes the behavior and lowers hesitation.
8  ·  Self-serving bias
Founders frame the deception as serving the greater good — saving jobs, protecting the company — which downplays the harm caused.
9  ·  Temporal discounting
Immediate pressures, such as impressing investors this quarter, outweigh long-term consequences that feel abstract by comparison.
10  ·  Moral licensing
Founders who see themselves as hardworking or visionary feel entitled to bend the rules as a reward for the sacrifices already made.
11  ·  Anchoring on peers
A second pass at confirmation bias: citing other startups' unpunished misconduct as evidence that the practice is survivable.

Framework

Nine habits counteract the biases, but only if practiced before the pressure arrives.

Section B: behavioral-science interventions a founder can build into ordinary operating habits.

01
Awareness
Frequent reflection and bias literacy
Founders who can name overconfidence, loss aversion and anchoring in themselves are better placed to notice the moment those biases are steering a decision.
02
Structure
Transparency, pause-and-reflect, ethical frameworks
Slowing the decision down, committing to open books, and running choices through a written framework counteracts the impulsiveness that temporal discounting produces.
03
Accountability
External audits, board oversight, codes of conduct
Independent checks remove the founder's sole discretion over the numbers, which is where most of the biases above do their damage.
04
Culture
Reframing failure, simulated dilemmas, incentivizing honesty
Treating setbacks as part of the process rather than existential threats, and rehearsing ethical dilemmas before they are live, builds the habit before the stakes are real.

Details

Section C reframes the question: what built the capacity to resist in the first place?

Biases (A) drive the decision; countermeasures (B) can blunt it; but B's availability under real pressure is shaped by habits and emotional patterns laid down in childhood (C).

Parental and societal modeling teaches a child, long before any startup exists, whether ethical dilemmas get navigated honestly or avoided. Early education in critical thinking — debating fairness, reasoning through problems — strengthens the same muscle.

How a child is taught to relate to failure matters as much as any of that: a value-based growth mindset toward setbacks, rather than treating failure as catastrophic, carries forward into how an adult founder handles a bad quarter.

Resilience and self-regulation — the capacity to delay gratification, to manage frustration — are what temporal discounting exploits in adulthood when they were never built in childhood.

Evidence

The argument is neurobiological, not metaphorical: pruning and myelination make ethics automatic.

During childhood and adolescence, synaptic pruning strengthens or eliminates neural connections based on repeated experience; myelination then makes the surviving pathways fast and close to automatic under stress.

ARGUED A
Repeated ethical exposure builds default pathways
Experiences that emphasize honesty and resilience strengthen those neural connections into the brain's dominant default mode, rather than a conscious override it has to reach for under pressure.
ARGUED B
Myelination is what makes it hold under stress
If integrity and accountability are reinforced repeatedly during critical developmental stages, the resulting pathways process fast enough that the behavior looks instinctive rather than effortful.
ARGUED C
Weak early wiring leaves the biases room to win
Individuals who lack that reinforcement have poorer-myelinated pathways for ethical decision-making, and under pressure are more likely to succumb to the eleven biases above rather than override them.
ARGUED D
Strong wiring is not immunity, only resistance
Extreme emotional distress, systemic corruption, or a total absence of accountability can still push even a well-myelinated individual to rationalize fraud — these are exceptions, not evidence against the pattern.
What this does not claim

Early ethical development is presented here as a resilient moral compass under situational pressure, not as a guarantee. The argument explicitly allows for well-formed individuals who still fail under extreme enough conditions.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Implications

Individuals with well-pruned ethical pathways are far less likely to commit fraud, even under pressure.

The piece closes by drawing the societal implication out of the neurological claim: this is a case for investing in early ethical development, not just in founder-stage compliance training.

This underscores the need for a societal focus on early ethical development as a foundation for long-term behavioral integrity.

— (3 of 7) What Biases Involved When Founders Commit Fraud?
Investing in early ethical education
Schools, families and communities are asked to emphasize honesty, accountability and resilience specifically during the developmental windows when pruning and myelination are active.
Resilience-building programs
Teaching children to manage failure and regulate emotion is framed as hardwiring the capacities that later resist temporal discounting and loss aversion.
The role of parents and role models
Children mirror the ethical behavior they repeatedly see; parental modeling is where the neural pathways this piece describes actually get built.
