From Boston to Madura: An Autoethnographic Journey of Innovation and Leadership

A model built out of MIT training and presented to a Jakarta ministry only became real when two academics in Madura rebuilt it for their own students, spreading across eight semesters what had been compressed into two. What made them able to do it was standing between two systems rather than importing one, and the contradiction they leave the author with — quick MSME activity against long industrial building — is not resolved.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

I unveiled an MIT-trained model in a Jakarta ministry room, and “Lu Lagi, Lu Lagi” was the critique it answered.

November 2019, the Kementerian Koordinator Ekonomi conference, and a model presented as a bridge rather than an import.

"The model I was poised to unveil, the Innovation Driven Enterprise University Based model, was a synthesis of my rigorous academic training at MIT and the vibrant entrepreneurial spirit of Indonesia. This model was more than an academic exercise; it represented a bridge I had built between the systematic methodologies of Western academia and the fluid, dynamic business landscape of my homeland."

What it was answering is named in four words of Indonesian. The phrase "“Lu Lagi, Lu Lagi” (you again, you again), which critiqued the stagnation within our entrepreneurial ecosystem, struck a chord with me, echoing my own frustrations with the repetitive impediments to innovation in Indonesia." The complaint is that the same small circle keeps appearing — and the model is an attempt to widen the circle.

The role the day fixed is the one the rest of the piece tests: "a defining moment of introspection and commitment. It marked a realization of my dual role as a bridge and a mediator between the structured rigor of Western academic frameworks and the dynamic entrepreneurial spirit of Indonesia."

The Rupture

The pandemic replaced the room with pixelated faces, and the model had to be rebuilt.

The disruption is described as a problem of teaching rather than of logistics, and the correction it forced is concrete.

"The usual vibrancy of face-to-face interactions, so fundamental to my way of engaging with learners and peers, was abruptly replaced by the sterile interface of digital workshops and forums." What he records struggling with is not the technology: "Each online session became a mirror reflecting my struggles and adaptations, as I sought to inject warmth and genuine connection into the pixelated faces staring back at me."

The article credits the constraint with producing something. "The digital barrier forced a confrontation with my own biases towards traditional educational environments, pushing me to innovate within these constraints while maintaining the depth of my educational goals."

On 8 September 2020 the first series of online forums launched, and out of them came a named design: the "“2 credits course, 26 credits feel” model" — "a precursor to the later developed Mickey Mouse shape entrepreneurial teaching model and the Venture Tactics model".

The Turn

They spread across eight semesters what we had compressed into two.

The pivotal move in the article is made by someone else, and the author records it as a correction to his own design rather than as an application of it.

"Simultaneously, Dr. Mardiyah and Dr. Taufik R D A Nugroho expanded their curriculum to spread entrepreneurship modules across an eight-semester timeline, rather than compressing them into just two."

The reason given is not scheduling. The broader span "allowed for a more thorough and reflective exploration of entrepreneurial concepts, providing students the space to grow and adapt their skills progressively." And the article insists on the distinction: "This approach was not just about logistical restructuring but was deeply rooted in a philosophical shift towards fostering long-term competence over transient knowledge spikes."

Set against the "2 credits course, 26 credits feel" design, the contrast is exact. One intensifies a short container; the other refuses the container. The article does not adjudicate between them, and the deck does not either.

The Scene

A Soft Skill Lab behind glass and a Salt Lab smaller than its mission.

First visit to Universitas Trunojoyo Madura, 20 March 2022. Two rooms carry the whole observation.

OBSERVED A
Arriving on the island
"Stepping onto Madura Island, a place heralded for its vibrant salt farms, I was immediately struck by the crisp, saline air—a sensory reminder of the island's robust natural and economic activities." At the gates, "a scene that perfectly juxtaposed modern educational ambitions against a backdrop of entrenched community values."
OBSERVED B
The Soft Skill Labs
"Peering through the transparent facades, I was captivated by the sight of students deeply engrossed in vibrant discussions." The glass is the point: soft skills are "often touted in academic discourse," but here the teaching of them is made visible. The labs are read as "cultural interfaces where traditional educational structures meet innovative pedagogical practices."
OBSERVED C
The Salt Lab
"a modest yet profoundly significant facility that encapsulated the essence of Madura’s natural bounty—salt. This lab, although small in size, struck me with the weight of its mission, which far exceeded its physical confines." It prompts the article's sharpest question about research itself: "How do we, as part of the global academic community, engage with local communities to ensure that our research agendas do not merely extract value but also contribute sustainably?"

Field Notes

Over five and a half days the mentors positioned themselves as learners.

The bootcamp, with mentors drawn from ITB Electrical Engineering and UGM's Nuclear, Electrical and Industrial Engineering departments.

"Over the 5.5 days of the bootcamp, what unfolded was not merely an instructional session but a dynamic interplay of ideas and worldviews. As mentors, we positioned ourselves as learners too, embracing every query and discussion as an opportunity to refine our own understandings".

The article uses a word it knows will sound wrong and defends it. A "spiritual awakening" "within the academic rigor of a bootcamp might seem misplaced, yet it perfectly encapsulates the profound internal shifts experienced by all participants. This was a transformation rooted not in religious revelation but in a sudden, deep recognition of our interconnectedness and the potential for collective educational empowerment."

What followed the closing ceremony is what the article treats as the real evidence: the bonds "did not simply fade away with the closing ceremony." The months after brought "a flurry of emails, virtual meetings, and collaborative projects that extended the dynamic spirit of the bootcamp" — together with mentorship of startups emerging from the university — "not only in providing technical guidance but also in instilling a sense of resilience and adaptive thinking among the young innovators."

What It Revealed

Their authority comes from being cultural brokers, not from having studied in Japan.

The article's explanation of why the eight-semester redesign was possible there and not elsewhere.

The biography is given precisely because the inference depends on it. Both were "active in political and social spheres during their time at a renowned university in East Java, an experience that not only shaped their worldview but also deeply ingrained a sense of community commitment." Both then stayed local for their master's and doctoral degrees — a choice the article sets against their "significant international experience in Japan".

The Japan period is described as "not merely an academic pursuit but a profound cultural immersion that enriched their understanding of global innovation practices." But the capacity the article credits is the combination rather than the credential: "This experience has enabled them to act as cultural brokers, skillfully integrating international standards with the nuanced cultural values of Indonesia."

And it is recorded as hard rather than natural. "Dr. Mardiyah often reflects on how this balance is critical, yet challenging, in fostering a local environment conducive to innovation-driven entrepreneurship." What the author takes from watching them is a leadership claim about his own country: "leadership in the Indonesian context is as much about guiding change as it is about embodying the values that underpin our society."

Gotong royong, named as the mechanism

Their emphasis on relationship-building "reflects the Indonesian cultural emphasis on 'gotong royong'—a spirit of communal cooperation and mutual assistance—which is pivotal in my own approach to leadership." It is offered as the reason support extends "beyond academic guidance," not as decoration.

What I Carry

Two academics earning less than their corporate peers raised two children into prestigious schools and two alumni into founders.

The article's evidence for their philosophy is domestic before it is professional, and it says so deliberately.

"Despite earning less than their corporate counterparts, Dr. Mardiyah and Dr. Taufik have managed to provide their children with a robust education, leading to their attendance at prestigious institutions—one graduating from a renowned design school and the other pursuing Economic Development." The second choice is read as inherited values rather than as a career decision: a field "that aligns closely with the family’s deep-rooted values of social contribution and sustainable growth."

The same pattern is traced outward to two alumni entrepreneurs. One "recently won a significant SME competition, demonstrating the practical impact of the lessons imbued by Dr. Mardiyah and Dr. Taufik." The other launched a business that "pivots from the common product-oriented model to a sustainable, customer-centric approach, showcases the transformative potential of learning under mentors who prioritize long-term value over immediate profit."

What the article claims from the pair of cases is modest and specific: "the ripple effect of authentic teaching and its capacity to not just educate but also inspire and transform communities." Two students is not a study, and the piece presents them as illustration rather than as proof.

The Unresolved

MSMEs are quick economic activity, and nobody has decided whether they are a strategy.

The article ends its analysis on a question it puts to itself and does not answer.

Should we continue to advocate for MSMEs as a quick path to economic activity, or should there be a more strategic emphasis on building larger, more sustainable industrial ecosystems?

— From Boston to Madura, 28 July 2024
Where the question comes from
"The prevalence of MSMEs in Indonesia, as Dr. Taufik points out, reflects a broader national inclination towards short-term economic activities, a trend that is deeply rooted in the cultural dimensions identified by Hofstede." The diagnosis is attributed to a colleague and the cultural frame to Hofstede; neither is presented as the author's own finding.
Why it is not resolved
The tension is named as personal as well as national — "the personal and professional tensions we navigate as educators and leaders striving to meld these complex elements." The article's own position is a balance rather than a choice: it commits to "foster environments that support both MSMEs and broader industrial growth."
What the piece asks of itself
Its opening sets three criteria — personal experience and reflexivity, cultural analysis, and narrative visibility — and warns that a text focused on "describing events and outcomes without sufficiently connecting these to the author's internal reflections or cultural critique" may not meet them. The article is written against its own test.

Sources

Sources

The article carries no reference list. Hofstede's cultural dimensions are named without a work or year, and the Innovation Driven Enterprise University Based model, the "2 credits course, 26 credits feel" design, the Mickey Mouse shape entrepreneurial teaching model and the Venture Tactics model are the author's own, described but not cited; none is resolved to a full entry here. Its material is the author's own record — the Kementerian Koordinator Ekonomi conference of November 2019, the online forums launched 8 September 2020, a first visit to Universitas Trunojoyo Madura on 20 March 2022, and a 5.5-day bootcamp — together with continuing conversation with two named colleagues. The two alumni entrepreneurs are named in credit for achievements the article reports; the first is given in two different forms within the same passage, and neither form is adopted over the other here. Their mentors' children are discussed but not named. The three-criteria primer on autoethnographic standards that opens the article is generic methodological apparatus and is not decked.
