Trained Like ChatGPT: Lessons from Starting a Career Abroad in Schlumberger Wireline, 1994

A filtering discipline that took years to acquire abroad — ignore the noise, read the intent — turned out to be what a machine does by design, and turned out to be insufficient the moment he came home.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

A recruiter told him his Indonesian instinct to read tone would cost him his performance.

Alexandria, ETC-46, November 1994. Before the first international assignment, the instruction that shaped everything after it.

In 1994 he was beginning "a career that would span 26 years across 51 countries, primarily in individualistic cultures." His recruiter, Ainul Azhar Ainul Jamal, gave him advice about communication before he left.

"As an Indonesian, you may overthink what people say, and that could affect your performance." The instruction that followed was to learn to disregard the noise — to ignore emotions, tone, or strong words — "and focus on what the speaker is trying to achieve."

It is worth naming what that advice actually asks of a person. It does not ask for thicker skin. It asks him to treat the emotional register of a conversation as interference in the channel rather than as part of the message, and to route around it.

The instruction, in one line

Separate the signal from the delivery, and act only on the signal.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Failure

He failed miserably at the advice before it became second nature.

The skill did not arrive with the instruction. It arrived years later, through error.

"At first, he struggled to apply this advice." More than struggled: "he failed miserably and ironically, often misinterpreting or overreacting to feedback."

The cost was not abstract. "His tendency to overanalyze emotions and tone in conversations created unnecessary tension and even held him back in his early assignments." A communication habit that works at home was, in that environment, a performance problem.

"But these failures became his greatest teachers." Through trial and error he learned to filter out the noise in conversations "and focus solely on the intent behind the message." The article is explicit that "This transformation didn't happen overnight" — and that only once the skill became second nature did it help him in environments "where clarity and problem-solving often matter more than emotional nuance."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Turn

Sales management required a second skill, aimed beneath the words rather than past them.

Promotion did not extend the first discipline. It asked for something close to its opposite.

Still at Schlumberger, promoted to Sales Manager, "he was trained even further" — this time to probe and to listen actively, uncovering what the article calls the reasons behind the reasons.

"This deeper skill set taught him how to go beyond words to understand motivations and underlying concerns, sharpening his ability to communicate and negotiate effectively."

The two disciplines are not the same, and the sequence matters. The first discards what sits on the surface of a conversation. The second digs beneath it. Together they describe a person trained to treat everything except stated intent as either noise to drop or a clue to follow — which is precisely the position from which the next observation becomes possible.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Mirror

ChatGPT described his hard-won filtering as its own default setting.

He asked the model how it handles frustration or anger, especially when people curse or use strong language while chatting with it.

When you express anger or frustration, even with strong language, I interpret it as feedback that something didn't meet your expectations.

— ChatGPT, quoted in the article
The rest of the answer
It went on: "I focus on identifying where I might have missed the mark" — whether that is tone, relevance, or precision — "and work to correct it immediately."
Why it landed
"Hearing this, he laughed at how closely this mirrored his own journey." Like the model, he had learned to focus on intent, tune out the noise, and respond with purpose. What cost him years of failed assignments is, for the machine, the specification.
The turn inside the recognition
The mirror is not the point of the piece. "But this reflection also made him think about the limits of that approach." Recognising that a skill can be described as a rule is also recognising that it will be applied where it does not fit.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Limit

Coming home, the noise he had trained himself to discard was the message.

"While filtering noise and focusing on intent had worked well internationally, he realized it wasn't always enough in every context."

What the training assumed
✗Tone, emotion and word choice are interference around a message
✗Clarity and problem-solving outrank emotional nuance
✗The speaker's stated intent is the thing to act on
✗A single communication strategy travels across contexts
✗Efficiency in reading people is the measure of skill
What he found on returning
✓"When he returned to his home country after years abroad, he encountered a communication style that placed significant weight on emotions, tone, and word choice"
✓"The nuances of how something was said often mattered as much as the message itself"
✓What was efficient abroad read as inattention at home
✓"What had been an effective global communication strategy sometimes led to misunderstandings at home"
✓The filter was still working; it was filtering out the content
The lesson he draws

"This realization taught him an important lesson: while filtering noise is an essential skill, it must be balanced with adaptability and cultural sensitivity. Communication is not just about delivering a message but also about meeting people where they are."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
What It Settles

Filtering is a skill; knowing when to stop filtering is the harder one.

The article addresses itself to students preparing for global careers, professionals managing diverse teams, and executives striving to lead internationally, and leaves them two lessons rather than a programme.

Communication isn't just about speaking or listening; it's about understanding the people and the environment in which we connect.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun
Lesson one
"The ability to focus on intent and disregard “noise” is critical for success in global settings."
Lesson two
"Adapting your communication to different audiences is equally essential" — "building trust and understanding starts with meeting people where they are."
Why both are needed at once
"This dual lesson—balancing clarity with adaptability—holds value for anyone navigating global or diverse contexts." Neither discipline is retired by the other; the difficulty is holding both and knowing which context is which.
Where it leaves him
"His journey of unlearning and relearning continues to inspire those around him." The account does not close the loop — it reports a person still switching between two settings, twenty-six years after being told to keep only one.
