(1 of 5) The Beatitudes: Not Just Hedone or Eudaemonia Happiness, But Divine One — Makarios

The word Jesus chose for "blessed" in the Beatitudes, makarios, names a happiness neither earned through virtue nor triggered by pleasure — a divine, unshakable state that God alone grants, and that a simple four-step reading algorithm can surface in each of the eight Beatitudes in turn.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

1.35 billion search results on happiness, and none of them are asking Jesus's question.

A sermon heard in 1999 in Interlaken, Switzerland has stayed vivid for 25 years — long enough to earn its own reading algorithm.

Situation

Happiness is one of the most searched, written-about subjects there is — 284 million results for books on finding it, more than 23,000 books with the word in their title.

Complication

Almost none of that literature is asking about the specific happiness Jesus names in Matthew 5 — a Greek word, makarios, that the flood of self-help content has no equivalent for.

Question

What does makarios actually mean, and why does Jesus reach for it instead of the words Greek philosophy already had for happiness?

Answer

Makarios is divine and unearned — closer to a gift bestowed than a state achieved — and each Beatitude follows the same four-step logic once that distinction is in view.

Framework

Greek already had two words for happiness. Jesus reached for a third.

Makarios does the work neither existing Greek term for happiness could do.

Eudaimonia and Hedone
✗Eudaimonia: human flourishing through virtue and purpose — noble, but dependent on one's own moral effort.
✗Hedone: fleeting sensory pleasure — food, comfort, entertainment — that fades as fast as it arrives.
✗Both are, in different ways, human-centered and conditional on circumstance.
✗Modern neuroscience confirms the pattern: sensory pleasure triggers a brief dopamine burst lasting only seconds.
Makarios
•A state of divine favor and joy that transcends external circumstances entirely.
•Not contingent on human achievement — it is bestowed by God, not earned.
•Unshakable: unaffected by life's challenges or successes alike.
•Echoed in the Hebrew ashrei of Psalm 1 — the same claim, that real joy comes from walking with God, not from circumstance.

Details

A four-step algorithm unlocks the same structure in every Beatitude.

Applied across all eight verses in Matthew 5:3-10, each one yields to the same reading.

1  ·  Name the divine gift
Identify what God bestows in this specific Beatitude — the kingdom of heaven, comfort, inheritance of the earth, satisfaction, mercy, sight of God, sonship.
2  ·  State that makarios is granted, not earned
The happiness named is a state of favor coming from God, not a reward for the disposition that precedes it in the verse.
3  ·  Recognize the connected disposition
The disposition named — poor in spirit, mourning, meek, hungering for righteousness — is not the cause of the blessing but its context.
4  ·  Close with the exclamation
"How blessed they are!" — the algorithm's fixed closing move, restated identically across all eight Beatitudes to keep the emphasis on gift, not merit.

Evidence

Applied to all eight Beatitudes, the pattern never breaks.

Matthew 5:3-10, each verse read through the same four-step lens.

APPLIED A
The poor in spirit and the persecuted (vv. 3, 10)
Both frame verses promise the same reward — "theirs is the kingdom of the heavens" — bracketing the whole set with the same divine gift.
APPLIED B
Those who mourn and the merciful (vv. 4, 7)
Comfort and mercy are both received, not achieved by grief or generosity alone — the state follows from God's action, not the disposition's intensity.
APPLIED C
The meek and the pure in heart (vv. 5, 8)
Inheriting the earth and seeing God are both promises about relationship to God's created order and God's own presence, not rewards for personality traits.
APPLIED D
Those hungering for righteousness and the peacemakers (vv. 6, 9)
Satisfaction and sonship both name an outcome that exceeds what the disposition itself could produce on its own.

Implications

Makarios recurs across the New Testament as a state, not a moment.

Three further examples the piece draws on to show makarios extending beyond the Beatitudes themselves.

Mary is called makarios in Luke 1:45 not for her role as mother of Jesus alone, but because she trusted God's promise. James 1:12 calls the one who remains steadfast under trial makarios — a reward for enduring, but still granted rather than self-generated.

Revelation 14:13 extends makarios even to "the dead who die in the Lord" — evidence that this is not a this-life-only category, but an enduring state that continues beyond it.

Paul's own life, especially Philippians 4:11-13 ("I have learned to be content whatever the circumstances"), is offered as the lived proof: contentment unwavering through suffering and imprisonment, because its source was never circumstantial to begin with.

Conclusion

Makarios sustains through mourning, meekness and persecution alike — because none of it was ever the cause.

The piece's own closing statement, setting up the four remaining parts of this series.

This kind of blessedness allows us to find peace in mourning, stay meek, hunger for righteousness, show mercy, remain pure, and make peace.
