LinkedIn Article  ·  Strategy · Where to Compete, and How  ·  19 September 2023

The Yin and Yang of Business Strategy: Balancing External and Internal Views

The article teaches two families of strategy theory by keeping them apart until the last move. Porter looks outward, supplying five forces, the value chain, the diamond and three generic strategies, and his sharpest contribution is negative: operational effectiveness is not strategy, because best practice is copied as soon as it is demonstrated. Firm Resources Theory looks inward through VRIN, with the resource-based view a subset of it rather than a synonym, extended by dynamic capabilities into sensing, seizing and transforming. Schlumberger is then read through both lenses at once, and Airbnb is used to show where the framework fails, since the non-substitutable claim collapses once hoteliers professionalise. The closing formulation is what the whole piece is built to deliver: Porter answers where to compete and Firm Resources answers how, so a firm using only one of them has answered only half the question.

Introduction

The opening act of the lecture is catching itself in a misquotation.

The article was about to attribute "I know that I know nothing" to Socrates, and then checked. "I was about to write “I know that I know nothing” and pretentiously about to type “Socrates 4BC”, but then I learned the exact wording is not found anywhere in ancient texts “belonged” to Socrates."

What it substitutes is the passage that does exist, from Plato's Apology, where Socrates concludes after questioning politicians, poets and craftsmen: "it seems that I am wiser than he is to this small extent, that I do not think that I know what I do not know."

The correction is not decoration. It demonstrates the method the rest of the piece runs on, which is checking a claim against its source before repeating it, and it does so at the author's own expense rather than someone else's.

The audience is named as students and aspiring entrepreneurs rather than executives, on the argument that "understanding strategy isn't just for CEOs" and that these frameworks are "applied daily by companies large and small to gain competitive advantages".

A stated blind spot, unusual in a teaching piece

"I never paid attention to the grand-theories." The twenty-three years before this article were spent on middle-range and low-range theory instead, judged by the books read and the case studies discussed, and the article adds that he attended only schools using the HBS participative case-study method.

Framework

Porter looks outward, and answers the question of where a firm should stand.

Three frameworks and one strategic choice, all concerned with the environment a firm operates in rather than with what it owns.

1  ·  Five Forces
Competitive rivalry among existing firms; bargaining power of suppliers; bargaining power of buyers; threat of new entrants, meaning "The ease with which new competitors can enter the market"; and threat of substitutes, being "The extent to which other products or services can replace the ones offered by the firms in the industry."
2  ·  The Value Chain
Primary activities covering inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service. Support activities covering firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development and procurement. Used to find where value can be added or cost removed.
3  ·  The Diamond Model
Four attributes governing national competitive advantage: factor conditions such as skilled labour and infrastructure, demand conditions at home, related and supporting industries that are internationally competitive, and "Firm Strategy, Structure, and Rivalry", covering how companies are created, organised, managed and how fiercely they compete domestically.
4  ·  Three generic strategies
Cost leadership, differentiation and focus, each requiring different resources, competencies and target markets. The principles attached are uniqueness against efficiency, trade-offs in how to compete, and fit among activities, since "For a strategy to be sustainable, there needs to be a good fit among the various activities in which a firm engages."
The risks the article attaches to each generic strategy

Cost leadership invites price wars and margin compression, and "maintaining that lead requires continuous cost optimization". Differentiation invites feature bloat, "A focus on adding more and more unique features can lead to complexity, higher costs, and potential alienation of the core customer base who may not need all those features."

Gap

Operational effectiveness is not strategy, because best practice is copied the moment it works.

This is the sharpest claim in the external half, and it is stated as a negative: a great many management tools improve operations without producing any advantage that lasts.

Strategy is about doing things differently, not simply doing them better than everyone else. And it's the key to competitive advantage.

— The Yin and Yang of Business Strategy, 19 September 2023
The tools named
Total quality management, benchmarking, time-based competition, reengineering, change management. The article grants that "The resulting operational improvements have often been dramatic" before turning on them.
Why the improvement does not hold
"Yet many companies have failed to translate those gains into sustainable profitability. Simply improving operational effectiveness does not provide a robust competitive advantage because rarely are “best practice” advantages sustainable."
The mechanism of erosion
"Once a company establishes a new best practice, its rivals tend to copy it quickly." Demonstrating that something works is what makes it imitable, so the better the improvement, the faster it spreads.
What this sets up
If doing the same things better cannot hold an advantage, then the advantage has to come from position or from something a rival cannot acquire. The first is Porter's answer, and the second is what the internal half of the article supplies.

Details

Firm Resources Theory is wider than the resource-based view, and the distinction is load-bearing.

The article spends real effort separating two terms that are usually used interchangeably, because the hierarchy between them decides how the rest of the internal half is organised.

Firm Resources Theory is the umbrella. It "is a broader framework that studies how firms utilize various types of resources to achieve competitive advantages", and it "can encompass multiple frameworks and perspectives, not limited to the RBV".

The resource-based view is the specific case within it, concerned with resources that are valuable, rare, inimitable and non-substitutable. "In essence, RBV can be considered a subset of Firm Resources Theory."

The attribution differs accordingly. RBV is linked to Jay Barney and Birger Wernerfelt, while the umbrella has no single author: "Firm Resources Theory doesn't have a single attributed developer; it is an overarching theme or area of study within strategic management that has been shaped by contributions from many scholars".

The VRIN criteria, as the article defines them

Valuable, enabling strategies that improve efficiency or effectiveness. Rare, "not controlled or possessed by many competing firms". Inimitable, difficult for competitors to copy. Non-substitutable, "Resources that cannot be easily replaced with an alternative." VRIO adds organisation, since a firm must be structured to capture the value.

Evidence

Dynamic capabilities extend the internal view from what a firm has to what it can become.

The extension matters because VRIN describes a stock of resources at a moment, and markets do not hold still.

1  ·  Sensing opportunities
"This is about recognizing changes in the external business environment. Companies with strong sensing capabilities are good at market research, customer feedback analysis, and recognizing disruptive technologies."
2  ·  Seizing opportunities
Acting on what has been sensed, whether by developing new products, entering new markets, or taking up new technologies. The article treats this as the step most often missed by firms that saw the change coming.
3  ·  Transforming resources
"The ability to transform or reconfigure existing resources and capabilities in response to changing conditions. Companies need to have flexible structures and processes to adapt successfully."
4  ·  The three worked examples
Tesla sensed electric vehicles and seized with battery technology, transforming through self-driving investment. Netflix "began as a DVD rental service but sensed the opportunity in online streaming", seizing it by rebuilding its business model and making original content. Spotify sensed a shift in consumption and used a freemium model that changed the industry's revenue structure.
The knowledge-based view, added alongside

Tacit knowledge residing in employees and hard to transfer; explicit knowledge, codified as patents; and social complexity, "the intricate relationships and culture within a firm, which are often difficult for competitors to replicate". The article notes the practical difficulty: knowledge resources resist measurement and leak.

Case

Schlumberger carries both lenses at once, which is what makes it the article's worked example.

One firm is analysed at length, and it is analysed twice over: once through VRIN and the resource view, once through Porter's positioning and five forces.

On the resource side, the valuable asset is "Schlumberger's extensive reservoir monitoring and data analysis capabilities". Rarity comes from patented technologies. Inimitability comes from scale and relationships: "Their international scale and long-standing relationships with many national oil companies make them difficult to imitate." Non-substitutability rests on R&D investment and specialised training.

On the Porter side, the strategy is read as differentiation rather than cost. "Schlumberger predominantly employs a differentiation strategy", offering customised technological solutions that justify premium pricing, with focus supplied by concentrating on large enterprises and national oil companies within one sector.

The five forces reading is mixed rather than flattering: size gives some bargaining power over suppliers, buyers retain significant leverage that differentiation only partly offsets, capital and technical expertise deter entrants, substitutes are limited while oil and gas demand persists, and rivalry is intense among a small number of major players.

Disclosure the article itself does not carry

The author spent thirteen years at Schlumberger, across a career covering fifty-two countries. The article presents the case study without stating that connection, so the level of operational detail in it comes from inside the firm rather than from public sources.

The Objection

Airbnb is where the author stops agreeing with the framework and says so.

The article applies RBV to Airbnb and then breaks off to dispute its own analysis, in the only passage of the piece written as direct disagreement.

The objection opens bluntly: "Sorry, but the N in Airbnb is the hoteliers themselves : much more professional." If professional hoteliers can meet the same need, the resource is substitutable, and the N in VRIN does not hold.

The second objection relocates what is genuinely inimitable. "Inimitability is within the founders and its innovation ecosystem not about strong brand, interface, rating system." Brand and interface are copyable; the founding vision and the culture around it are not.

The rest is conceded with precision rather than wholesale. Rarity holds, "because it addresses non consuming market as opposed attacking the chain hotel's client", and value holds, but "for the clients mainly", which is a narrower claim than the framework usually makes.

What the disagreement establishes about the framework

The article's own reply concedes the point and draws the general lesson: the criteria "are not static and can evolve over time as the company and its competitive environment change". A VRIN assessment is therefore dated, and often "more easily identified in hindsight or once a company has achieved a large scale".

Conclusion

Porter answers where to compete and Firm Resources answers how, so either one alone answers half.

The closing synthesis is the reason the two halves were kept apart for thirty-eight pages, and it is stated as a division of labour rather than as a ranking.

Porter, the macro view
•Concerned with the external business environment
•Five Forces to read the industry landscape
•Relative positioning against competitors
•Answers: where should we compete?
•Identifies the market or segment offering an edge
Firm Resources, the micro view
•Concerned with internal assets and capabilities
•VRIN and the knowledge-based view to read what a firm holds
•Which resources are most valuable and how to deploy them
•Answers: how should we compete?
•Identifies what makes the firm unique on the inside
How the two are meant to be used together

"For instance, a firm could use Porter's strategies to identify a niche market and then apply the VRIN framework to identify which internal resources would give them an advantage in that market." The article calls the combination a 360-degree view, sequencing the external question first and the internal question second.
