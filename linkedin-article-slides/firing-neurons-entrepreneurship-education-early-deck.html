Firing Neurons: Why Entrepreneurship Education Must Start Early

The article argues that entrepreneurship education is timed wrongly, and it argues it from neurobiology rather than from pedagogy. The brain builds far more synaptic connections than it keeps, prunes on a use-it-or-lose-it rule through late childhood and adolescence, and myelinates what survives, so the cost of acquiring a disposition rises sharply once the plastic window closes. Dormant neurons are therefore not lost cells but connections never formed, and reactivating them later is harder than stimulating them on time. The article maps five neurochemicals onto traits normally described as temperament, assembles a set of early starters from Buffett and Branson to Khabib Nurmagomedov and Rudy Hartono, and draws the conclusion that matters for policy: if adaptability, innovation and risk assessment are now general life skills rather than commercial ones, entrepreneurship education is not vocational training and cannot wait for university.

Introduction

Entrepreneurship is taught to adults and built in children, and the gap between those two facts is the article's subject.

Situation

The word evokes tenacious innovators challenging conventions in adult markets. The article's opening move is to reject that as the starting point: "the roots of entrepreneurship run far deeper than just adult ambition or market strategy", and to locate them in neurobiological development instead.

Complication

The biographies of famous entrepreneurs keep showing commercial behaviour at ages long before any business education, "from Warren Buffet buying his first stock at age 11 to Richard Branson's budding ventures selling Christmas trees and parakeets". Precocity of that kind is not explained by curriculum.

Question

What gives rise to that early capability, and if the answer lies in how the brain forms, at what age does an intervention still work?

Answer

The answer the article gives is structural. Neurons, synapses and the pathways formed and reinforced in the formative years are what later reads as entrepreneurial disposition, so the effective intervention is early exposure rather than late instruction.

Why this is not only a business question

The article widens the definition before it starts: adaptability, innovative thinking, resilience and a problem-solving mindset are treated as the content of entrepreneurial thinking, and those are held to be valuable across fields rather than confined to starting businesses.

Framework

The brain overbuilds early, then spends two decades deciding what to keep.

The developmental sequence the article sets out is one of surplus followed by selection. The figures are its own, and where they differ between sections they are reproduced here as printed.

At birth
One hundred billion neurons, none of them committed
"At birth, the brain is brimming with a staggering 100 billion neurons", each ready to connect. The article stresses that they are dynamic rather than static, so every interaction has the potential to create synapses.
Age 2 to 3
Synaptic density peaks at roughly twice the adult level
The count peaks at "peaking at around 15,000 synapses for each neuron". The article states the comparison plainly: "This dense network is about twice that of an adult brain." Everything from a lullaby to the texture of a toy contributes to that web.
Age 3 to 7
The prefrontal cortex reaches its own maximum
"Synaptic density reaches its zenith, especially in the prefrontal cortex, crucial for executive functions." The article marks this as the window for structured learning and for creative work.
By age 6
Half the connections are already gone
The conclusion section states it as an established finding: "By age 3, a child's brain is dense with nearly 1,000 trillion synapses, which reduces by half by age 6, a phenomenon due to synaptic pruning" (Huttenlocher & Dabholkar, 1997; Tau & Peterson, 2010).
Age 12 to 25
A second surge, then the heavier pruning
"A secondary surge of synaptic proliferation, especially in the prefrontal regions, is followed by extensive pruning." This is the period the article credits with shaping higher-order cognition, abstract thinking and risk assessment.
A figure that does not sit still across the article

Section 2.9 places the thousand-trillion peak at two years: "Their brains, growing at a staggering pace, hold roughly 1,000 trillion synaptic connections by age two." The conclusion places it at three. The zenith of synaptic density is put at three to seven in one section and the halving at six in another. The figures are recorded here as the article gives them rather than reconciled.

Mechanism

Pruning runs on use it or lose it, so a childhood decides what the adult brain will find easy.

The optimisation the article describes is subtractive. In its own summary of the rule, "less frequently used connections are eliminated, and frequently used ones are strengthened", and it names the principle behind that as "use it or lose it".

The consequence it draws is specific rather than general. Where problem-solving, risk-taking and leadership are practised consistently in early life, those are the pathways that survive selection, and they are then available at low cost for the rest of a life.

The reverse case is the one the article spends most space on, and it is careful about what it is claiming. Underdevelopment of a region is not cell death: "This underdevelopment is not about the loss of neurons per se, but rather the suboptimal formation of synaptic connections among them, leading to what can be described as 'dormant' or less active neurons." Dormancy is a missing connection, not a missing neuron.

Its causes are listed as absence of varied experience, a monotonous environment, or specific traumas that lead a person to avoid certain stimuli. And the implications are sorted across cognition, emotion and behaviour, with the entrepreneurial case landing in the third: dormancy in regions tied to motivation, risk-taking or innovation is said to affect entrepreneurial drive.

The asymmetry that makes timing the whole argument

Dormant neurons can be reactivated with targeted intervention, therapy or training, "though it's generally more challenging than stimulating them during the brain's peak plastic periods." Later is possible. Later is expensive.

Mechanism

Myelination is what makes a practised pathway fast, and it runs on the same schedule.

The second process the article sets out is insulation rather than selection. Myelin wraps the axon in a fatty sheath and speeds the transmission of electrical signals, and the article uses the wiring analogy directly: without insulation the signal disperses, with it the signal is channelled.

The timing again does the argumentative work. "Myelination begins during the last trimester of fetal development and continues well into adulthood", but the most rapid phases are placed in early childhood and adolescence.

Firing and insulation operate together, and Hebb's formulation is the one the article leans on: "neurons that fire together, wire together" (Hebb, 1949). Repetition is what converts a transient connection into a permanent pathway, which is why the article treats a hyper-plastic brain as the cheapest place to install a habit.

The case study offered for the whole mechanism is linguistic rather than commercial, and it is the strongest evidence in the piece because the effect is uncontested: "children exposed to multiple languages before the age of seven are more likely to be fluent, multi-lingual speakers" (Costa et al 2014), while adults acquiring the same language struggle with accent and grammatical nuance.

The illustration it draws from outside entrepreneurship

The motor cortex in musicians who begin training young is cited as showing significant growth and heightened activity, because the repeated fine movements of playing an instrument reinforce that region. The article's point is that the same logic should apply to the regions entrepreneurship draws on.

Evidence

Five neurochemicals carry the traits usually described as entrepreneurial temperament.

The article assembles what it calls a Neurochemical Drivers of Entrepreneurship Matrix, giving each chemical a role, a relevance and a research citation. The matrix itself is an image that does not survive text extraction.

1  ·  Dopamine: the motivator
Associated with pleasure, motivation and goal-directed behaviour. Success or positive feedback releases it, which the article says reinforces further risk-taking and innovation. Nicolaou et al (2019) is cited for the finding that "elevated dopamine levels could drive individuals to be more proactive".
2  ·  Serotonin: the mood stabiliser
Regulates mood, appetite and sleep, and balanced levels are tied to stable decision-making under pressure. Miyazaki (2020) in Science Advances is cited for the finding that "serotonin levels profoundly impact patience and decision-making, essential attributes for entrepreneurs".
3  ·  Cortisol: the stress responder
Released under stress to prepare a fight-or-flight response. The article treats sustained elevation as a cognitive liability, citing Souza et al (2009), which "linked prolonged high cortisol levels to decreased cognitive performance".
4  ·  Endorphins: the resilience boosters
Natural painkillers released during physical activity. Dunbar et al (2021) is cited for the position that "endorphin release might play a role in social bonding and group-cohesive behaviors", which the article reads as relevant to team-based ventures.
5  ·  Oxytocin: the trust hormone
Released during close personal interaction and tied to social bonding. Kosfeld et al (2005) in Nature is cited for the demonstration that "oxytocin increases trust in interpersonal interactions", which the article applies to negotiation.
The emotional case made alongside the chemical one

Section 2.5 argues that entrepreneurship is emotional as well as cognitive: the amygdala shows different activation patterns in entrepreneurs assessing risk (Lahti et al., 2019), high-emotion events are recalled with greater detail than neutral ones (Barnier et al, 2007), and a history of overcoming personal adversity is associated with higher resilience in business (Leipold, 2018).

Details

The early starters did not merely accumulate more hours; they accumulated them while the brain was still plastic.

The article gathers a set of figures who began very young, and its reading of them separates the practice argument from the plasticity argument.

CASE A
Warren Buffett
"At the tender age of six, Warren Buffett began his entrepreneurial journey, selling packs of gum to neighbors." Golf balls, newspaper rounds and pinball machine investments followed in his teens, and the article credits those years with teaching compounding, discernment in investment and the merit of hard work.
CASE B
Richard Branson
"Richard Branson's first venture was a magazine named 'Student' that he started at 16." The article traces branding, customer engagement and the habit of diversifying ventures back to that undertaking, and reads Virgin Records as its continuation.
CASE C
Bill Gates
Before Microsoft, Gates entered software by "creating a tic-tac-toe game at age 13", which led to the traffic-measurement program Traf-O-Data, described as his first entrepreneurial endeavour. The lesson drawn is the ability to foresee technological trends.
CASE D
Khabib Nurmagomedov
Trained by his father Abdulmanap, a decorated judo and sambo fighter, "Khabib began training in combat sports when he was merely a toddler", moving into judo and wrestling in his formative years. The article's cover image is captioned on the childhood training with a bear.
The rest of the set, and the rule it is used to question

Maria Callas at eleven in Athens, Rudy Hartono at ten in Surabaya and eight All England titles, Michelle Wie at four, Naomi Osaka at three, alongside Mozart before five, Magnus Carlsen at five, Serena Williams and Picasso at seven. Against Gladwell's claim that "10,000 hours of deliberate practice can lead to expertise in a field", the article's position is that the early start matters for the plasticity as much as for the hours.

Implications

Once these are life skills rather than commercial ones, entrepreneurship education stops being vocational training.

The final substantive section widens the claim deliberately, and the widening is what turns a neuroscience argument into an education-policy argument.

Entrepreneurship education as normally positioned
✗A subject for those intending to start a business
✗Delivered at university or in professional programmes
✗Measured by ventures founded
✗Confined to commerce, finance and management
✗Optional, sitting beside the core curriculum
What the article argues it has become
✓Adaptability, as technology keeps redefining job profiles
✓Innovation as a way of reading challenges as opportunities
✓Risk assessment, from personal investment to public questions
✓A problem-solving disposition applied to climate, inequality and instability
✓Global citizenship, given problems no single country resolves alone
The societal case, as the article states it

Young entrepreneurs are credited with stimulating local economies, creating jobs and bringing solutions to long-standing social problems, and with carrying sustainable practice into business as they mature, so the return is economic and environmental at once.

Conclusion

The timing argument, once accepted, reassigns the responsibility from business schools to early education.

The conclusion restates the developmental evidence and then draws the consequence that the rest of the article has been building toward.

Training in these areas from a young age not only shapes business leaders but also individuals prepared for the complexities of our changing societal landscapes

— Firing Neurons: Why Entrepreneurship Education Must Start Early, 24 August 2023
What the developmental evidence establishes
Pruning strengthens vital connections and optimises the brain for varied learning (Paolicelli et al., 2011; Casey et al., 2005), while myelination continues through childhood and adolescence with differing effects across regions (Fields, 2008; Yeung et al., 2014).
What the biographies contribute
The article reports a growing consensus that early ventures influence brain pathways in ways conducive to business intuition and acumen, citing Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews and Kelly (2007) on perseverance and passion for long-term goals.
Why the qualities are now general
In its own phrasing, "the qualities integral to entrepreneurship - adaptability, innovation, risk assessment - are increasingly crucial" (Robinson, 2011; Kuratko, 2005), which removes the ground for treating them as a specialist subject.
Where Part 2 takes this
The article directs the reader forward to Part 2 on brain formation and lifelong journeys, which tests the same framework against a single life rather than against the literature, and against the question of what happens to the brain after the plastic window closes.
