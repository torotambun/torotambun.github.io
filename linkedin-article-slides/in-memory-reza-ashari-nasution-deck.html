In Memory of Prof. Reza Ashari Nasution (2 March 1976 - 12 May 2024)

Students attach to their professors rather than to their universities, and the literature says so. The evidence offered here is one man who taught twenty-one sessions while terminally ill without saying so, who answered emails in sentences rather than in acknowledgements, and who at professorial rank welcomed a student's new frameworks. What he left behind is not an institutional legacy but a set of exchanges the student can still name.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

He coughed, turned off his camera, and I did not know he was ill until the third class.

Twelve sessions in the Preparation Exam class, nine in Behavioral Science, in the author's first PhD semester.

"He often coughed during class and would sometimes politely ask for a brief time out to refresh himself, turning off his camera. At the time, I didn't realize he was ill." The interruptions were read as interruptions, nothing more.

"Despite these interruptions, his positive energy, inviting demeanor, and engaging voice always drew me in, encouraging me to listen attentively and prepare my questions." And then the sentence that gives the piece its centre of gravity: "He was younger than me, he traveled less than me, but I wanted to know him more, as he seems to know something about life that I don't."

"It wasn't until the third class that someone mentioned his illness. I was shocked and found myself questioning the universe, but who am I to judge such matters?" What he did with that knowledge was small and is recorded exactly: "I recall one instance when I wished him well at the start of a class. I looked at his Zoom window and saw him nod and say thanks, ever the gentleman, so calm and composed."

The Claim

Students attach to professors, not to universities, and the literature says so.

The article makes an argument, not only a tribute, and it brings six studies to it.

"Students often prioritize their relationship with their professors over their connection with the university itself." The support is assembled rather than asserted: student-teacher relationships "significantly impact various student outcomes, such as academic achievement, engagement, and overall well-being" (Kincade et al., 2020), and understanding what students expect of professors "is crucial for enhancing the professor-student relationship and improving academic performance" (Sánchez et al., 2011).

The institutional consequence is the part that matters for the argument. The quality of out-of-class relationships "not only influences academic effort but also affects students' sense of connection to the university and their overall educational experience" (Ellis, 2012). The university is downstream of the professor, not the other way round.

And what makes a professor effective is not only what he knows: they are "characterized not only by their subject matter expertise but also by their ability to engage students, focus on student needs, and build rapport with them" (Kim & Olson, 2016). Where trust exists, "students trust their professors, they tend to engage more in lessons, leading to better learning outcomes" (Özer et al., 2018).

Why the literature is here at all

A memorial does not require citations. This one uses them to convert a personal loss into a general claim — that personal interaction is "a high-impact practice associated with positive outcomes" (Cistulli & Snyder, 2022) — so that what is being mourned is legible as something institutions can either produce or fail to.

Field Notes

He replied in meaningful sentences, not Thanks and Noted.

The evidence for the claim is a set of small, checkable behaviours across four channels.

OBSERVED A
What his emails showed
"His email communication indicated he spends times to digest, to discern and to show compassion." And the concrete test: "He replies with meaningful sentences, not only exclamation words: "Thanks", "Noted". He shows humanity in him." That last line is repeated after each channel in turn.
OBSERVED B
Every channel, without hierarchy
"Whether through email," through WhatsApp, "or through his likes on my LinkedIn posts or article, or via telephone conversation, he ensured I received the support needed, never missing an opportunity to provide feedback or guidance." What the article names as the quality: "His openness to various communication channels made him an accessible and supportive figure for me."
OBSERVED C
Responsiveness as a trait, not a courtesy
"Prof. Nasution's approach to teaching was characterized by his exceptional responsiveness." It is listed first among his qualities, before expertise — which is consistent with the literature the article has just cited.
OBSERVED D
What is not in the record
The article reproduces screenshots of the email and WhatsApp exchanges it describes. These are images and do not survive text extraction, so the messages themselves are not available; what can be reported is the author's characterisation of them, which is what this slide carries.

What It Revealed

A professorship in his mid-forties, and enough humility to take a student's new framework.

The asymmetry the article keeps returning to: seniority in one direction, openness in the other.

"What set Prof. Nasution apart was his genuine interest in my work and ideas and his open-mindedness." The rank is stated so the reader can weigh it: "Despite his own vast knowledge, having earned his Professorship in Digital Business Strategy in his mid forties, he was so humble that he welcomed and encouraged the exploration of new frameworks and perspectives from his students."

A professorship at that age is unusual, and it makes the openness costly rather than cheap — there was standing to defend, and he did not defend it.

The comparison the author reaches for is one he has written about before: Prof. Nasution "embodied the qualities of an exceptional educator described by RM Sosrokartono" — the subject of his own Jakarta Post column on the transformative role of educators. "Prof. Nasution was not just a teacher but a motivator in his own way and a friend in need (..is a friend indeed)."

What survives is domestic in scale and stated without inflation: "His legacy lives on in my class-notes left in the Microsoft Notes and presence he imparted, both inside and outside the classroom."

What I Carry

I am still hoping someone can pass this message to his family.

The article ends by addressing people it cannot reach, and says so in a parenthesis.

May they find strength and comfort in the cherished memories of his life and the enduring impact he had on so many.

— In Memory of Prof. Reza Ashari Nasution, 29 June 2024
The request, made in the open
"(I hope one of my connections can pass this message to his beloved family if it is appropriate)." Published on a professional network six weeks after the death, with no assurance it will arrive — the conditional "if it is appropriate" is doing real work.
What the death is offered as
"His journey reminded us all of the fragility of life and the importance of unselfishly serving and doing good for others. We only live our lives once, and Prof. Nasution's example inspires us to make a positive impact every day."
The prayer that was already said
"In recognition of my respect for him and wishing him well, I recall starting a class by expressing my prayers for his health and happiness through Zoom." It returns to the moment in the opening — the nod, the thanks — and lets that be the whole exchange.
