He Is Entering Pangkur

Tembang Macapat reads eleven Javanese poetic meters as one human life, and its ninth phase — Pangkur, the turning away — is not decline to be managed but withdrawal to be honoured. A man at the end of Durma is entering it. The young founders reading are not behind; they are in Sinom, and Sinom is where the map says they should be.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

Most of the world has never encountered the map Javanese literature drew of a life.

Told in the third person, as the article tells it. He has been sitting with this map recently — not as an academic, as a man.

Tembang Macapat is eleven classical poetic meters embedded in Javanese court literature. Each carries a name whose etymology is so precise, and so achingly human, that over centuries the meters were interpreted not merely as verse forms but as an allegorical map of the entire human journey.

It runs from the soul's first floating in the darkness of the womb to its final return to the Source of all things. What he sees looking back at him from that map is Phase Nine.

But before arriving there, the whole road has to be walked.

The claim the map makes

"Eleven phases. One life. Nothing wasted." No stage is a detour and none is a delay. That is the premise everything else in the piece depends on.

The Map

Eleven phases, one life, and not one of them wasted.

The arc rises through worldly achievement, peaking at Dhandhanggula, and then begins deliberately to let go.

Phase 1
Maskumambang — The Floating Gold
The soul before and during conception: pure, weightless, uncorrupted. Every human life, the tradition insists, begins as something luminous.
Phase 2
Mijil — The Emergence
Birth. The rupture into light and noise and radical vulnerability. The world receives a new stranger.
Phase 3
Sinom — The Young Shoot
Adolescence and early youth. Growth, curiosity, the first formation of character. Roots forming beneath soil not yet tested by drought.
Phase 4
Kinanthi — The Guided One
From kanthi, to be accompanied. The season of being led by mentors, elders and teachers. You do not walk alone here. You are held.
Phase 5
Asmaradana — The Fire of Love
From asmara, love, and dahana, fire. The most combustible phase. Longing, passion, the search for a life partner. A fire that can illuminate everything, or consume it.
Phase 6
Gambuh — The Union
From jumbuh, to harmonise, to become one. Marriage. Family. The merging of two lives into a shared vision and covenant.
Phase 7
Dhandhanggula — The Sweet Vessel
The apex of worldly achievement: career, wealth, recognition. But the Javanese were never naive about sweetness — the vessel must hold something more than sugar, or it remains, despite its beauty, empty.
Phase 8
Durma — The Giving Phase
From darma, to give, to serve. Having accumulated, one must now distribute. Mentorship. Generosity. The self expands beyond personal triumph toward something communal and lasting.
Phase 9
Pangkur — The Turning Away
From mungkur, to turn one's back on. Withdrawal from worldly noise. Contemplation. Drawing closer to God. The soul's deliberate, sacred release. This is where he stands.
Phase 10
Megatruh — The Separation
From megat, to separate, and ruh, spirit. Death. The soul's departure from the body. The closing of the temporal chapter and the opening of the eternal.
Phase 11
Pocung — The Return
The shrouded body. The soul journeys beyond the cycle to Sang Murbeng Dumadi, the Source and Sovereign of all existence. Sangkan Paraning Dumadi. Not an ending. A homecoming.

The Western Parallel

Erikson asks whether a life was lived well; Macapat asks what is left to release.

Erikson's eight stages are arguably the most influential life-stage framework in Western scholarship. Notice what they assume.

Erikson (1950), Eight Stages
•Trust vs. Mistrust; Autonomy vs. Shame; Initiative vs. Guilt
•Industry vs. Inferiority; Identity vs. Role Confusion
•Intimacy vs. Isolation; Generativity vs. Stagnation
•Ego Integrity vs. Despair — the final stage asks whether one lived well enough to avoid despair
•The interior work of a life is a psychological negotiation with the past
Macapat, Eleven Phases
•Not did I do enough, but what is left to release
•Not was my life meaningful, but to whom and for what am I now being given
•Withdrawal is a named phase, not a symptom of one
•The arc continues past death into return, rather than closing at integrity
•"Macapat does not pathologize withdrawal. It sanctifies it."

Phase Nine

He is at Pangkur, which is not performed humility but an interior turning.

He has crossed fifty-two countries, built companies, earned a doctorate summa cum laude, written hundreds of columns and articles, lectured, advised ministers, and sat on boards.

That is a life carried at full velocity across Dhandhanggula and Durma. He is not done. But something in him is shifting.

What he finds himself turning away from is the noise — the compulsive need to be present in every room, the subtle tyranny of relevance, the measuring of life in metrics that were never truly his to begin with. Borrowed currencies from a world that moves fast and rarely stops to ask: fast toward what, exactly?

"Pangkur is not a press release. It is not performed humility." It is quieter than that, and more interior. "It is the moment a man stops building his biography and starts listening to his soul."

What he wants now

"What he wants now is depth over volume. Presence over productivity." Silence that teaches over noise that merely stimulates. To know God more than to be known by men.

A Note to the Young

The young founder is in Sinom, and Sinom is not a lesser place.

Addressed directly to young founders reading the piece, and framed as location rather than deficiency.

01
Where you are
Sinom, perhaps Kinanthi
Probably still finding your footing between the two. That is not an insult. That is the truth of where the journey begins — and the map treats it as a phase, not a shortfall.
02
Why it feels otherwise
The confidence of the uncharted
The Dunning-Kruger effect describes the tendency of those early in a learning curve to overestimate their competence, precisely because they do not yet know enough to understand how much they do not know. Most young founders live here. He did too, once.
03
What the map asks
Do not rush to Dhandhanggula
"Sinom is sacred. Kinanthi is sacred. Do not rush to Dhandhanggula." Do not perform Durma before you have lived it. "You are not behind. You are exactly where the journey requires you to be."
The sentence that closes the note

"Every phase is necessary. Every phase is enough." There is a map; the instruction is to trust it and walk it fully, not to find a way around it.

What He Carries

A human being must not lose their humanity in the rush to be impressive.

Wong Jowo ojo ilang Jowone — a Javanese must not lose their Javaneseness. The piece then widens the claim past Java.

But perhaps — for all of us, wherever we come from — a human being must not lose their humanity in the rush to become something impressive.

— He Is Entering Pangkur
Pangkur is the reminder
Not a retirement announcement and not a withdrawal from usefulness. A named phase that the tradition treats as sacred rather than as decline, offered to a reader who may have no vocabulary for it.
He is listening
The piece ends on that, and then on Rahayu. Rahayu. Rahayu. — a blessing, not a conclusion. It makes no policy claim and offers no recommendations, and none are supplied here.
