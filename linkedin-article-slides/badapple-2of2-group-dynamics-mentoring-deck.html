(2 of 2) How a Bad Apple Can Spread—or Stay Contained? Exploring Group Dynamics and the Importance of One-on-Group Mentoring

The same cohort's own personality averages showed nothing alarming, yet high Agreeableness became the mechanism that let one student's emotional volatility spread into a near-mutiny — and the piece's own resolution is structural, one-on-one mentoring, not blame.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

The 2024H1 cohort's own averages looked cooperative and organized, with nothing to predict what followed.

The whole batch exhibited mid-to-high scores in key traits, with nothing on the surface to suggest disruption.

The 2024H1 program presented an unusual challenge, with personality traits that, while not extreme, played a significant role in group dynamics. The whole batch exhibited mid-to-high scores in key traits, with Neuroticism averaging 54.94, Openness at 69.61, Conscientiousness at 71.17, and Agreeableness at 70.50. These figures indicate a generally cooperative, organized, and conscientious group. On the surface, there were no clear signs that these traits would lead to disruptive behavior.

Yet the group nearly caused a mutiny, raising questions about external factors that went beyond their personality traits. High Agreeableness typically suggests a preference for harmony and cooperation, but when cohesion breaks down, this same trait can facilitate collective resistance, especially when frustrations are not addressed.

Gap

The traits alone did not predict rebellion — the environment created a context in which conflict could easily emerge.

Despite average to high Neuroticism, above-average Conscientiousness, and high Agreeableness, external influences appear to have triggered the disruption.

External influences such as leadership dynamics, perceived fairness, or unmet expectations appear to have triggered the disruption. These situational factors, combined with personality traits, were likely responsible for the group's behavior.

In particular, perceived injustice or poor leadership decisions can spark frustration within a highly conscientious group. This frustration, when voiced, can be amplified by the group's strong tendency toward agreement and cooperation. The solidarity that high Agreeableness fosters can quickly turn collective, spreading dissatisfaction and leading to resistance.

Framework

One student's emotional volatility found quick support among her highly agreeable peers.

Compounding the dynamic was a student with high Neuroticism, whose emotional volatility made her more susceptible to frustration.

Initially showing signs of high Openness and Conscientiousness, this student's emotional instability undermined her ability to maintain these positive traits. Her concerns resonated with the group, and once voiced, her frustration found quick support among her highly agreeable peers. The group, instead of resolving the issue individually, collectively aligned with her grievances. The high Agreeableness, which ordinarily supports teamwork, became a powerful force that accelerated the disruption.

This interaction demonstrates that a single source of instability can spark discontent that quickly spreads in an environment where other traits — like high Agreeableness — are present. What started as one individual's emotional response turned into a collective issue because the group, prone to align with each other, absorbed and magnified the discontent.

Evidence

Three separate accounts converge on the same reading: not one bad apple, but conditions that let it spread.

The piece walks through the individual, the leadership, and the institution in turn.

ACCOUNT A
The individual of interest
Having never gotten spotlight, she may indeed feel a sense of injustice — an emotional response linked to a perception of being overlooked or undervalued, fueling dissatisfaction.
ACCOUNT B
The project manager
While possibly not performing optimally as a leader, is part of an environment where growth and learning are encouraged rather than blame being assigned, aligning with the broader goal of developing leadership skills.
ACCOUNT C
The institution
Provided consistent and clear leadership, doing its best to meet the group's expectations for structure and conscientiousness — communication was open and accessible, with the team always reachable.

Implications

Fairness was evident — bonuses, treats, digital promotions — yet individualized attention was still missing.

Leadership development is influenced by factors beyond the immediate classroom.

Family upbringing plays a crucial role, as do the shared cultural assumptions that shape leadership expectations within a nation or generation. While guidance and learning opportunities can be provided in class, the deeper aspects of leadership behavior and, more importantly, sense-making often rest on these foundational influences.

Fairness has also been evident, with many students receiving tangible rewards such as monetary bonuses, treats, and digital promotions. These efforts show that the institution has actively acknowledged and rewarded performance. The challenge of managing influential individuals within a group is understood, and while it's recognized that individual management isn't always feasible, it remains a task that can be addressed when necessary.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

A "bad apple" does not just spoil itself — it can lead to the rotting of the entire basket if the conditions allow for it.

One area for potential improvement stands out above the rest.

A "bad apple" or a person prone to emotional instability can indeed influence an entire group, but it is the interaction between individual vulnerabilities and the group's overall traits that creates the real disruption. High Agreeableness made the group more susceptible to aligning with the emotional volatility of a single individual, turning what could have been a contained issue into a larger collective action.

One area for potential improvement is the implementation of one-on-one or small-group mentoring sessions. This approach, used in institutions like ITB and other programs, isn't a luxury but a necessity. Had this been incorporated earlier, it might have offered a more direct way to address concerns, guide leadership development, and prevent the collective frustrations that led to the disruption.
