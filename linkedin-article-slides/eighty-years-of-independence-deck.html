🇮🇩 Eighty Years of Independence

Written almost entirely as questions, and deliberately so. Each colonial arrangement is set beside a present-day one — the Cultivation System beside global value chains, priyayi schooling beside curriculum borrowing, the batig slot beside debt service — and the reader is left to decide whether the shape has changed. The piece answers none of them, and that refusal is the argument.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

Pride is real, and the questions underneath it are still uncomfortable.

A message on Independence Day morning, from a respected friend who is not Indonesian.

Situation

"I'm genuinely grateful to be born in an independent country, recognized by the United Nations as a sovereign state. That pride is real."

Complication

"I was touched—then I stopped and thought" — who gets the value when nickel, palm oil and coal leave our shores; whose knowledge defines good schooling; what reduces stunting faster than free meals for all students; and whether meritocracy and access look any different from the old gatekeeping.

Question

Can a country be genuinely independent while the structures that shape its value, its curriculum and its opportunity are still set elsewhere?

Answer

The article does not say. "If these questions make you a bit uncomfortable, that's the point. We can be proud and still be honest."

The Method

Every colonial arrangement in the piece is matched to a present-day one.

The article is built as six chapters of paired questions. Almost nothing in it is asserted.

Chapter one asks what colonialism was here: whether Javanese peasants were pressed into the Cultivation System to grow sugar, coffee and indigo for export; whether the batig slot — the colonial profit remitted to the metropole — funded canals, railways and Dutch public finances while Java bore the costs; whether schooling was a narrow gate reserved for elites and priyayi; whether access to office was filtered by birth and patronage rather than open competition.

Chapter two puts the same questions in the present tense. "If formal empire ended, has domination vanished—or has it shifted into trade rules, debt, and corporate control"? "When debt service constrains budgets more than domestic priorities, is that not tribute by contract rather than by colonial ledger"?

Each pairing is supported by citations — Fasseur, Elson and van Zanden on the colonial economy; the World Bank and Gereffi on value chains; Stiglitz and Robison & Hadiz on conditionality. The evidence is named. The conclusion is left to the reader.

Value

Raw material leaves; higher-value goods return; value capture stays elsewhere.

"When raw resources flow out and higher-value goods flow in, are we not living inside global value chains that keep value capture elsewhere"?

On nickel: whether "the backbone of EV batteries" is "still leaving mostly as material while design and branding profits cluster abroad". On palm oil: whether pricing and branding are "shaped up-chain by multinationals while smallholders sit on the thinnest margins". And on coal: "Is coal exported in vast tonnage while environmental costs remain local"?

On Papua: "When a major foreign mining house held Papua's copper-gold giant for decades, did Indonesia retain only a fraction of value compared with what was removed"? The question is put with citations to Ballard & Banks and to Leith, and left standing.

"Did crisis-era conditionality in 1997–1998 tilt policy toward external priorities more than domestic industrial aims"? Here too the sources are given and the verdict withheld.

Knowledge

Curriculum borrowing carries hidden assumptions about whose knowledge counts.

The chapter that asks whether policy borrowing is neo-colonialism by another name.

Then
•Schooling largely reserved for elites and priyayi
•A narrow gate producing a loyal administrative class rather than broad opportunity
•Merit effectively filtered by birth and patronage
•Reforms designed and controlled from above — "charity without power transfer"
•Whose knowledge counted was settled outside the classroom
Now
•Frameworks and language lifted from the US and Europe — "models that travel with hidden assumptions about “what works”"
•"If global 21st-century skills packages look strikingly similar across countries, does that reflect real local fit"?
•"When PISA/OECD comparisons become the yardstick, do they not pull systems toward one template"?
•"If the promises are ambitious but teacher capacity, support, and school conditions are uneven, are we not seeing a template that outruns context"?
•"So if “curriculum import” sidelines Indonesian epistemologies and classroom realities, does it echo colonial logics of whose knowledge counts—even without flags and soldiers"?

Nutrition

Stunting falls fastest in the first thousand days, not with universal meals.

The one place where the article's questions rest on a hard and specific evidence base.

"If stunting drops most when we focus on the first 1,000 days (pregnancy to age two), should targeting put pregnant women and under-twos first—before universal meals for all students"? The citations behind that window are Black et al. and Victora et al. in The Lancet, Martorell on the first 1,000 days, and Hoddinott on the adult consequences of early-life growth failure.

"Could large, centralized food supply chains create procurement leakages unless designed with transparency and local oversight"? "Wouldn't local kitchens, community monitoring, and simple, auditable flows preserve dignity and impact—so help arrives without reproducing top-down paternalism"?

"And in the long run, wouldn't jobs and incomes for parents deliver the dignity everyone wants, while nutrition support narrows tightly to the evidence-critical window"? The policy question is put as a question; the evidence for the window is not.

The Warning

Nkrumah warned in 1965 that dependence survives the departure of troops.

The final chapter reaches for the one authority the piece treats as having already answered.

"Was Kwame Nkrumah already right in 1965 when he warned that neo-colonialism means no troops on the ground, but dependence all the same"?

Even here the form holds: the claim arrives as a question about whether someone else was right, not as an assertion that he was. The article will not make the statement in its own voice.

What follows is not a conclusion but two more questions — and they are the last words of the piece.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

The piece ends without answering, and that refusal is the argument.

Eighty years, six chapters, fifty-five references, and no verdict offered anywhere.

So, after 80 years of independence, what kind of freedom do we truly have? And what kind of freedom do we still need to build?

— 🇮🇩 Eighty Years of Independence
Why the form is the content
An essay that answered these questions would ask the reader to accept a verdict. An essay that only pairs them — Cultivation System beside value chain, priyayi school beside PISA benchmark — asks the reader to notice a shape and decide for themselves whether it has changed. The second is harder to dismiss.
What it refuses to give up
Not the pride. "We can be proud and still be honest." The piece is written on Independence Day by someone grateful to have been born in an independent country, and it treats the gratitude and the discomfort as compatible rather than competing.
How this deck handles the form
The section titles above assert what the article establishes by its method — that the pairings are constructed, that the evidence is named, that the questions go unanswered. They do not answer the questions on the author's behalf. Where the article asks, this deck asks.
