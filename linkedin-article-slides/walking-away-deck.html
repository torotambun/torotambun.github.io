Walking Away

Three restaurants on one Sunday afternoon left responsibility technically allocated but relationally unavailable. That finding survives. What does not survive is my standing as its narrator: I demanded recognition from organisations while barely recognising the person who actually gave it to me.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

I stood at a counter that was no longer where an order began.

An autoethnography of three ordinary restaurant encounters, and of the older professional who could not read the rooms he entered.

Situation

I stood in front of the Burger King counter for five to seven minutes before understanding that it was no longer where an order began. The counter was still there, with illuminated menu boards above it. Two employees moved around the kitchen while a woman in a dark uniform pushed a mop slowly across the dining-room floor. "The restaurant was operating, but the room did not tell me how to enter its operation."

Complication

I did not call out. I did not walk towards the kiosk. "I stood where an earlier Burger King had taught me to stand and expected the room to recognise that posture." The woman with the mop put her bucket aside, walked to the ordering machine, and gestured. I understood. I thanked her too quickly, did not look again at the menu, and left without ordering.

Question

I had entered wanting a Double Whopper and left with nothing, embarrassed and unable to name why. What had actually been threatened at that counter?

Answer

Not the technology. What the afternoon exposed was a demand for recognition I had never examined — and the fact that I was least able to see the one person in the room who had been noticing me all along.

The Afternoon

Three restaurants made responsibility hard to locate in three different ways.

Subway first, then Burger King, then Pizza Hut. By the third stop I was looking for more than lunch.

At Subway, a server greeted us and I shifted my weight towards the counter, assuming the order was about to begin. She returned almost immediately to the kitchen, leaving no one in front of us. "The exchange had opened and then lost the person who seemed to own it." I did not ask whether someone else would continue.

At Burger King I carried that unfinished encounter into the next one, and read the unattended counter not as a new ambiguity but as another refusal to tell me what came next. At Pizza Hut we paid and waited twenty-five minutes. The duration itself was not unreasonable; pizza requires preparation and cooking. "What troubled me was that no one gave the waiting a shape."

The salad bar still stood on its island in the middle of the room, but the colour and movement had gone. I watched the nervous manager photographing a brochure, the waiter with nothing visible to do, a Middle Eastern family who talked and talked and were still talking when I left, and the woman preparing pizzas without hurry. "Instead, I found myself watching her movements and turning them into evidence." I could have asked how much longer the order would take. I did not.

The Body Decided First

"Only later did I understand that my body had reached a decision before my explanation did. I shifted my weight towards counters, watched faces, thanked too quickly, avoided the screen, and finally walked away." Spry (2001) treats the body not as a passive container for thought: discomfort, gesture, movement and withdrawal can disclose cultural knowledge before it becomes available as argument.

The Younger Men

Each brand held a younger version of me, and none of the workers knew it.

Only while writing did I recognise that the three restaurants had never existed in my memory simply as brands.

1  ·  Burger King, Singapore, May 1988 — the teenager representing Indonesia
I was seventeen, at the Singapore Youth Science Fortnight, staying at York Hotel above Orchard Road. Each introduction began with the same sentence: "I am from Indonesia." New Zealand students invited me to walk downhill to the Burger King near Wheelock Plaza. Some became pen pals whose handwriting I learned to recognise before opening the envelopes. "Burger King therefore carried more than a meal. It carried mobility: the possibility that an Indonesian teenager could enter an international room, speak his country's name, and be received without having to prove that he belonged there."
2  ·  Pizza Hut, Yogyakarta, after 1989 — the student refused as overqualified
My father introduced me to the franchise owner, who asked the operations manager at the outlet near Tugu to interview me for a part-time position. The interview went well. After speaking with the owner, the manager returned with a different decision: they considered me overqualified. "What I remember as an individual attempt at independence was never independent. My father's standing opened the interview, and another older man's judgement closed it."
3  ·  Subway, Kuningan, 2001 — the new sales manager at WesternGeco
My boss took me to the branch near Plaza Festival. The ordering process fascinated me because the sandwich was assembled in front of the customer, the employee remaining present while each answer changed the final object. "It seemed to stage a small form of participation: the procedure was standardised, but my answers still mattered."
4  ·  The burden was unfair to the restaurants
"No contemporary employee could know that Burger King contained my experience of representing Indonesia, that Subway marked my first years at WesternGeco, or that Pizza Hut still carried my father, the franchise owner, the operations manager, and a job I never held. I brought a private history into public establishments and interpreted ordinary workers through memories they had no part in making."

The First Explanation Fails

The machine was never the problem; legibility was.

A robotic coffee system at a Singtel office near Killiney Road contradicted my own account of myself.

A metal arm positioned the cup, prepared the espresso, and presented it without a barista. I enjoyed the coffee because I knew where to begin and what would happen next. "The machine was not the problem. Legibility was. At the robotic coffee system, the steps received me." Burger King had offered no such clarity.

The architecture and the organisation were telling different stories. To me, the counter still announced: begin here. To the kitchen staff, perhaps, it announced nothing — perhaps their work started only after an order appeared on the kitchen screen, and the counter marked only the edge of the kitchen. "The machine had neither rejected me nor malfunctioned. Once she showed me where to begin, I could have completed the order within minutes."

"I had never thought of myself as someone who resisted technology." My professional life had involved systems, innovation, and teaching students that organisations must adapt. "Yet when adaptation required me to become the uncertain newcomer, I walked away."

Artefacts Travel Faster Than Assumptions

Schein (2009) separates the visible artefacts of an organisation — counters, kiosks, uniforms, scripts, procedures — from its espoused values and its deeper assumptions about authority, judgement and whose action is trusted. "A kiosk may signal technological modernisation without producing a shared assumption that someone remains answerable when a customer cannot understand the system."

What the Writing Removed

Each revision removed one defence and exposed another.

Wall (2008) describes the difficulty of autoethnographic writing as more than the difficulty of remembering events. The writing changes the writer's relationship to what happened.

The Explanations I Wrote
✗Automation caused it — an impersonal kiosk replaced a person
✗Responsibility was fragmented across screens, stations and roles
✗The multinationals failed to transmit the organisational culture they represented
The Version of Me Each One Protected
•The competent customer, wronged by an impersonal system
•The organisational critic, able to identify fragmented responsibility
•The moderniser, who believed imported corporate culture could repair habits belonging to Indonesia rather than to himself
Where the Faith Was

"Writing that sentence embarrasses me. It assumes that imported organisations possessed virtues local institutions lacked. It turns “Indonesia” into a culture waiting to be corrected and allows me to speak as though I had escaped the habits I criticised." I had not escaped them.

The Reversal

The person who had been noticing me was the one I had barely been watching.

What her action reveals with greatest certainty is not her character or the organisation's culture. It reveals how I ranked the room.

OBSERVED A
The hierarchy briefly reversed
"I thought I was waiting to be noticed by the “important” people behind the counter." The employees behind the counter occupied what I read as the formal centre of the restaurant; she moved along its edge. When she pointed towards the kiosk, the hierarchy through which I had read the room briefly reversed.
OBSERVED B
And my sentence restored it
"Even now, however, I identify her through the mop and the work I saw her performing, while the others remain “employees.” My language restores the ranking her action unsettled. I criticise organisations for placing judgement low in the hierarchy, then reproduce that hierarchy in the sentence."
OBSERVED C
Praise can still appropriate
"I made her gesture evidence that judgement could emerge from the organisational margin. I made her prove that the corporation had failed to recognise leadership. I made her carry a national argument about hierarchy and cultural change." She did not consent to any of those meanings. I can describe what her gesture did to me, but not what it meant to her (Adams, 2006).
OBSERVED D
The Subway server may have been answerable elsewhere
I had seen only two employees running the establishment. "I stood waiting for one person to remain answerable to me while she may have been answerable to the bread, the kitchen, another customer, the register, and the unfinished work behind her." I still felt disappointed. Empathy entered the memory beside the disappointment.

What It Revealed

I wanted initiative, but I also wanted to remain the person who recognised it.

The restaurants did not create that contradiction. They made it visible.

"I had criticised people who waited for authority, but I had also benefited from being the authority they watched. I encouraged initiative, but not always when it contradicted my judgement. I wanted younger people to act independently while continuing to receive the respect attached to age, education, and professional position."

In many local restaurants — Ayam Goreng Mbok Berek, Ayam Goreng Ny. Suharti, a warung Padang in Pasar Senen, Lapo Ni Tondong Ta in Pramuka, Tino'or Manadonese Restaurant, New Cahaya Lestari in Kelapa Gading, an ordinary warung Tegal — the boundaries of a job were often difficult to see. The person who greeted me might take the order, carry the food, refill a glass, pack leftovers and remain nearby. "They moved towards whatever the encounter required." I do not romanticise this: the language of family can make exploitation difficult to name, and long service may reflect dependence as much as belonging.

The uncertainty follows me into teaching. I can arrange a syllabus, platform, timetable, assessment criteria and materials until the path appears obvious to me. "Yet a student may still stand before the course as I stood before the Burger King counter: surrounded by signs, uncertain where to begin, reluctant to reveal confusion, waiting for a person to look up."

Answerability

"I use answerability to name more than task ownership. It is the felt possibility that someone will recognise an interruption and remain connected to what happens next." The three restaurants disturbed me because responsibility appeared technically allocated but relationally unavailable — and the inquiry also exposes the entitlement inside that expectation.

What I Carry

Recognition is something people demand, withhold, and mistake for proof of their place.

The cultural contribution lies in holding two claims together rather than resolving them into one.

Organisational systems can distribute tasks so efficiently that recognition becomes difficult to locate. At the same time, people who experience themselves as overlooked may enter such systems carrying status, nostalgia, pride, and unexamined expectations about who should notice them.

— The article's own statement of its contribution
Both statements survive
"I can still say that the three encounters lacked answerability. I can also say that I entered them carrying an ageing body, professional pride, memories of mobility, and a belief that multinational corporations represented a cultural future." I wanted food. I also wanted recognition from organisations that had become part of how I recognised myself.
No stable lesson is available
Bochner (2014) and Bochner and Ellis (2016) resist the demand that narrative inquiry close experience into a stable lesson. "I therefore cannot conclude that the restaurants failed, that the kiosk caused alienation, or that Indonesian hierarchy explains what occurred. Each statement contains part of the afternoon and protects me from another part."
The question turned back on me
"Sometimes I imagine a student standing before one of my carefully designed courses, reading the instructions I believed were clear. I do not know whether I looked up."
The Closing Lines

"The woman with the mop looked up. She pointed towards the kiosk. I understood. I left." … "The counter remains, brightly lit and empty."
