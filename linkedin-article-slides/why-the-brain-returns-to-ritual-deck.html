(2 of 5) Why the Brain Returns to Ritual — A Series: Why I borrow the ancient prayer?

After retirement the body returned to the earth's rhythm, and spontaneous prayer stopped carrying the solemnity it once had. What was needed was not sincerity but structure — the texture of liturgy. The ancient text was already in muscle memory, and old notes led back further still, to the Amidah. Set side by side, the two prayers share reverence, provision and forgiveness but not form: Jesus does not condense eighteen blessings. He reconfigures the logic entirely.

Opening

Retirement returned the body to the earth's rhythm, and the old words stopped working.

"Long after I retired, my biological clock gradually returned to something resembling normal."

Situation

"My body now responds to the rhythms of the earth, typically growing tired around 2 a.m.—a marked improvement from the earlier pattern of fatigue only arriving at 4 or 5. Being alone at the wee hours, and often feeling alienated in this country, I’ve felt a quiet pull toward higher ground—a longing for transcendence."

Complication

"Unlike when I was a child—when I could pray fervently for hours—I now found my words insufficient. Sixteen years ago, I had shifted my vocabulary of gratitude to speak of "the universe," but recently I’ve realized something was missing. My spontaneous expressions no longer carried the weight of solemnity I sought."

Question

If sincerity was not the missing element, what was?

Answer

"I needed a return to structure. I needed the texture of liturgy."

The Return

The prayer was already in muscle memory, and the notes led further back.

"So, I turned to the ancient text embedded in my muscle memory" — the Lord's Prayer, recited in full in the article.

"Later, I revisited my old notes—records from a period when I had explored the Lord’s Prayer line by line using hermeneutic methods, drawing on various theological texts. That study led me back even further, to its roots in Jewish prayer practice—specifically, the Amidah, also known as the Shemoneh Esrei."

"The Shemoneh Esrei (The Amidah – standing) is a central element of Jewish liturgy, originally comprising eighteen blessings, later expanded to nineteen. It is traditionally recited three times a day and dates back to the Second Temple period in Jerusalem (516 BCE to 70 CE)."

"The formalization of the Amidah is often credited to the Men of the Great Assembly (Anshei Knesset HaGedolah), a group of Jewish sages and prophets active during the post-exilic restoration. The text likely reached its final form during the early Rabbinic era, after the destruction of the Second Temple."

"The Lord’s Prayer, by contrast, is recorded in the Gospels of Matthew (6:9–13) and Luke (11:2–4) and is attributed directly to Jesus." "While shorter, it carries a structural logic similar to the Amidah—framing praise, requests, repentance, protection, and ultimately, submission to divine order."

The Formation

Training in five philosophies left an appetite for architecture.

The reason the return to liturgy took this particular form is methodological, and the article says so plainly.

"Having been trained originally in positivism, but later shaped by interpretivism, critical realism, pragmatism, and post-modernism, I’ve developed a strong appreciation for structure."

"Both the Amidah and the Lord’s Prayer offer just that: a clear architecture of thought grounded in reverence, repentance, and petition."

"Once I mapped the prayer to its original, perhaps this is how my brain was wired after the myelination—I could connect more deeply and pray with greater authenticity."

The claim is unusual and worth holding onto: authenticity here is not the opposite of form. It arrives through form. A mind trained across five research paradigms could not find its way into unstructured speech, and recovered access only once the prayer's architecture was made visible.

The Comparison

The Amidah and the Lord's Prayer share themes but not form.

The article maps the two prayers against each other and then, more usefully, lists what does not map.

Themes the Lord's Prayer carries over
✓Reverence — praise before petition
✓Provision — the request for sustenance
✓Forgiveness — repentance built into the structure
✓Protection — the plea for guidance and deliverance
✓Submission to divine order as the closing posture
Amidah blessings it does not address
✗Communal identity
✗National restoration
✗Healing
✗Judicial righteousness
✗"dimensions the Lord’s Prayer does not explicitly address"
Why the omissions are the interesting half

Four of the Amidah's concerns are corporate and national. Their absence is what makes the shorter prayer portable: nothing in it requires a particular people, a particular land, or a particular court. That is the precondition for the access the next instalment takes up.

What It Shows

Jesus does not condense the Amidah; he reconfigures it.

"While the Lord’s Prayer echoes several themes found in the Amidah—such as reverence, provision, and forgiveness—its form is strikingly different."

Its power lies not in summarizing Jewish prayer, but in reshaping its logic for direct, inclusive, daily use.

— (2 of 5) Why the Brain Returns to Ritual
The move that is not abbreviation
"Jesus does not condense the Amidah’s eighteen blessings; he reconfigures the structure entirely. Rather than enumerate petitions, he delivers a compact prayer aligned with his own theological priorities: relational access, kingdom orientation, and moral responsibility."
What the piece asks of the reader
"In these challenging times, it's crucial to find moments of mindfulness and peace. By reflecting on our connections and the support we receive, we can cultivate empathy toward those facing difficult circumstances." "Let's be grateful for every little thing, from the kindness of friends to the lessons learned from adversity."
Where the series goes next
Three priorities are named here — relational access, kingdom orientation, moral responsibility — and the first of them becomes the whole subject of the next instalment. The word carrying it is Abba.
