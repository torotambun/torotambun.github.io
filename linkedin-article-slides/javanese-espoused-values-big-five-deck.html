LinkedIn Article  ·  Culture · Javanese Sayings and the Entrepreneurial Personality  ·  26 February 2023

Javanese' Espoused Values on Openness, Conscientiousness, Agreeableness and Emotionally-stableness

The article starts from recruitment and the Big Five profile of entrepreneurs, reports the author's own finding that few of the proto-entrepreneurs he recruited become innovation-driven entrepreneurs, and asks how innovation values are planted early enough to matter. It then sets thirty Javanese sayings, with the author's own English glosses, under four traits: openness, conscientiousness, low agreeableness and emotional stability. It closes on the point that espoused values are not enough to mobilise a country, and that turning them into basic values runs through the family, the school and the peer group and takes generations.

Introduction

Entrepreneurs score higher on openness and conscientiousness, and the author's own recruiting finds few proto-entrepreneurs.

Situation

The article opens on recruitment: "Recruitment is the key to having a strong corporate-fit culture". Accelerators, incubators and bootcamps are said to use tools such as Strengthfinder and the Big Five personality test, with an individual interview, to predict entrepreneurial success.

Complication

On the Big Five, entrepreneurs are said to score significantly higher than managers on openness to experience and conscientiousness, significantly lower on agreeableness and neuroticism, and no differently on extraversion. The author's four years of recruiting and co-learning with proto-entrepreneurs found "only less than 2% end up becoming innovation-driven entrepreneurs (IDE) and 5% choose to become Small Medium Enterprise entrepreneurs".

Question

How many proto-entrepreneurs are there to recruit, and what would make more of them possible?

Answer

Few, and the article accepts it, calling its figures "unpleasant numbers and brutal facts". It also reports that "80% of them graduate with having more entrepreneurial thinking than they were before", and that with no recruitment criteria the population would be split "93% who work in their linear industry of study, 6% wish to enter government-back companies and only 1% wish to be entrepreneurs or others".

Who the numbers are aimed at

The article cautions those who announce goals of hundreds of thousands of entrepreneurs a year in a province, or thousands in a large university, to learn from Admiral Stockdale on facing the most brutal facts of current reality.

Evidence note

The percentages come from the author's own experiments. The article gives no sample size or method, and cites no source for the Big Five comparison of entrepreneurs and managers or for its figure that about 2.5 percent of Z and millennial students at state universities are pro-entrepreneurs.

Gap

Skills that need an early start cannot be recruited late, so the author turns to how innovation values are planted.

The article compares entrepreneurship with the performing arts and sport, such as singing, piano, violin, ballet, painting, badminton, tennis, golf, boxing, gymnastics and swimming, where training starts very early through a long, stringent curriculum that creates a neuron pathway for the rest of a person's life.

It offers a shortcut: a robust learning process of one to four years, with a curriculum suited to the purpose, in the right ecosystem, taught by passionate and expert teachers, which it says normally creates a mass of ready-to-go people in a certain industry. It then asks what makes entrepreneur creation different.

The author says most of his work has focused on the later part of the process, and turns to the earlier part: "I become increasingly curious about the beginning of the process that lies in the planting of innovation culture as basic values not just espoused values". He adds that "The process of converting espoused values into basic values requires a lot of experiment and long-term commitment from all stakeholders", and cites an African proverb, "It takes a whole village to raise a kid".

His hope rests on the sayings that follow: "My hope sparkles when I learn there are innovation espoused values in my countries", tied to openness, high conscientiousness, low agreeableness and low neuroticism. He limits his reading to Javanese culture because, in his words, it holds "the most protrude and prevalent values shared in Nusantara".

Details

Thirteen Javanese sayings express openness to experience.

The article prints each saying in Javanese with its own English gloss, and the deck follows the glosses.

1  ·  Gurung bengawan weteng segara
Willing to accommodate any criticism or aspiration from all walks of life.
2  ·  Manjing ajur-ajer
Always flexible, adapting to every condition and situation.
3  ·  Ora rumongso bisa, nanging bisa rumongso
Feeling like the least able in the room, so that there is always room to learn hungrily.
4  ·  Manunggaling kawula gusti
Keeping vision and mission in line with the aspiration of the team, the people or the organisation.
5  ·  Empan papan
Keeping four kinds of awareness: of surroundings and place, of attitude and behaviour, of role, and of change through time.
6  ·  Nglurug tanpa bala, menang tanpa ngasorake
Not relying on one frontal tactic, and weighing many tactics around love, passion and the welfare of the people.
7  ·  Mikul dhuwur mendhem jero
Always remembering the teachings, wisdom and graciousness of predecessors.
8  ·  Ojo dumeh, tepa seliro, ngerti kuawalat
Aware of one's position, never concluding to know everything, considerate toward others so as to learn most, and aware of living in a larger complex system.
9  ·  Anak polah bapa kepradhah
Supporting the cause of the team and showing commitment to it.
10  ·  Sepi ing pamrih, rame ing nggawe
Completing tasks responsibly for the greater cause of improving people's lives, without short-sighted motives of fame or money.
11  ·  Wani ngalah luhur wekasane
Willing to step back after a stagnant or temporary defeat, so as to contemplate failures and weaknesses and learn best practice from the surroundings.
12  ·  Tepa seliro
Introspecting, and learning continuously from oneself and from one's ecosystem.
13  ·  Nyebar wiji sederakan lan wiji utamining kejawen ing manca negari
Striving for a remarkably high networking density and welcoming new networking opportunities.
Evidence note

The sayings appear with the author's own glosses and no textual source. The article reports no study linking them to Big Five scores, so the match to the four traits rests on the author's reading.

Details

Seven sayings express conscientiousness, and two of them tie haste to unexamined risk.

The last two sayings in the group carry the article's risk argument, and the second is set against the reputation of being slow.

1  ·  Landhep panggraitane
Intelligence, good sense-making and a sharp mental model for the problems faced.
2  ·  Tan angkah marang keutamaan
Always striving to comply with wisdom.
3  ·  Gemi, taberi nastiti, ngati-ati
Thrifty, structured, organised, careful and compliant, conscientious and not wildly risk-taking.
4  ·  Ajining diri soko pucuke lathi, ajining raga soko busana
Careful with the choice of words, reflected in outward expression: fashion, manner and gesture.
5  ·  Ngilo githoke dhewe
Projecting the impact of one's acts on others, and being introspective.
6  ·  Kebat kliwat, ngangsa marakake brahala
Not hurrying in deciding and acting, and calculating the risk comprehensively before committing.
7  ·  Alon-alon asal kelakon
Read against the misconception that it means being slow and laggard, it says the same as the saying before it: analyse the risk and provide a route of options and feedback.

Details

Five sayings express low agreeableness and five express emotional stability.

The article reads low agreeableness as holding to principle, and emotional stability as patience, persistence and self-control.

Low agreeableness
•Tan ban cindhe ban siladan: not discriminating, and upholding law and principle inclusively, even against family, friends and networks
•Ora gumunan, ora kagetan, lan ora sumuk: not carried away by ideas too easily, and not reacting too swiftly
•Ngeli neng ora keli: receptive yet not carried away, especially on principle or reasons
•Dede tekad pamrih, nanging tekad asih: principled, pivoting on love and compassion, and not selfishly asking what is in it for oneself
•Becik ketitik ala ketaro: upholding ethics and integrity at all costs, because reputation matters
High emotional stability
•Neng, ning, nung, nang: mindful in acts, peaceful in the making, self-controlled, keeping one's reason for being
•Sabar subur: patience is a virtue
•Yen kepergok ing pacoban aja lumaris: never giving up and never quitting
•Rukun agawe sentosa, crah agawe bubrah: pursuing happiness in the work, and not an objective that sacrifices the morale and mental health of the team
•Wani mengku, antebing ati, kencenging piker, boboting kekuatane: strong conviction and determination, in harmony with what one contains

Conclusion

Espoused values are not enough to move a country, and turning them into basic values takes generations.

The final section, The Question, calls the sayings noble, says they must become automatic in each citizen, and asks whether the country has what it takes to make them so.

Without having the right basic values, a culture may vanish (more subtly integrated as inferior subculture) when confronted with other more inundated cultures.

— Javanese' Espoused Values, 26 February 2023
Basic values, not espoused values
The article says "having espoused values is not enough to mobilize a country to reach a greater cause", and that the sayings must become muscle memory, mental model and system thinking in each citizen.
Where basic values form
"It starts at the nucleus family, expanded into the schools and peers", and the exposures of a citizen's adolescent days are said to decide what the brain myelinates afterwards.
The questions it asks
How parenting systems work, what the educational model and practice are, how role models pass on their values, and whether the country has the right sub-culture factory or machine.
The comparison it draws
Japan, China, Korea and Singapore, and a fifth country printed only as United, are each said to have one. Investing in human capital is said to be their theme, and it takes generations with no shortcut.
