LinkedIn Article  ·  Entrepreneurship · Valuation, Paying Customers and Venture Funds  ·  27 September 2019

For Start-Ups: Don't get owned by your valuation. Focus on your paying customers, how you address their pain and the target addressable market.

The article says valuation is an excitement that a classroom simulation can produce from nothing: a pair of eyeglasses worth $3,000 reached a $100 million valuation in twenty minutes. Start-ups should therefore focus on adding value, on paying customers and on the pain the product addresses, bootstrap first, and then engage angels and compatible venture capitalists. It cites a Kauffman Foundation report in which 66 of 88 venture funds missed expected returns, and lists the four steps the foundation takes to mitigate the risk.

Introduction

Valuation is an excitement, so start-ups should not be owned by it and should focus on paying customers and their pain.

Situation

In each valuation class the author runs a simulation of a supposedly hot start-up. The object is his own 2011 limited-edition Cartier Panther pair of eyeglasses, which he pitches at a $100,000 valuation.

Complication

Within 20 minutes the pair changed hands more than 10 times and reached a $100 million valuation. The article says this shows how irrational behaviour prevails in the industry. Separately it says greed, a survival need embedded in the brain, leads to many disasters and unhappiness through irrationality.

Question

How should a start-up treat valuation when excitement alone can raise it?

Answer

The article's advice to students is not to get owned by valuation excitement and euphoria. They are to add and create value in the product, service or offering, focus on paying customers and the pain addressed, and aim at this from day 1.

Evidence

A $3,000 pair of eyeglasses reached a $100 million valuation in twenty minutes and was still worth $3,000.

SIMULATION A
The pitch
In a valuation class the author pitched a pair of glasses valued at $3,000 at a $100,000 valuation, as a supposedly hot start-up.
SIMULATION B
Twenty minutes
Within 20 minutes the pair had changed hands more than 10 times and reached a $100 million valuation, while the class was noisy with the excitement of valuing it.
SIMULATION C
The reconvene
Asked what the glasses were worth, most people quickly said $100 million. After a while they realised the glasses were the same and still worth $3,000, or less through wear and tear.
SIMULATION D
The lesson
The pitching, the bells and whistles and the excitement made the glasses seem worth $100 million although they were the same glasses as at the start. The simulation is meant to make students aware not to get owned by valuation excitement and euphoria.
Evidence note

The exercise is described from the author's own account, with no record of class sizes or runs. The remark on greed as a survival need embedded in the brain has no source.

Framework

Focus on paying customers, bootstrap first, then engage angels and compatible VCs, who profit when the company profits.

01
Focus
Paying customers
Focus on paying customers and iterate the product, service or offering to address their pains: "do the right thing right in the first time". Do not wrap up an unfinished product and chase investors who either eat the founders up, if they are good, or quickly say no.
02
Fund
Bootstrap first
Bootstrap as much as possible, then engage fit angel investors and compatible VCs. A bare link to a piece on business angels follows the framework, with no comment on it.
03
Choose
Compatible VCs
Compatible VCs like to see the company they invest in making a profit, so they profit from the carry and not only from the fees. The article says many exercise the 2-20 but focus on the 2 and not the 20.

Evidence

In the Kauffman sample, 66 of 88 venture funds missed expected returns, and 69 funds, 78 percent, fell short after fees and carry.

REPORTED A
The first result
Of 88 venture funds in the sample, 66 failed to deliver expected venture rates of return in the first 27 months, before serial fundraises.
REPORTED B
After fees and carry
The cumulative effect of fees, carry and the uneven nature of venture investing left 69 funds, 78 percent, with returns not sufficient to reward patient, expensive, long-term investing.
REPORTED C
The misalignment
The report says limited partners do not pay VCs to beat the public market. VCs are typically paid a 2 percent management fee on committed capital and a 20 percent profit share, which pays more for raising bigger funds and can lock in fee-based income even when the general partner fails to return investor capital.
REPORTED D
The black box
The report says limited partners regularly accept the risks of a black box of VC firm economics: institutional investors commonly invest in VC funds without information on general partner compensation, carry structure, ownership, or firm-level income, expenses or profits.
Evidence note

The report as quoted describes the fee structure and results for its own sample of 88 funds. The wider statement that many VCs focus on the 2 and not the 20 is the author's reading. The article gives no author or year for the report.

Details

Kauffman's own response is smaller funds, direct investment, co-investment and a shift toward public markets.

The article introduces these as what the Kauffman Foundation does to mitigate the risk of dealing with VCs. They are the foundation's steps as an investor and not advice to founders.

1  ·  Smaller funds with a record
Invest in VC funds of less than $400 million with a history of consistently high public market equivalent (PME) performance, in which the general partners commit at least 5 percent of capital.
2  ·  Direct investment
Invest directly in a small portfolio of new companies, without being saddled by high fees and carry.
3  ·  Co-investment
Co-invest in later-round deals side by side with seasoned investors.
4  ·  Public markets
Move a portion of the capital invested in VC into the public markets. The report says there are not enough strong VC investors with above-market returns to absorb even the foundation's limited investment capital.
