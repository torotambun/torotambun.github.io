Why Climate Simulation in an Entrepreneurship Class?

A climate model belongs in an entrepreneurship bootcamp because of what the negotiation does, not what the model outputs. Eight stakeholder roles turn conflict into structure rather than blame, and the temperature number moves only when groups combine solutions. The class's own result is the argument: the ambitious groups reached for 1.5°C, and the group that had to reconcile everyone landed at 2.0°C.

Introduction

An entrepreneurship bootcamp is an unlikely place to run a climate model, which is the reason to run one there.

Situation

"experiential learning environments such as Entrepreneurial Bootcamp EL-4062 are increasingly recognized for their role in preparing future leaders"

Complication

These platforms are "often underestimated in their capacity to engage students in real-world problem-solving"

Question

What does a climate simulation teach that an entrepreneurship syllabus does not?

Answer

"The transformation begins with a simple premise: this workshop will be different." The design is "a blame-free, multi-solving environment where equity and diverse stakeholder perspectives are central".

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

Three questions replace blame, and each one is a systems question rather than a moral one.

En-ROADS, developed by Climate Interactive and the MIT Sloan Sustainability Initiative, "invites a mindset grounded in collaborative thinking over adversarial framing."

01
Interdependence
How does solving my problem help solve yours?
The article's gloss: "It nurtures a mindset of interconnected action." The question makes another party's constraint part of your own solution rather than an obstacle to it.
02
Inclusion
Who needs to be here?
"This emphasizes the need for broad stakeholder inclusion, reinforcing the importance of listening to voices across boundaries."
03
Connection
How can we strengthen the connections between us?
"The simulation promotes synergy across sectors—breaking silos between roles, disciplines, and organizations."
What the tool refuses to supply

"Rather than suggesting a one-size-fits-all solution, the simulation demonstrates that addressing climate change is a collaborative process—more akin to planting a garden than solving an equation." The framing is Elizabeth Sawin's multi-solving approach, and the article repeats her terms: "it takes many seeds to plant a garden," "no magical fix," "no one big solution," "no panacea".

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Design

Eight roles make the tensions structural, so no group can be the villain of the exercise.

"To simulate real-world negotiations, participants are divided into stakeholder groups: Climate Justice Advocates, Industrialists, Automobile Sector Representatives, Conventional Energy Producers, Technology and Innovation Startups, Agriculture and Land Use Sector, Renewable Energy Leaders, and Government Policymakers."

"These roles offer insight into the diverse priorities and limitations of each group, helping students understand systemic tensions while envisioning points of collaboration."

The design deliberately removes the register students arrive with. It "moves beyond the dichotomy of “most righteous” or “most at fault” groups", and the workshop "moves away from narratives of blame and righteousness, shifting attention to the mechanisms of systemic change".

What it exercises is not climate knowledge. "The workshop fosters multiple soft skills: interaction during sessions, engaging in dynamic discussions with open-minded peers, and participating in strategic negotiations."

What Happened

The ambitious groups reached for 1.5°C and the group that had to reconcile everyone landed at 2.0°C.

The gap between the two columns is the finding, and the article treats it as instruction rather than as failure.

✓"Their vision reflects the risk-taking, future-oriented mindset embedded in the learning process of EL-4062"
✓The bootcamp's role, on this reading, is "fostering bold, system-aware thinkers"

Policy Maker and Government
•"Government stakeholders modeled the inclusive thinking central to EL-4062."
•"Their final framework integrated diverse proposals to arrive at a realistic target of 2.0°C."
•"Their process reflected the principles of negotiation, prioritization, and equity."
•The number is worse and the method is the one the exercise was teaching
•Reconciliation, not ambition, is what the constraint rewards

Conclusion

What the exercise establishes is that technology was never the binding constraint.

This simulation underscored that solving climate change requires not just technological solutions, but governance frameworks capable of embracing multiple truths and long-term, collective interests.

— Why Climate Simulation in an Entrepreneurship Class?
Why it belongs in this class
The workshop "equips students with a deep understanding of the complexities of climate change" and "empowers them to think critically and innovatively, being mindful and compassionate in tackling climate change." The transferable skill is negotiating under interdependence, which is the entrepreneurial problem restated.
What the tool models
It is described as "notable for its balanced and humble approach", promoting "a mindful and compassionate methodology in addressing climate challenges" — a claim about disposition, not about accuracy.
Where the author stands
The article is written from inside the practice, not about it: "I am happy to share my passion as En-Roads Climate Ambassador." It reports one class, and it does not generalise beyond it.
