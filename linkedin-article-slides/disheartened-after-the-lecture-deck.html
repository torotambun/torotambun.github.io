Disheartened After the Lecture: A Reflection on Lost Impact and Exhaustion

Three journal submissions, seventeen LinkedIn articles, and two newspaper columns of preparation meet an audience that does not engage — and the piece resolves not through better technique but through surrendering the outcome to God rather than judging the audience for it.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

Three journal submissions and seventeen LinkedIn articles prepare for one lecture to 200 students attending mainly for the attendance mark.

It was Friday, around noon, and 200 students belonging to Kelas Agama Kristen gathered at their unit on campus.

Most of them were attending primarily out of obligation for class attendance, as I learned afterward. This was no ordinary session for me — I poured my brain wholeheartedly in the midst of auto-ethnographic work for my PhD dissertation, a commitment that resulted in three academic journal submissions aimed at indexed publication.

I crafted over 166 reading minutes across 17 in-depth articles on LinkedIn, aiming to bring rich context to the mental health themes I wanted to convey — articles that had more than 65,000 impressions as of the day of the lecture. I even published two newspaper articles, hoping to elevate mental health in public discourse. With each step of preparation, I thought of these students, each minute and word intended to give them something of lasting value.

The Scene

Two opening questions, meant to spark reflection, draw shrugs — four days after his own mother-in-law's death.

The session began late, and the framework question was posed to spark curiosity.

To spark their curiosity, I posed a simple but powerful question: "Is mental health a means to an end, or the end itself?" The answer for a deserved audience would have been an eye-opening one — yet, rather than thoughtful reflection, I was met with shrugs. The weight of the question seemed to drift past them, and I felt the first pang of disappointment.

My next question was left unattended, even though this question is a rhetorical question for me considering I have just lost my mother in law 4 days earlier: "Why we celebrated life, not so much dead?" I reminded myself to keep a no-blame mindset — perhaps this wasn't a topic they were prepared to approach seriously. I noticed their expressions — the tense faces, wandering eyes, and blank stares.

Field Notes

A story of a student's abuse by her own lecturer, meant as the session's turning point, passes without visible reaction.

Careful diplomatic groundwork had been laid with the class's own pastors and ministers beforehand.

Before the session, I had taken the initiative to meet with the pastors and ministers, recognizing that my role as a non-theologian could create some tension. I assured them I would stay strictly within my own expertise, honoring their theological authority. Though I had prepared with Greek translations of New Testament verses and Hebrew interpretations for Old Testament references, I promised them I would not include these elements in my presentation.

This may have been my around 400th speaking engagement, and not very often have I left the stage feeling disheartened. Earlier in the session, at minute 10, I had shared a story of a female student within a famous university who had endured sexual abuse by her lecturer, hoping it would underscore the importance of individualized mental health support and institutional accountability. Yet, the story seemed to go unnoticed — neither students nor teachers showed gestures of compassion, and the gravity of the issue appeared to pass without impact.

What I Carry

Elijah's consolation came from God serving him, not from his own striving — so the outcome is surrendered, not judged.

The reflection turns to the story of the prophet Elijah after his own effort met no visible response.

Whatever you do, work at it with all your heart, as working for the Lord, not for human masters

— Colossians 3:23
The word does not return empty
"so is my word that goes out from my mouth: It will not return to me empty" (Isaiah 55:11). After his tremendous efforts, Elijah found himself feeling alone, unappreciated, and frustrated by a lack of change — yet he ultimately found consolation not through his own striving, but from God Himself, who served him in that moment rather than expecting service from him. I have decided to lay low and surrender the results to Him, the true owner of this work.
