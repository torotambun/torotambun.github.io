A Reflection of Psalm 69: Facing Rejections in Scopus Race

The rejections stopped being about the research and started being about the researcher — and Psalm 69 turned out to be the only thing shaped like that experience. Not because it resolves the pain but because it holds it: a public lament, sung communally, never meant to be tidy. Lament is not weakness. It is structure, and it refuses to let professional disappointment become private shame.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Count

I have not stopped counting, and the counting is part of the lament.

"I haven't stopped counting. Not yet." "Every journal submission—Scopus-indexed. Every rejection—logged. I keep track. I study the letters. I look for structure. I look for clues."

RECORDED A
Fifty-four attempts from seventeen articles
"Out of 54 submission attempts from 17 different research articles: 26 desk rejections, 3 peer-reviewed rejections, and 3 withdrawals, 9 went through transfer desk…"
RECORDED B
Three accepted in Scopus-indexed journals
"I now have 3 articles accepted in Scopus-indexed journals (2 Q2, 1 Q1 under revision). Another 6 were published in non-Scopus journals."
RECORDED C
One manuscript revised eleven times
"I integrated every reviewer comment. I ran fresh data. I adjusted scope. Eventually, accepted in a Q2 journal." Another journal gave no reply for two months and then sent a template rejection; one simply said "not within remit".
RECORDED D
What could not be explained
"I tried to improve. I studied the critique. I revised. I resubmitted. Still, many were desk-rejected. I did not quit. But I cannot explain why those three made it through. The same work that had died in silence found life elsewhere." "I can only conclude: someone—an editor, a reviewer—read it. Gave it time. Thoughtfully. Generously. It was grace."
Sometimes there is no structure to find

"Sometimes, I find none." The looking is methodical and the method does not always return an answer — which is the condition the rest of the article is written from.

The Erosion

The rejection stopped being about the research and became about the researcher.

"And it wears on the soul. It's not just the research under evaluation. It becomes the researcher. The line between feedback and self-perception starts to blur."

"And while I try to receive each one professionally, the weight builds." A paper once presented proudly in newspaper columns and seminars, read and cited, "was now met with nothing".

"Sometimes I rewrote chapters only to be told—still not there. And I began to ask: is it the content that's being rejected, or is it me?"

"Each no—silent or stated—chipped at something foundational. I wasn't just doubting the research. I was doubting myself. My ability to think. To write. To be. The voice I used to trust became strained, hesitant, fractured. It wasn't just a professional toll—it was personal erosion. Like parts of my humanity were being slowly withdrawn with each unread PDF."

The Structure

Psalm 69 offered architecture, not answers.

"But in that space, I found Psalm 69. Not as theology. As mirror."

"Psalm 69 doesn't ramble. It moves—deliberately." A cry for help; then false accusations and isolation; then a plea grounded not in merit but mercy; then personal heartbreak; and finally a raw call for judgment, "not calmly, but with intensity".

"This pattern doesn't resolve pain—it holds it. I began to see that what I was experiencing in academia had a structure too: the first attempt, the wait, the confusion, the appeal, the silence."

"Psalm 69 didn't offer answers. It offered architecture. It gave shape to my pain, not solutions."

The Words

The waters reached nephesh, the soul, not the neck.

"Certain phrases wouldn't let me go."

1  ·  Verse 1 — the waters
"Ki-ba'u mayim 'ad-naphesh—“The waters have come up to my soul.” Most translations say “neck,” but the Hebrew says nephesh, soul. Not drowning physically—drowning in identity. That was me."
2  ·  Verse 20 — the broken heart
"Shavarti lev—“A broken heart.” But the verb shavar means violently crushed. Not poetic sadness—rupture."
3  ·  The word that haunted him most
"ḥerpah. Reproach. Disgrace. Not because of guilt, but because of humiliation with no defender. ḥerpah is what you feel when your effort is dismissed without hearing. It is the silence after “not within scope.”"
4  ·  And one more — shama
"And yet, the Psalm still holds one more word—shama. To hear. Not vaguely. Not passively. But to attend. “The Lord hears the needy…” (v. 33). It brought me back to Shema Yisrael. To be heard by God is to be remembered."

The Placement

The lament was public protest made sacred, never private confession.

"Psalm 69 is not polite poetry. It is desperate speech."

"Scholars trace it to David, but its language far outlives any one person." It was used by the community in times of public disgrace, not merely personal disappointment, and "likely served as a liturgical lament, sung aloud when Israel felt surrounded by shame or defeated by forces it could not name."

"That detail shifted everything for me. This wasn't private reflection. This was public protest, made sacred. That is what made the Psalm powerful—it was never meant to be tidy. It was meant to be heard."

It is quoted repeatedly in the New Testament — Jesus overturning the tables, the insult on the cross, the betrayal of Judas. "These citations do not merely suggest fulfillment. They frame the Psalm as a typology of righteous suffering—public shame endured not because of guilt, but faithfulness."

In Jewish liturgical tradition it is recited on fast days and associated with Selichot. "The suffering speaker becomes a figure who intercedes for the people—one whose humiliation is not just personal, but representative." "And that gave me permission. My wounds were not a distraction. They were part of the tradition of protest."

The question that follows from the placement

"I began to ask: if the Psalm could hold David's collapse, Israel's exile, and Christ's rejection" — could it not also hold his?

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Practice

Lament is not weakness; it is structure, and it refuses private shame.

"So what does all this mean—for another rejection email? Another silence? Another version of “still not there”?"

"It means lament is not weakness. It is structure. It is prayer. It is a refusal to turn professional disappointment into private shame."

"Psalm 69 does not rescue the speaker from pain. It relocates the pain. It says: “place it here.” Place your voice, even if trembling. Place your grief, even if raw. Do not wait for perfect clarity. Do not wait until you feel strong. The Psalm invites protest now, because God listens now."

"When I reread those letters—“not in scope,” “declined without review,” “we regret to inform”—I began to hear more than policy. I heard the cry of ḥerpah. A heart trying, breaking, trying again." "Psalm 69 taught me that pain does not need to be defended. It needs to be spoken."

Who this is addressed to, and who is thanked

"To those in academia, startup pitches, job applications, or grant submissions—who have been told “no” so many times the question shifts from what's wrong with the work to what's wrong with me: this Psalm is for that." And: "to the editors, reviewers, and journals who did accept my work—thank you. Not just for publishing it, but for reading it. For offering revision. For seeing the version that was not yet finished, and saying: we will walk with you through another round."

Conclusion

I may not control the outcome, but I can choose where to put my voice.

"I still don't fully understand the acceptance criteria—especially when most rejections cite “scope.” But I'm learning not to demand explanation."

I still count. I still revise. I still cry out. I still hope.

— A Reflection of Psalm 69: Facing Rejections in Scopus Race
What the Psalm does and does not do
"Psalm 69 does not promise immediate redemption, it does not tidy the wound, it sanctifies it. But it relocates the pain—out of private shame into covenant speech. It does not sanitize the cry. It makes the humiliation visible to God."
What the counting turns out to mean
"“I still count”—not only my submissions, but my tears, my voice, my worth. I still believe that pain brought honestly before God is not wasted." "And that, for now, is enough."
The choice that remains available
"I am learning to count rejection, and still pray. Yes, I have come to accept this: I may not be able to control the outcome, but I can choose where to put my voice." "And I have chosen to put it in the Psalms."
