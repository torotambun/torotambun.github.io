Deeper into Edgar Schein's OB Grand Theory

The article opens a working thread by defending a choice of theorist. An eight-month hypothesis rests on Schein's foundational shared values, and anthropology is reluctant to grant Schein standing because he was trained in business and engineering rather than in the discipline. The article answers that objection twice: once by precedent, noting that Malinowski was trained as a physicist, and once by excavation, tracing Schein's three-layer model back to the social psychology, Lewinian field theory, systems theory and cultural anthropology it was assembled from. What emerges is not a defence of borrowing but a claim about what Schein added. Where the anthropologists described structures, Schein built a model in which the deepest layer can be changed, which is what a nation-level hypothesis about shared values actually requires.

Introduction

An eight-month hypothesis rests on a theorist the discipline is reluctant to grant.

Situation

The hypothesis is stated in the first paragraph and it is national in scale: "In the last eight months, I have been proposing a hypothesis that to cultivate world-class, innovation-driven entrepreneurs who adhere to 'purpose' and 'Tikkun Olam' as guiding principles, a nation must embark on a journey to either build, possess, or nurture its foundational shared values, as articulated by Edgar Schein."

Complication

The authority it leans on is contested. "There's a notable hesitancy within the anthropological academic circle to adopt this framework set by Schein, who, despite his profound influence, isn't traditionally recognized as an anthropologist." His background is business and engineering.

Question

Can a model built outside anthropology carry an argument about a nation's culture, and if so, on what grounds?

Answer

The article answers by precedent and by excavation. The precedent is that the discipline has absorbed outsiders before. The excavation shows that Schein's model was assembled from social psychology, Lewin, systems theory and the anthropologists themselves, which makes it continuous with the field rather than foreign to it.

Gap

The discipline has admitted a physicist before, so training is not the test.

The precedent the article reaches for is pointed, because it names the figure anthropology treats as foundational. "This reminds me that even Bronisław Malinowski, often heralded as an anthropological beacon, was fundamentally educated, and trained as a physicist."

The observation does more than score a point. If the field's own exemplar arrived from outside it, then the objection to Schein cannot rest on where he was trained, and has to rest instead on whether the model does work the field recognises.

That is the test the rest of the article sets out to meet. "After delving into the works of prominent anthropologists, I am startled in awe by the marked parallels in their stratifications." The claim being made is structural similarity, not borrowed authority.

The model itself is three layers deep and the article is careful about which one matters. Visible artifacts sit at the apex, the espoused values beneath them, and at the base "the basic underlying assumptions: the unconscious, rarely questioned beliefs that truly steer behavior".

The middle layer, which the later articles lean on

"Beneath this tangible layer nestle the espoused values, essentially representing a group's vocalized beliefs and behavioral rules." This is the layer that maps onto a nation's stated values, and it is the one the thread keeps returning to.

Framework

Schein assembled the model from four bodies of work, and naming them is what answers the objection.

The article's method is to take the model apart. Its governing line is that "Schein's work is original in many respects, he didn't develop his ideas in a vacuum."

1  ·  Social psychology
Group dynamics supply the unit of analysis, since organisations are large groups. Asch on conformity explains how cultures persist and how members come to accept shared beliefs. Socialisation explains how newcomers learn the ropes. Tajfel's social identity theory explains departmental loyalty and us-versus-them. Festinger's cognitive dissonance explains how employees rationalise norms that look counterintuitive.
2  ·  Kurt Lewin, whom Schein worked with directly
Field theory holds that "behavior is a function of both the person and the environment", written B=f(P,E), with life space covering everything bearing on behaviour at a given moment. Lewin's change model runs unfreeze, change, refreeze, and the article reads Schein's account of changing entrenched assumptions as a reflection of it.
3  ·  Systems theory
Interdependence explains why the three layers move together. Feedback loops explain how norms are reinforced or challenged. Hierarchy explains subcultures nested inside an organisational culture. Emergence explains culture that is not the sum of individual beliefs. And homeostasis explains resistance: "Systems tend to resist change in order to maintain equilibrium, a principle known as homeostasis."
4  ·  Cultural anthropology
Mead and Benedict from the culture and personality school, where Benedict held that "underneath observable behaviors, there are patterns of beliefs and values". Boas for cultural relativism, Malinowski for function, and Geertz for culture as a "system of shared symbols and meanings", read through Weber's interpretive sociology and delivered as thick description.
The three sources the article names last and does not develop

The human relations movement, which established organisations as social rather than purely economic systems; T-group training and group dynamics, which shaped Schein's 1960s work on how culture shows itself in groups; and existential and humanistic psychology, credited with the emphasis on meaning-making at the unconscious level.

Conclusion

What Schein added to the anthropologists is that his deepest layer can move.

The article's closing distinction is the one that makes Schein usable for a hypothesis about building shared values, rather than merely describing them.

while both anthropological insights and Schein's work address underlying cultural structures, Schein's model is more dynamic and focused on change, evolution, and the interplay between different levels of culture.

— Deeper into Edgar Schein's OB Grand Theory, 27 September 2023
Why the difference is the point
A hypothesis that a nation must build, possess or nurture foundational shared values requires a model in which the deepest layer is reachable. A purely descriptive account of deep structure cannot support that claim, however accurate it is.
What the article concedes
The anthropologists are credited with providing the broader understanding of how cultures operate and with informing Schein's thinking "even if not directly cited". The debt is asserted rather than documented, and the article does not claim otherwise.
