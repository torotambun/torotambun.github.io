Bridging Ideals and Reality: The Evolving Role of Educators in the University Setting

Sosrokartono's portrait of a teacher is read as a specification rather than as devotional writing, and the article measures the contemporary professor's job against it point by point. The divergences it finds are of two kinds and the article keeps them apart: structural pressures that no individual chose, and personal failures that no institution can be blamed for. Its proposed bridge is not a softer ideal but a different kind of educator, one who has worked outside the academy and can carry theory back into the world, which is also a description of the author's own position.

Introduction

Sosrokartono's teacher is self-reliant, impartial and ever-present, and gives without expectation.

The article opens on Drs. RMP. Sosrokartono's text The Teacher, rendered into English across three scanned pages. Six attributes carry the portrait, and the article treats them as requirements rather than as praise.

1  ·  Self-reliant
"The life of a teacher is characterized by self-reliance, living modestly on their own means and not depending on the generosity of others."
2  ·  Giving without expectation
They share their knowledge and wisdom without expectation, which the article renders as being as unconditional as nature's gifts.
3  ·  Impartial
They look upon every soul with impartiality, discarding prejudices built on societal constructs, and showing humility rather than pride in their scholarly pursuits.
4  ·  Spiritually capable
Possessing a deep understanding of spiritual matters, the teacher can guide their own spirit and those of their learners, with speech that is measured and profound.
5  ·  Ever-present
"A teacher is ever-present, particularly in times of need, exemplifying the ideal of selfless service to the community." They become a caretaker and a confidant to those in distress.
6  ·  Never abusing the position
"They provide a canopy of protection, nurturing care, and guidance, honoring their role with integrity, never abusing their position or the privileges that come with it."
What the six add up to

"This collection of attributes forms the educator not as a mere conduit of information but as an illuminator of paths, a mentor, and a lifelong learner in the boundless classroom of life, constantly adapting alongside their students."

The Gap

That portrait is a specification rather than a nostalgia piece, and contemporary academia diverges from it sharply.

The article's method is what distinguishes it from an appreciation. Each attribute is taken as a testable requirement and set against what the job now contains, and the divergence it reports is described as stark rather than gradual.

The clearest case is availability. Sosrokartono required the teacher to be available and fully present; the modern professor carries a workload extending well past teaching, with research, publication and administration diluting the time and energy available to students. The casualty the article names is the ideal of the teacher as mentor and guide.

A second divergence is structural rather than temporal. Sosrokartono envisaged teachers and students on a mutual journey of learning, and the article notes that hierarchical structures in modern institutions position teachers as authoritative figures rather than as collaborative partners.

The article is careful that this is not an indictment of individuals. It calls for rethinking academic policies and evaluation criteria, more support and resources for educators, and a culture in which the exchange of ideas between teachers and students is valued, which places the fault in the design.

Diagnosis

Four structural pressures pull the modern professor away from the portrait.

These are the divergences the article treats as features of the system rather than as choices made by any educator inside it.

What the ideal requires
•Presence, and time enough to mentor
•Knowledge pursued for its own worth and society's
•Security enough to commit to a student's development
•Teacher and student on one mutual journey
•Hours given to the people in the room
What the job contains
✗Evaluation on research output and publication record
✗Publish or perish, rewarding quantity over contribution
✗Adjunct posts, part-time or temporary, without security or benefits
✗Hierarchies positioning the teacher as an authority, not a partner
✗Administrative duties and the pursuit of accreditations
On the adjunct case specifically

The article notes that adjunct professors "may embody the passion and dedication to teaching as extolled by Sosrokartono, yet they face systemic constraints that limit their ability to fully commit to their students' development." The disposition is present and the conditions are not.

Details

Publish or perish displaces the axiology of research, which was knowledge for its intrinsic worth and society's benefit.

The article gives this pressure its own chapter and names precisely what it costs. The drive to publish is described as serving not only intellectual advancement but institutional recognition and career survival, and the article's term for what it displaces is axiological rather than practical.

The consequence it identifies is a change in what research is for. Where educators are pushed towards constant publication, the essence of teaching and of genuine scientific inquiry become secondary concerns, and the output tends towards quantity rather than meaningful contribution to knowledge or societal benefit.

The article sets this directly against Sosrokartono's position that knowledge exists for moral and societal upliftment, which makes the conflict a disagreement about purpose rather than a complaint about workload.

What is displaced

The article writes that the drive to publish "can overshadow the true axiology of research" and identifies that axiology as the pursuit of knowledge for its intrinsic value and societal benefit.

The Personal Failure

Defensiveness and moral lapse fail the ideal from inside, where no institution can be blamed.

Having attributed four divergences to the system, the article turns to two that cannot be. Sosrokartono asks teachers to personify humility and a willingness to learn alongside their students, which requires a vulnerability that no workload prevents.

The failure is specific and observable. A teacher who answers criticism with defence rather than reflection has declined the very thing being taught, and the article treats that as a missed opportunity to model growth and openness rather than as a lapse of manners.

The second failure is graver because of what a teacher is. Moral failings or ethical lapses undermine the trust placed in an educator, and the article's point is that the damage does not stop at the individual: it casts a shadow over the credibility of the entire institution.

The article's formulation makes integrity a professional requirement rather than a private virtue, fundamental to the trust and respect that form the backbone of the educational relationship.

On defensiveness

"When a teacher meets criticism with defensiveness rather than reflection, it represents a missed opportunity to model the growth and openness that Sosrokartono holds essential for the learning process."

On moral failure

The erosion of moral integrity in educators, the article holds, "does more than harm their personal standing; it compromises the very foundation of the educational process."

Implications

The bridge is the educator who has lived outside the academy and can carry theory back into the world.

The article's constructive answer is not a relaxation of the ideal but a different supply of people to meet it. Educators who have worked in other professions, faced real-world problems, or moved between cultural and social settings are held to bring insight into the classroom that scholarship alone does not produce.

The mechanism it proposes is concrete. Such educators can illustrate theoretical concepts with practical examples, which makes learning more relatable, and they can advise on applying knowledge in practical settings and on the skills actually in demand.

This is also the article's answer to the ivory tower charge it raises by name in its own text. Where academic research is seen as disconnected from everyday challenges, the remedy it proposes is educators who have been on the other side of that distance.

The final requirement is contextualisation. Integrating academic theory with practical insight, drawing from a range of experiences and disciplines, and encouraging students to apply knowledge critically and creatively are named as what it takes to meet Sosrokartono's vision inside a modern institution.

Retired professors as the existing proof

The article points to emeritus professors as people already living the ideal. Unburdened by administrative and scholarly pressure, their continued engagement in intellectual discourse, and their advocacy for foundational learning such as early childhood education, reflect commitment to pedagogical ideals over institutional duties.

Conclusion

Education remains a moral journey, and the teacher's legacy is character rather than output.

The article closes by returning the whole argument to what a teacher leaves behind.

This collection of attributes forms the educator not as a mere conduit of information but as an illuminator of paths, a mentor, and a lifelong learner in the boundless classroom of life, constantly adapting alongside their students.

— Bridging Ideals and Reality, 20 December 2023
What is asked of the institution
Rethinking evaluation criteria to weigh teaching quality and student engagement, and supporting educators well enough that the pedagogical role is possible rather than heroic.
What is asked of the educator
To balance scholarly obligation against pedagogical duty rather than choosing between them, and to keep the humility the ideal requires when criticism arrives.
What the legacy consists of
The article's position is that the legacy of a teacher extends beyond academic achievements to the cultivation of character and the fostering of a just and ethical society.
