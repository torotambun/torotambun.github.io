Sense-Making: The Foundation of Strategy (and others)

The article's claim is one of priority: sense-making is not another framework alongside VUCA and BANI but the capability those frameworks presuppose. Acronyms name conditions, and naming a condition does nothing until someone can read a situation and construct a narrative to act on. The argument is built from Weick's seven properties, of which the decisive one is that plausibility beats accuracy, and it is anchored historically in Drucker, who in 1994 described the same failure mode without the word: organisations doing the right things fruitlessly because their assumptions no longer fit reality. The closing position is modest and exact, that sense-making buys better handling of surprises rather than fewer of them.

Introduction

The speed of change is not slowing, and VUCA is over-rated with its meaning degrading further each year.

The article opens with a judgement rather than a definition, and the judgement is aimed at the vocabulary the subject usually arrives in. Scholars reach easily for VUCA, and the article's position is that the reaching itself has worn the term down.

The premise behind the urgency is stated in a single clause and attributed. The rate of change has not decelerated, which is why the capacity to interpret what is happening matters more now than it did.

The diagnosis that follows is about method rather than effort. Organisations and individuals are bombarded with information, much of it contradictory or incomplete, and the article's claim is that traditional linear approaches to problem-solving fall short in exactly those conditions.

A friend of the author prefers the word mindset for what follows; the article notes the preference and keeps its own term. The deck refers to him by relation rather than by name, as it does with a second person credited later for pointing the author to Drucker.

Framework

Sense-making turns the raw data of experience into a narrative that can be acted on.

The definition the article gives is procedural rather than cognitive. Sense-making is the process by which individuals or groups interpret and construct meaning from experience, and the operation it performs is a conversion: scattered, often confusing information becomes a coherent narrative.

What that narrative is for is the load-bearing part. It forms the basis for understanding, for decision-making and for action, which is why the article treats the capability as prior to strategy rather than as a component of it.

Weick's account adds the element that makes it more than interpretation. Sense-making is not only processing information but structuring and framing it so that it aligns with existing beliefs, experience and expectation, which means the sense-maker is party to the result.

The article extends the same capability past the present tense. It is also about anticipating what follows, foreseeing potential outcomes and implications, which in an era of accelerating change is where it earns its place.

Weick's description, as the article renders it

Sense-making is "a way people structure the unknown, transforming the chaos of the world into a reality they can understand and work within."

Origins

Weick, Dervin and Ackoff built it from three directions: the social, the individual's gap and the system.

The article credits three contributors, and their three entry points are what give the concept its range.

01
The organisational theorist
Karl E. Weick
Sensemaking in Organizations (1995) explores how people in organisations construct, interpret and give meaning to their experience. Weick's emphasis is interactive: it is through social interaction and shared narrative that members build a common understanding of their environment.
02
The communication scholar
Brenda Dervin
Grounded in how individuals seek and use information. Her model turns on gap-bridging, with people actively seeking information to close gaps in their understanding, a process that is dynamic and shaped continuously by context and experience.
03
The systems thinker
Russell Ackoff
Contributes the insistence on the whole rather than the components. To make sense of a situation one must consider the broader system's structure and dynamics, not isolated events or issues.
What the three together establish

The article's summary is a shift in what organisations and individuals are taken to be: not passive reactors to an environment but active interpreters and constructors of their own reality. That shift is what makes the rest of the argument possible.

Details

Seven properties govern it, and the decisive one is that plausibility beats accuracy.

Weick's seven properties are set out as the article gives them, and the last is the one that separates sense-making from analysis.

1  ·  Identity construction
Sense-making is bound up with the identity of the sense-maker. Individuals and organisations understand the world in ways consistent with their self-image and their role in a group or society.
2  ·  Retrospective analysis
It often works backward. Events acquire meaning and significance through retrospection rather than at the moment of occurrence.
3  ·  Enacting environments
Rather than only responding to an external environment, individuals and organisations help create the context in which they operate, through their own actions.
4  ·  Social interaction
Inherently social. Understanding develops and is refined through interaction with others, a process involving negotiation, collaboration and conflict.
5  ·  Ongoing projects
Not a discrete event but a continuous process, occurring inside the flow of everyday activity and shaped by projects and goals already in motion.
6  ·  Extracted cues
Attention falls on certain elements of experience while others are ignored. Those extracted cues form the basis of the sense made and guide what is done next.
7  ·  Plausibility over accuracy
"In sense-making, plausibility often trumps accuracy. The aim is not to find the one true explanation but rather a story that makes sense and is useful in guiding action."
Why the same event yields different readings

The article names sense-making as highly contextual: cultural background, organisational norms, past experience and current challenge all shape it, so the same event can be interpreted differently by different people without either reading being a failure.

Evidence

Drucker named the same thing in 1994 and called it the theory of the business.

The article's strongest move is historical. Before the vocabulary existed, Drucker described the failure sense-making is meant to prevent, and described it as a paradox rather than as incompetence.

His diagnosis relocates the fault from execution to premise. Organisations in crisis are usually doing the right things, and the reason it does not help is that the assumptions the organisation was built on no longer correspond to the world it operates in.

Those assumptions have three parts, which Drucker sets out and the article reproduces: assumptions about the environment, meaning society, market, customer and technology; about the organisation's specific mission; and about the core competencies needed to accomplish it.

The four specifications for making that theory work are where the article does its own interpretive work, annotating each with the phrase hence sense-making. The assumptions must fit reality; they must fit one another; the theory must be known and understood throughout the organisation; and it must be tested constantly. The article reads that fourth requirement as anticipating emergent strategy, which it attributes to Clayton Christensen.

The paradox, in Drucker's words

"The root cause of nearly every one of these crises is not that things are being done poorly. It is not even that the wrong things are being done." And the explanation: "The assumptions on which the organization has been built and is being run no longer fit reality."

The author's own gloss on the fourth specification

"here he predicted the emergent strategy that then introduced by Clayton Christensen" — the attribution is the article's.

Implications

Every acronym since names conditions, and sense-making is what answers them.

The article maps sense-making onto both dominant frameworks, and the asymmetry is the point: each letter is a description of a difficulty, and the response in every case is the same capability.

VUCA (1987), and what answers it
•Volatility: interpreting rapid change, constructing coherent narrative amid flux
•Uncertainty: creating contexts that offer clarity when the future is unpredictable
•Complexity: discerning patterns within intricate, interdependent systems
•Ambiguity: developing multiple hypotheses and flexible strategies
•Originated at the U.S. Army War College to describe post-Cold War conditions
BANI (2020), and what answers it
•Brittleness: recognising fragile systems and building resilience against sudden breaks
•Anxiety: supplying meaning and understanding where uncertainty produces unease
•Nonlinearity: interpreting dynamics where small inputs produce large effects
•Incomprehensibility: simplifying and contextualising toward actionable insight
•Coined by Jamais Cascio of the Institute for the Future
And six more competing for the same ground

The article also surveys TUNA (turbulent, uncertain, novel, ambiguous), VUCA Prime (vision, understanding, clarity, agility), PACE (persistent, agile, coordinated, energetic), DANCE (dynamic, adaptive, nonlinear, co-creative, emergent), DEAL (digital, engaging, agile, lean) and the Cynefin framework, which sorts problems into simple, complicated, complex, chaotic and disorder.

Conclusion

It does not promise fewer surprises, only better handling of them.

The article's closing claim is deliberately bounded, and the bound is what makes it credible.

Sense-making does not promise a world with fewer surprises; rather, it provides the tools to deal with surprises more effectively.

— Sense-Making: The Foundation of Strategy (and others), 26 November 2023
What it enables
Discerning the signal from the noise, innovating amid chaos, and providing clear direction when the path forward is obscured. The article treats these as the practical outputs rather than as aspirations.
Why it is not only interpretation
Sense-making empowers leaders not only to interpret their environment but to shape it, influencing the narrative and steering organisations toward intended futures, which follows from Weick's enacting-environments property.
The status the article claims for it
The quintessential skill for the twenty-first-century leader, and a continuous process rather than an exercise, equipping leaders to manage the present and prepare for what follows.
