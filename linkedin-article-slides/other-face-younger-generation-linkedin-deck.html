Not So Often Published: The Other Face of Younger Generation - Brutal Honest Facts - What Can We Do?

The article sets the handful of celebrated graduates the author taught between 2020 and 2022 against the typical Indonesian millennial, who has a high-school education and earns about US$150 a month, on figures quoted from Tehusijarana (2020). It traces the gap to a rent-seeking business model that supplies the consumption term of GDP while the investment term is missing, and it proposes education change in three horizons: seven core technologies now, teacher mobilisation next, and early-childhood formation with system-dynamics, entrepreneurial and innovation-values teaching for the long term. It sets the proposal beside a World Economic Forum map and the European key competences, and closes by asking whether the elementary curriculum pivots around such competences.

Introduction

The celebrated few are a handful, while the typical millennial has a high-school education and earns about US$150 a month.

Situation

From 2020 to 2022 the author co-learned with students at the country's most prestigious institute and university. He describes only a handful of them, bright, visionary and diligent, who stand for what the mainstream media depicts as "upper-middle class, highly educated, latte-sipping and (future) white collar employees". They sit in the 10 percent bracket of university graduates to be absorbed into industries.

Complication

A journalist's report (Tehusijarana, 2020) shows the larger part of the millennials, the core of the demographic bonus, as the opposite: they "only have a high-school level education, work in blue-collar or low-level service jobs, with an average monthly income of only around US$150". Statistics Bureau data from 2017 put about 20 percent in blue-collar work and about 25 percent in sales or service jobs, against 1.4 percent in managerial posts and 7 percent in professional jobs. The Wittgenstein Centre puts post-secondary education at 14.4 percent and graduate degrees at 0.38 percent. In Greater Jakarta, a 2010 study by the Australian National University and the University of Indonesia found about 25 percent of men aged 20 to 34, and 38 percent of women, with junior high school diplomas or lower.

Question

What can be done, when industry is short of the absorbing capacity that graduates need and most of the generation has not reached graduation?

Answer

The article answers on two fronts. The industries that would absorb graduates are missing the investment side of the economy, and education has to change in three horizons, short, mid and long term.

The persona in the documentary

A Jakarta Post documentary (2020) of the same title follows one blue-collar millennial who earns USD107 a month, spends all of it paying off a motorcycle and a smartphone, lives on a USD3 per diem plus occasional tips, and has no savings.

Evidence note

The millennial figures are Tehusijarana's (2020), and the newest of them dates from 2017. The 10 percent bracket for graduates is the author's own and carries no source.

Gap

Rent-seeking companies supply the consumption term of GDP while the investment term is missing.

The article says the country needs industries to absorb its graduates: innovation-driven enterprise, heavy machinery and capital-intensive manufacturing. What it finds instead is a predominance of the rent-seeker business model.

What rent-seeking supplies
•The "C" variable of the GDP equation, to which the rent-seeker model contributes extremely well
•Early adoption of consumer products, especially cheaper ones, alongside laggard or pragmatic adoption of technology as a lever for developing the country
•A shortcut around an innovation ecosystem that takes 15 years or more, with more reward going to rent-seeking than to building R&D, brand factories or low-end disruptive businesses
•A trillion-dollar fund of dry powder held by risk-capital institutions, which amplifies commerce built on demographics and goes to growth-platform matchmaking businesses
•A scarcity of sustainable innovation-driven business, attributed to a not-so-entrepreneurial mental model, thin entrepreneurship curricula and low STEM popularity
What the innovation ecosystem needs
•The "I" variable of the GDP equation, which the article says the country is missing
•The same risk capital allocated to the long-term development of innovation-driven enterprises
•An organic effort of the whole ecosystem: risk capital, large corporations, government in a supporting and regulating role only, universities as the main source of entrepreneurs and inventors, and entrepreneurs who start with passion and paying customers (Budden & Murray, 2019)

Framework

Education has to change in three horizons: seven core technologies now, teacher mobilisation next, and early-childhood formation for the long term.

The author says a respected scholar and businessperson asked which skills would improve the competency of the Indonesian workforce. His answer is structured around three findings he calls empirical: "live the life to the fullest", "know thy calling" and "cherish the moment".

1
Short term, and for the short term only: graduates in seven core technologies, applied to Indonesia's own industries
Produce as many graduates as can proportionally be produced who learn the seven core technologies that alter every dimension of modern human lives: biotechnology, wireless mesh networks, nanotechnology, pervasive computing, robotics, machine learning and 3D printing (Segars, 2018). They apply the knowledge in industries unique to Indonesia's ecosystem: agriculture, fisheries, food, forestry, energy extraction and tourism. The article adds that the innovation-entrepreneurial cluster ecosystem must be ready to absorb them (Tambun, 2020).
2
Mid-term: mobilise graduates, industrialists and retirees to teach the teachers
Invest in mobilising graduates, industrialists, retirees and productive workers who meet the minimum of three skills (system dynamics, entrepreneurial skill and structured logic), with rewards and wages that are not limited to money. They either teach the existing teachers or teach directly in elementary, junior high and senior high schools. The article says this narrows the knowledge gap and fills the motivation gap among low-performing teachers.
3
Long term: start with early childhood, then teach three things across grades 1 to 12
Ensure that infants are born healthy, that mothers have enough nutrition and that children are safe during their first five years, an approach explained by the firing-neuron pruning framework. Then invest in grades 1 to 12 in the ability to think dynamically, the ability to act entrepreneurially, and the planting of the basic values of innovation (novelty, authenticity, perspective, diversity and curiosity) beside the noble values of respect, piety and "preparing earth as a better place to live for our future generations". The article expects a full capacity of innovative Indonesia in 12 years, and STEM graduates and new PhD graduates per capita in 17 to 20 years, with high-quality STEM education, many scientists and engineers, and researchers per million population (Budden & Murray, 2019).
Evidence note

The three findings are called empirical and are named without a study. The 12-year and the 17-to-20-year horizons are printed as given, and the article does not tie either to a source.

Conclusion

The proposal sits close to two international frameworks, and the article closes by asking whether the elementary curriculum does.

The reflection sets the proposal beside a World Economic Forum transformation map and a European recommendation on key competences, and then turns to the curriculum itself.

The Purpose of Human Life is to Serve and to Show Compassion and the Will to Help Others

— Albert Schweitzer, as quoted at the close of the article
The World Economic Forum map
The article cites a transformation map from the Forum's Strategic Intelligence (Welpe & Rank, 2023) as in line with its "humble proposal". The map says the Education and Skills theme should be addressed through lifelong learning pathways, quality basic education, 21st century curricula, relevant specialised education, digital fluency and STEM skills, and education innovation.
The European key competences
Set against the eight competencies for lifelong learning in the 2006 recommendation of the European Parliament and of the Council, the author says the proposal is "not far off at all". The eight are the mother tongue, a foreign language, mathematical, scientific and technological competence, digital competence, learning to learn, social and civic competences, a sense of initiative and entrepreneurship, and cultural awareness and expression.
The closing question
The article asks whether the elementary curriculum pivots around those competences, or is instead "siloed with unnecessary mechanical routines" that push pupils toward becoming either street smart or in-class smart. It names the aim as people with a noble reason for existing, compassion toward others and the environment, and ends on the Schweitzer line.
