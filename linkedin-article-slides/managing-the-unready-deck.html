Managing the Unready: Technology, Dependency, and the Illusion of Progress in Indonesia

Indonesia adopts technology rapidly as a consumer and invents little of it, and that pattern signals reliance rather than capability. Falling behind carries real costs, but so does boarding unready — premature AI adoption in a culturally unprepared society produces dependency, not progress. The remedy is not technical. It is education, and specifically the critical thinking without which digital inclusion produces passive consumers.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

Indonesia adopts technology quickly as a consumer and invents little of it.

The article's own abstract frames it as examining the prerequisites for fostering an innovative and technologically advanced nation.

Situation

"Indonesia has historically not been an early adopter in production or technological invention and is often regarded as a laggard in industrial innovation."

Complication

"Although the nation rapidly embraces new technologies as a consumer—evident in its early and widespread adoption of digital platforms and artificial intelligence—this pattern signals a reliance on external advancements rather than domestic creation." "Furthermore, the utilization of AI in Indonesia primarily centers on consumption rather than production or invention."

Question

If adoption is fast and invention is slow, does accelerating adoption close the gap or widen it?

Answer

"This reality raises important considerations, as technological progress alone does not guarantee sustainable development." What determines whether innovation benefits society is "the individuals behind its application—critical thinking, value-based decision-making, and the capacity for broad sense-making".

The Flashback

The Javanese caution against gumun applies exactly here.

Apprehension about technological change is old, and so is the corrective.

"Historical examples, such as the printing press disrupting established social hierarchies, industrial automation in the 1800s raising concerns about job displacement, and the calculator altering mathematics education in the 1970s, demonstrate that apprehension over technological change is not new. The current rise of AI has triggered similar debates."

"The progression of technology cannot be halted. Resistance to technological change is ultimately ineffective; instead, adaptation and openness to transformation are crucial." The article is not arguing for refusal.

But it is arguing against dazzlement. AI is not recent — the term was introduced by John McCarthy at the 1956 Dartmouth Conference, following Alan Turing's foundational work in 1950. "In this context, the Javanese concept of “gumun” (Irawanto & Ramsey, 2011)—which cautions against superficial amazement and uncritical trend-following—remains relevant."

The line the article borrows for it

"As Mark Twain observed, “It ain't what you don't know that gets you into trouble. It's what you know for sure that just ain't so” (Stone, 2019)."

Two Risks

Missing the boat and boarding it unready are both real risks.

The article sets out both, and does not treat either as the safe option.

If a Country Misses the Boat
✗Geopolitical: superior AI capability confers strategic advantage and shifts the balance
✗Cultural erosion: adoption can dilute traditional practices, values and identities
✗New forms of imperialism: reinforced power asymmetries and technological dependency
✗Cybersecurity: greater vulnerability to algorithm manipulation and data poisoning
✗Domino effect: one nation's breach cascading into regional or global instability
If It Boards Unready
✗Economic disparity: infrastructure costs widen the gap rather than closing it
✗Technological dependency: reliance on externally developed AI reduces sovereignty
✗Job displacement without effective retraining, deepening social inequality
✗Ethical exposure: biased algorithms and privacy violations under weak regulation
✗Knowledge, skills and data gaps that constrain the ability to benefit at all
Why technical measures are not the answer

"Mitigating these risks demands more than technical solutions. While robust cybersecurity, responsible AI governance, and international cooperation are essential (Trattner et al., 2021), such measures are insufficient without addressing foundational issues." The article's three adaptation patterns — path-following, stage-skipping and path-creating (Kang et al., 2020) — are choices about capability, not about procurement.

Priorities

Eleven initiatives, and the caveat that four of them must come first.

The article proposes eleven early-education initiatives, then narrows them. "It may take one or two generations, but we must equip future generations with the necessary skills and mindset for technological advancements…"

1  ·  Investing in early childhood education
"Supporting early childhood education programs that nurture creativity, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills, with a special focus on introducing STEM subjects to young learners." Named as a first priority — foundational, with the greatest long-term impact.
2  ·  Ensuring equal access to quality education
Policies to bridge the urban–rural gap and prioritise marginalised communities. Also named as a first priority: together with early childhood education it nurtures core cognitive skills "and reducing systemic disparities at the outset".
3  ·  Improving educational infrastructure
The third priority. "Without adequate facilities and technological resources, the delivery of effective education and training will remain constrained."
4  ·  Empowering teachers through professional development
The fourth. "Skilled educators are essential for successful integration of technology and pedagogical reform."
5  ·  Promoting digital inclusion
Also placed among the immediate priorities: "Bridging the digital divide must be addressed concurrently to ensure all citizens, regardless of geography or socioeconomic status, benefit from technological advancements." — but see the section that follows.
6  ·  The remaining six, sequenced after
Technical and vocational education aligned to emerging industries; addressing basic needs such as nutrition and healthcare; recognising indigenous knowledge and local innovations; public-private partnerships; research and development; and support for entrepreneurship. "These measures lay the groundwork for other initiatives, enabling the subsequent development of technical and vocational education, research and development, and entrepreneurship support."

The Caveat

Digital inclusion without critical thinking produces passive consumers, not creators.

The sharpest turn in the article, and the one that qualifies its own recommendation.

"While promoting digital inclusion is essential, doing so without simultaneously developing critical thinking skills exposes citizens to significant risks." Three follow.

"Vulnerability to Digital Imperialism: Without critical thinking, citizens may become passive consumers of digital products and services dominated by foreign technology firms, particularly those from Silicon Valley. This dependence can lead to a form of digital imperialism, where national identity, autonomy, and even economic value are subsumed by external interests."

"Susceptibility to Misinformation and Manipulation" follows from the same absence. And "Limited Innovation Capacity: Digitization alone does not guarantee innovation. Without the skills to question, create, and problem-solve, the population risks remaining consumers rather than contributors or creators in the digital economy."

"Therefore, prioritizing digital inclusion must be accompanied by deliberate efforts to foster critical thinking and digital literacy. Failure to do so may undermine national sovereignty and perpetuate dependence on external technological powers."

Conclusion

Invest in people first, because innovation starts in the classroom not the marketplace.

The article closes on a series of provoking questions and answers each one.

Invest in people first: education, critical thinking, digital literacy, and cybersecurity.

— Managing the Unready: Technology, Dependency, and the Illusion of Progress in Indonesia
On adoption without foundations
"What happens when a nation prioritizes digital adoption but neglects to foster critical thinking and foundational skills?" — "The result is a population of passive technology consumers—vulnerable to digital imperialism, external influence, and manipulation—rather than a generation of innovators and creators."
On repeating the late-adopter trajectory
"Can Indonesia afford to repeat the late-adopter trajectory in the era of artificial intelligence and rapid digitalization?" — "No. Remaining a technological laggard in production and invention only deepens dependency, narrows economic opportunity, and risks surrendering national autonomy to dominant global platforms."
On where innovation actually begins
"Because sustainable innovation starts in the classroom, not in the marketplace. Without foundational investment in education, any gains in technology use remain superficial and fragile." And short-term fixes will not do: "Only a deliberate, multi-generational strategy—focused on continuous learning, adaptive policy, and inclusive innovation—will prepare Indonesia for the uncertainties and opportunities ahead."
