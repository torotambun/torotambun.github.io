When a Man Who Walks by Choice Meets a Man Who Walks by Life: An Autoethnographic Note from Menteng

The encounter produced no theory about the Baduy, and this piece refuses to supply one. What it exposed was my own approach — that urban life trains initiative until persistence begins to feel like virtue, and that one polite refusal should have been enough. The sequence of boundaries is the finding, not the man I met.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

Trust did not begin in abstraction; it began after a refusal.

Yesterday, on a pedestrian path in Menteng, walking with two former students and talking about innovation and life after graduation.

Situation

A Baduy gentleman sat under an old beringin tree in front of an old hotel, his young boy pressed closely beside him, four bottles of wild honey on the ground in front of them. A cool pocket along a busy stretch near crowded restaurants, where at night the same pavement is often used by people sleeping there.

Complication

I approached out of curiosity and began with the most ordinary question: how much was the honey. I tried to bargain the price down to Rp150,000. He said no. I then asked what margin he actually hoped to make from one bottle, given the effort of bringing it all the way there.

Question

He mentioned a number that, to me, sounded fair, and I paid him the original price he had asked. Only after that did I ask his name. Why does that sequence matter?

Answer

"Trust did not begin in abstraction. It emerged after an exchange, after refusal, and after I stopped trying to reduce the value of his effort." Autoethnographic writing weakens when it leaps from event to abstraction; meaning is often carried by pacing and transition rather than by conclusion.

Field Notes

The observations disrupted an urban expectation I had not admitted to carrying.

What I noticed, recorded as observation and nothing more.

OBSERVED A
His feet looked unexpectedly clean
After a journey that may have covered well over 100 kilometres on foot, the detail did not fit what a Jakarta habit of mind had already prepared to see.
OBSERVED B
His teeth were strikingly white
A second detail in the same direction. Recorded here because it is what I saw, not because it licenses any inference about diet, health, or a way of life.
OBSERVED C
There was none of the body odor one might lazily expect
The article's own word is lazily. The expectation was mine, and it was operating before I had named it.
OBSERVED D
Four bottles in front of him, ten brought in total
I asked why bring only four after coming all that way. He answered that he had brought ten and kept the rest somewhere else. "That reply grounded the encounter in logistics rather than symbolism." "It was a man managing quantity, distance, heat, movement, and place in a city that was not his own."
What these observations do not authorise

"These were only observations. I am careful to state them that way. They do not authorize me to make biological or cultural claims that the encounter itself cannot support." What they do reveal is that "my own urban expectation was disrupted before I had even fully admitted that I was carrying one."

The First Boundary

The first boundary was set by me, not by him.

At one point I asked whether I could visit his village. To my surprise, he did not reject the idea. He was welcoming.

But almost immediately I withdrew the question myself. I changed my mind on the spot. "What crossed my mind was not fear, but embarrassment."

I did not want to become one more urban visitor arriving with curiosity wrapped in moral vanity, one more Instagram tourist treating the Baduy as material for display, consuming difference and then returning home with a polished story about authenticity.

"This was not yet an interpretation of him. It was an exposure of myself." "So in that moment, the first boundary was set not by him, but by my own refusal to turn his openness into my access."

The Whisper

What mattered was not the answer but the form of its disclosure.

I then asked, carefully and politely, about a formal category which citizens here are still required to declare on official identity documents.

I asked partly because questions of formal identity had already appeared in public reporting about communities like his, and partly because the state still demands such a declaration in the first place — a requirement that reflects an older and more backward administrative order, one that modern societies ought to have outgrown.

He did not refuse the question. He answered it. But he did so quietly, leaning close enough to whisper into my ear.

He could have refused. He could have answered vaguely. He could have kept his distance. Instead he lowered his voice and gave a stranger a sensitive answer quietly, almost under protection. "What mattered was not only the content of the answer, but the form of its disclosure. In that whisper there was, at least briefly, a glimpse of trust."

What is withheld here, and why

"I leave the content aside here. He gave that answer in confidence, and I do not want the article to violate the very trust that made the moment significant." The answer is not stated in the article and is not stated in this deck.

The Second Refusal

What unsettled me was my own difficulty in letting one refusal be enough.

I invited him to join us for lunch at a nearby restaurant. He declined, politely and simply. That should have been enough.

But after entering the restaurant, I asked my assistant to go back and invite him once more. Again, he declined. Again politely. Again clearly.

"What unsettled me later was not his refusal alone, but my own difficulty in letting one refusal remain sufficient."

Only later did another thought become possible. Perhaps I would have done the same. Had I been alone in a city not my own, approached by a stranger and invited into an expensive restaurant, I too might have declined. "Caution would not have meant ingratitude. Distance would not have meant rejection. It might simply have meant proportion."

What It Revealed

Urban life trains initiative so deeply that it begins to imagine itself as virtue.

This is where the encounter moves beyond scene into analysis — and the subject of the analysis is me, not him.

How the Habit Presents Itself
•"To approach is good. To invite is good. To persist is often treated as sincerity."
•Good intention is assumed to justify another step forward
•A second invitation reads as warmth rather than as pressure
•Curiosity presents itself as respect for the other person
•The initiative is never examined, because it feels generous
What It Can Quietly Become
✗"Yet that habit can quietly become a form of intrusion."
✗Openness gets converted into access without anyone granting it
✗One polite refusal is treated as an opening position rather than an answer
✗Curiosity becomes a claim upon another person
✗"Autoethnography is not license for projection."
The sequence that is the actual finding

"I remember the sequence of boundaries. I stepped forward. Then I stepped back. He stepped forward in trust. Then he stepped back in refusal." Somewhere inside that exchange what appeared was not romance, not purity, and not a usable symbol for urban self-critique, but proportion, trust and limit.

What I Carry

Not every openness is permission, and not every polite refusal should be tested twice.

In a city where approach, display, speed and performance so often dominate social life, that brief exchange felt like a correction.

not every openness is permission, not every curiosity deserves entry, and not every polite refusal should be tested twice

— When a Man Who Walks by Choice Meets a Man Who Walks by Life
What the encounter did not produce
"The encounter therefore did not leave me with a grand theory about the Baduy. One brief meeting does not give that right." Not because his world should be romanticized as our moral cure, and not because he existed to teach me anything.
What he actually did
"The Baduy man did not lecture me. He did not rebuke me. He did not turn the moment into a moral lesson." He answered what he was willing to answer, accepted one possibility, trusted me with one sensitive disclosure, and declined another invitation twice.
Where the piece finally belongs
"not as a claim to explain the Baduy, but as an autoethnographic account of how one encounter exposed the terms by which I myself had approached it in the first place"
