Design Thinking as a Generic Tool: The Cultural Prerequisites

Design thinking's method is not what decides whether it works — unmet basic needs and a hierarchical culture foreclose the blank-slate mindset the method assumes, so without dignified work and a supporting culture, design thinking is reduced to a management gimmick wearing a method's clothes.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Design thinking is celebrated as a universal method, but its effectiveness depends on who is using it and what surrounds them.

The piece states its own claim before making the case for it.

Design thinking is often celebrated as a universal framework for creativity and problem-solving. However, its effectiveness depends not just on the method itself but on the people using it, the surrounding context, and the structural foundations that support it. Without these prerequisites, design thinking risks becoming a superficial exercise.

The piece explores the cultural and structural elements critical to design thinking's success, showing why its impact hinges on good sense-making, supportive environments, and deliberate practice.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

Unmet basic needs foreclose the sense-making design thinking demands, before any workshop begins.

Before creativity or a blank slate can even be discussed, a harder question comes first.

How can people think critically or creatively if their basic needs are unmet? Malnutrition and poverty directly impact cognitive abilities, making it nearly impossible for individuals to engage in the kind of sense-making or open-mindedness that design thinking demands.

"One cannot think well, love well, sleep well, if one has not dined well" — Virginia Woolf.

The solution is not as simple as providing free food. While charity might alleviate immediate hunger, it does little to address the deeper, systemic issues that perpetuate poverty. The real answer lies in creating dignified jobs — jobs that allow parents to put food on the table and feel a sense of pride and purpose.

"Food in the end, in our own tradition, is something holy. It's not about nutrients and calories. it's about sharing. It's about honesty. It's about identity." — Louise Fresco. Without this foundation, design thinking remains an elitist concept, inaccessible to those grappling with survival.

Details

A blank slate is not an absence of knowledge — it is a psychological readiness that hierarchy and hunger both foreclose.

Critics are right that design thinking requires a blank slate. The piece's argument is about what makes that state possible.

Achieving the mental freedom to think creatively, wildly, boldly, and daringly is only possible in cultures and systems that address basic needs and promote curiosity, individuality, and constructive rebellion. Education and economic opportunities play critical roles here.

In cultures where hierarchy dominates and obedience is emphasized, design thinking struggles to take root. Where parent-child relationships are revered over adult-adult relationships, questioning authority is discouraged, and voicing dissent in classrooms is taboo — the clean-slate approach design thinking desires becomes almost impossible to adopt.

Rebellion, healthy competition, and the courage to question established norms are vital cultural traits for design thinking to flourish. But even these traits cannot emerge in environments plagued by hunger or desperation.

Evidence

Edgar Schein's shared assumptions decide whether a culture's unseen framework helps or stifles the process.

Culture is not atmosphere — it is the operating logic design thinking either fits or fights.

As defined by Edgar Schein, culture is the set of shared assumptions that a group has developed over time to address external challenges and internal integration. These shared assumptions shape how people think, act, and interact within a group, forming the unseen framework that drives behavior.

The principle of "defer judgment," central to design thinking, requires a culture where participants feel safe expressing unconventional ideas without fear of ridicule. In hierarchical settings, where questioning authority is discouraged, this principle often fails. Similarly, the concept that "enlightened trial and error outperforms the planning of the lone genius" clashes with cultures that celebrate individual achievement over collective innovation.

Implications

Four failure modes recur when the prerequisites are missing, and each is a different way the method goes hollow.

Without the right cultural and structural foundations, design thinking risks becoming ineffective for these reasons.

1  ·  Deficient basic needs
Hunger, poverty, and a lack of dignity prevent individuals from reaching their creative potential. Design thinking cannot thrive where survival is the priority.
2  ·  Deficient sense-making
Individuals lacking intellectual curiosity and critical thinking skills struggle to generate meaningful insights, reducing design thinking to a hollow exercise.
3  ·  Inhibited collaboration
Cultures rooted in assumptions that prioritize individualism or enforce strict hierarchies hinder the collective ideation essential for design thinking, which thrives on team-oriented innovation.
4  ·  Superficial adoption
Organizations treating design thinking as a trendy buzzword often fail to address the deeper cultural transformations needed — fostering curiosity, embracing experimentation, tolerating failure — and the method devolves into a performative exercise.

Conclusion

Without dignified work and structural investment, design thinking is a management gimmick wearing a method's clothes.

The piece closes on the same three conditions it opened with.

Design thinking is a powerful tool, but its success relies on the people, culture, and structures supporting it. Addressing basic needs is non-negotiable — without dignified jobs and opportunities that restore pride and purpose, the creativity and boldness design thinking requires remain out of reach for many.

Cultivating a blank slate mindset requires more than intellectual effort; it demands cultural, educational, and economic systems that promote curiosity, encourage questioning, and support psychological safety. Without these critical elements, design thinking risks being reduced to yet another management gimmick.
