Beyond Shame: Ethical Dimensions of Mindsets in Corruption

The growth mindset is treated everywhere as an unqualified good, and this piece finds a reinforcing negative loop inside it. Shame is what should deter wrongdoing, and the growth mindset's characteristic strength is fluency in recovering from shame, so the trait that makes a person resilient also makes a corruptor recoverable. The argument runs through Benedict's shame and guilt cultures, placed by Koentjaraningrat in Indonesia and mapped onto older and newer regions of the brain. Its resolution is that mindset alone predicts nothing: what decides behaviour is the combination of mindset with either an upheld ethics or a surrender to instinct, which is what the 2x2 is for.

Opening

The growth mindset is celebrated, and it makes shame easier to survive, including for a corruptor.

The piece opens by naming its own paradox rather than building to it. Relating shame to Carol Dweck's fixed and growth mindsets gives a useful account of how people react to their own failures, and then the same framework turns on itself.

The mechanism is stated in one sentence and it is uncomfortable by design. The more a person holds a growth mindset, the more fluently they handle shamefulness and recover from the shame itself, which is exactly the quality that makes the trait desirable.

Applied to corruption, that fluency stops being a virtue. The article's claim is that the celebrated mindset's corruptor recovers more easily, and more victoriously, than someone conventionally written off as fixed mindset.

The loop is what the article calls it: a reinforcing negative feedback loop, in which the mechanism that should deter is dissolved by the trait everyone is being encouraged to build.

What this piece is

"This is an excerpt of my original article entitled “Value Driven Growth Mindset and Value Driven Fixed Mindset?”…" The original is a co-authored journal article; the deck carries the excerpt as published here, and the full reference is on the Sources slide.

The loop, in the article's own words

"The more growth mindset one is, the more able and fluent one handles the shamefulness / recover the shame itself." And on who benefits: "Because the so much adored growth mindset's corruptor will recover easily and more victoriously from their shame or failure compared with those who are traditionally doomed as being fixed mindset."

Framework

Benedict's shame culture and guilt culture divide on whether wrongness is felt when nobody sees.

The distinction comes from Ruth Benedict (1946), cited in the article through Koentjaraningrat (1969), and the dividing line is the presence or absence of an observer.

Shame culture
•The primary goal of work is position and rank
•A mistake is not a major concern while it goes unnoticed
•What matters is whether it leads to loss of face
•Attitude is unaffected and conscience undisturbed
•The feeling of wrongness does not arise from the wrongdoing itself
Guilt culture
•Wrongdoing is felt as wrong on its own terms
•Guilt arises whether or not superiors know
•Visibility to others is not the trigger
•The conscience continues to be disturbed
•The feeling attaches to the act rather than to exposure
Where the article places Indonesia

Koentjaraningrat (1969) is cited as holding that Indonesians predominantly adhere to a shame culture rather than a guilt culture, attributed to what he calls the priyayi mentality, in which being an employee shapes the approach to mistakes so that they count as serious only once a superior knows. The article notes that this can produce panic and potential mental disturbance when a mistake is exposed.

The Mechanism

Shame culture sits in the brain stem and guilt culture in the neocortex.

The article's next move takes the cultural distinction into neuroanatomy, and this is where its claim becomes physical rather than sociological. Shame culture is located in an older region functioning as a survival mechanism; guilt culture is associated with a relatively newer one.

The comparative figure it reaches for is the brain stem's overlap with another species, cited to Sagan and Druyan (1992), and it is offered as evidence that the responses involved are instinctive rather than considered.

The article then draws an inference from that mapping about the kinds of society in which each culture prevails. It is stated with hedges, twice, and those hedges are the author's own; they are reproduced below exactly as written rather than resolved into an assertion.

What the mapping is finally for is the argument about recovery. If shame is instinctive and older, then the capacity to process and move past it is a later, learned facility, which sets up the claim about who recovers and how fast.

The anatomical claim

"Shame culture is rooted in the brain stem, a much older part of the brain, functioning as a survival mechanism, whereas guilt culture is associated with the neocortex, a relatively newer region of the brain." The article adds that the brain stem's realm, governing instinctive responses, "shares a remarkable 96% similarity with the brain functions of chimpanzees" citing Sagan and Druyan (1992).

The inference the article draws from it, with its own hedges

"This similarity suggests that shame culture, regulated by these more primal brain areas, is characteristic of what might be considered less sophisticated societies. In contrast, guilt culture, linked to the more evolved neocortex, tends to be prevalent in societies deemed more sophisticated." The qualifications “might be considered” and “deemed” are the article's.

Details

A fixed mindset internalises failure as permanent deficiency, so shame lands hardest there.

Before the paradox can be stated, the article establishes what the two mindsets each do with a failure.

Fixed mindset
✗Failures internalised as personal deficiencies
✗Shortcomings read as evidence of an unchangeable self
✗Deep shame at failure, taken as proof of inadequacy
✗Avoidance of challenges
✗Lower resilience to shame
Growth mindset
✓Mistakes viewed as learning opportunities
✓Failure not read as a reflection of the core self
✓Less likely to experience debilitating shame
✓Constructive engagement with shortcomings
✓Failures used as catalysts for development
The qualification the article insists on

"A growth mindset does not make one immune to feeling shame in response to social judgment, failures, or mistakes." The difference is not exemption but processing: such individuals are better equipped to move past shame and to use it as a springboard.

The Paradox

Recovery fluency is a double-edged sword, and it may explain why a corruptor leaving prison is forgiven and forgotten.

Everything to this point reads as a case for cultivating the growth mindset in educational, professional and personal settings, and the article makes that case explicitly: shifting focus from inherent ability to effort and learning lets people use failure for development rather than as a source of shame.

Then it turns the same sentence over. The approach is called a double-edged sword, and the edge that cuts the other way is applied to the one population where society relies on shame doing its work.

The consequence is stated without softening, and it is the piece's sharpest line. A capacity built as resilience becomes, in this application, a capacity to be readmitted.

Nothing is proposed as a remedy here. The article's word is "may", and it leaves the observation standing as a problem for anyone recommending the growth mindset without asking what else is in place.

The claim, as written

"It may explain why individuals leaving penitentiaries after serving time for corruption can be forgiven or forgotten due to their apparent growth mindset traits."

The Model

Mindset alone predicts nothing, so the 2x2 crosses it with whether ethics hold or brain stem impulse wins.

The article's own contribution is a two-by-two placing the mindset axis against an ethical one, and it is the answer to the paradox rather than a summary of it.

1  ·  Growth mindset with upheld ethics and morale
Belief in personal development underpinned by strong adherence to ethical and moral values, producing what the article calls a dynamic yet principled approach to challenges and learning, adaptable without compromising integrity.
2  ·  Fixed mindset with upheld ethics and morale
A rigid perception of one's own abilities balanced by steadfast commitment to ethics. Reluctant to embrace change, but guided by strong values into consistent and principled decision-making.
3  ·  Growth mindset succumbing to brain stem impulse
Belief in one's capacity to grow, with actions driven by primal instinct. The article's concern here is behaviour that prioritises immediate instinctual response over ethical consideration, which is the quadrant the corruptor occupies.
4  ·  Fixed mindset succumbing to brain stem impulse
Resistance to growth combined with action driven by basic survival impulse. Lacking both the drive for development and moral restraint, the article describes this as potentially harmful, marked by stagnation and impulsivity.
What the model establishes

That neither mindset alone suffices to predict behaviour. The article's position is that it is the combination with an ethical compass or with instinctual drive that produces a complete picture, which is why a deck could not stop at the growth mindset and call it good.

Conclusion

Values are a deeper choice than mindset, and the article leaves open which combination is worse.

The closing passages are posed as questions rather than findings, and the article does not answer them.

Values, raison d'être, and purpose in life may be distinct from the concepts of growth and fixed mindsets, as they stem from a deeper, more conscious choice, as deep as brain stem.

— Beyond Shame: Ethical Dimensions of Mindsets in Corruption, 2 December 2023
What mindsets do not supply
Mindsets shape how challenges and learning are approached. Values and purpose supply the ethical framework and the motivation, which the article treats as a different order of thing rather than a further property of mindset.
The first question it leaves open
"Is it perhaps true that having a growth mindset without underlying values can lead to a sense of aimlessness and misery." The drive for improvement, unanchored by a moral compass, is held to lead to unethical choices and a lack of fulfilment.
The second
Or whether a fixed mindset devoid of values is worse still, producing rigidity and resistance to change without the principles needed to act ethically. The article states both and adjudicates neither.
What both share
The absence of values. The article's only firm conclusion is that either mindset without them produces a life lacking direction and moral grounding, which is why the 2x2 has an ethical axis at all.
