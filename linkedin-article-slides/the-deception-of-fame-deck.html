The Deception of Fame

A churchgoing student said plainly that he wanted to be famous, and it triggered a filter in use since 1997: is this driven by fear of not having enough, by the desire to be admired, or by compromise with a power other than God? Three figures in Acts failed on exactly those grounds. Fame is not harmless vanity. It masquerades as something desirable, and it is the stuff of judgment.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A student said he wanted to be famous, and my heart sank.

"Not long ago, I was speaking with a bright young student about his aspirations."

Situation

"In the middle of our conversation, he looked me in the eye and said plainly, “I want to be famous.”"

Complication

"In that moment, my heart sank." A deep pastoral grief — not anger but sorrow — for him and for many like him. "Here was a teenager who attends church regularly, yet his deepest ambition was not to be faithful or holy, but to be famous."

Question

"His comment triggered something in me. It took me back to 1997, when I began using a personal framework to discern my own life decisions at critical crossroads." What does that framework ask?

Answer

Three questions drawn from the wilderness temptations. "And as soon as I heard “I want to be famous,” I recognized that old second temptation rearing its head."

The Framework

Three questions from the wilderness have guarded the heart for decades.

At every major decision or tempting opportunity since, the article says, he asks himself three probing questions drawn from the wilderness temptations.

1  ·  Is this driven by fear of not having enough?
It echoes "Satan's temptation for Jesus to turn stones into bread – a lure to self-provision out of anxiety after 40 days of hunger" (Matthew 4:3–4).
2  ·  Is this driven by a desire to be famous or admired?
It reflects the temptation to throw himself from the temple pinnacle — "a stunt that would surely attract public awe" (Matthew 4:5–7).
3  ·  Is this pressured by a compromise?
It mirrors "Satan's offer of all the world's kingdoms if Jesus would only worship him, a temptation to gain power by bending the knee to evil" (Matthew 4:8–10). "These three questions – fear, fame, and false worship – have helped guard my heart for decades."
Why the second is the deceptive one

"Fame – that second question – is particularly deceptive because it masquerades as something harmless or even desirable (“I just want to make a difference, to be seen, to be appreciated”). But as we'll see, the pursuit of fame for its own sake is a deadly trap."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Wilderness

Even the Son of God was invited to chase fame and discerned the deception.

"Before we examine those human examples, we must start with Jesus."

"“If you are the Son of God,” Satan said, “throw yourself down [from the highest point of the temple]”" (Matthew 4:6). "Imagine the scene: the holy city, the crowds at worship, and Jesus floating down from the temple peak, borne up by angels. What a headline that would make!"

"The devil was enticing Jesus to dramatize His identity, to gain instant acclaim, and to test God's protection all at once. It was a temptation to fame and ego, a shortcut to messianic recognition without the cross."

"But Jesus would have none of it." "In other words, seeking attention and forcing God to save you for show is not trust – it's sin. Our Lord refused to seek fame on the devil's terms."

"Why start here? Because Jesus shows us that the craving to be spectacular – to be admired and famous – is a fundamental human temptation. Even the Son of God was invited to chase fame, but He discerned the deception and defeated it."

The Cases

Hypocrisy, ambition and self-deification are three faces of the same craving.

"The Bible gives us clear examples of how the desire for fame and glory can lead to spiritual ruin."

1  ·  Ananias and Sapphira — hypocrisy for recognition (Acts 5)
Having seen the esteem Barnabas received, "Perhaps they saw the esteem Barnabas received and wanted the same reputation.". "In other words, they wanted the glory of being thought sacrificial and holy, without actually being fully sacrificial. They craved the image of generosity more than the reality of integrity." "if we are giving, serving, or worshiping with a secret aim to be seen and admired by others, we are in spiritual danger."
2  ·  Simon the Sorcerer — ambition for power and prestige (Acts 8)
Before the gospel reached him, "Simon had built a brand around himself; he loved the applause and awe of the crowds". After baptism the old ambition resurfaced when he offered money for the power to impart the Spirit. "He wanted the Holy Spirit's power, but not for God's glory or the good of others – rather, so that he could regain his status as the great, impressive figure in town." Peter's answer named the root: "your heart is not right before God".
3  ·  Herod Agrippa — the folly of self-glorification (Acts 12)
The crowd shouted that his was the voice of a god, not of a man. "And crucially, Herod accepted this praise. He did not correct them or give glory to the true God. He basked in the adulation." "Here we see a blatant instance of what we might call the worship of fame – a man glorifying himself, receiving the worship of people who have effectively made him an idol." "This is the ultimate outcome of unchecked thirst for fame: it destroys a person from the inside out."
What Simon's case shows that the others do not

"It is possible to be baptized, to be in the church, and yet to be entangled in the old lust for fame and influence." The desire to be famous "can survive even after we turn to Christ, unless we truly allow the Holy Spirit to change our heart's ambitions." "We must continually return to the question: “Am I doing this for Jesus' fame or my own?”"

The Present

Value is not visibility, whatever the platform teaches.

"By now it should be clear that the desire to “be famous” – to elevate ourselves – is a deadly deception. It promises significance but delivers spiritual bankruptcy."

"It tempted Jesus, it corrupted a Christian couple, it nearly derailed a new believer, and it destroyed a king. Fame itself is fleeting; worse, the pursuit of fame can cost us our soul."

The article puts the ancient question in current terms: "What good is it to gain a million followers, or a wall full of awards, or the admiration of our peers, if in the process our heart turns from God?"

"My heart aches for that student who wanted to be famous, and for countless youths in a culture where being a celebrity is held as the ultimate goal. Many of them attend church regularly; they have an inherited form of religion but perhaps not a deeply rooted heart-relationship with Christ." "They are, in a sense, wandering in the wilderness, bombarded by Satan's offers – identity in possessions, identity in popularity, identity in power."

"Let's intercede for a generation at risk of believing the serpent's lie that says, “Your value is in your visibility.” We must counter that with the truth: our value is in being known and loved by God."

Conclusion

The only fame worth seeking is well done, good and faithful servant.

"First, I believe the Lord is calling each of us to repent of any way in which the deception of fame has taken hold of our hearts. Let's be honest: this temptation isn't just for other people out there; it hits us."

All other applause will fade, but the honor that comes from God is eternal.

— The Deception of Fame
The three self-examinations
"Have you found yourself making decisions out of the fear of missing out or not having enough, rather than trusting God's provision? Have you caught yourself doing things – even good things – mainly to be seen and admired by others? Are you under pressure to compromise your values to get ahead in your career, to bow to an “idol” of success or popularity?"
Why the framework ends in grace
"The good news is that because Jesus triumphed over these temptations, we can repent and find forgiveness and freedom. His victory in the wilderness is ours to claim." "There is grace waiting for the heart that turns from self-glorification…"
The inversion the kingdom runs on
In God's kingdom "the greatest is the servant, and the one who humbles himself will be exalted in due time" (Matthew 23:11–12). "The only fame worth seeking is to hear the Lord say," in the words of Matthew 25:21, "Well done, good and faithful servant".
