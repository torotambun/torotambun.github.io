LinkedIn Article  ·  Academic Culture · Publishing, Readership and the Teaching Relationship  ·  21 December 2023

Beyond the Ivory Tower: Confronting Intolerance in Academic Culture

A year-end accounting of the author's own output, written against a label some academicians have applied to it. The article's move is not to defend popular writing on its merits but to show, through a long passage from Johansson, that the academic alternative rests on a weak foundation: papers multiplied as a side effect of mass enrolment, most are never read, and writing for a broad audience is rational for society while being penalised in a career. Having established that, the article declines to argue with its critics and relocates the question entirely, to the teaching relationship, which is the subject of the companion piece it published the day before.

Opening

Sixteen Jakarta Post columns and eighty-six LinkedIn articles in one year, and some academicians call it pseudo-science.

The article opens with a year-end count and the judgement attached to it, in that order.

The tally is given precisely and with its date attached: in 2023, sixteen articles in the Jakarta Post, eighty-six self-published articles on LinkedIn, over five hundred and fifty posts, and two published through Amazon with more in the pipeline. The article calls the result a rich database of thoughts and insights.

It also names the category the work falls into, using the term it will later find in Johansson. This is writing for the masses, and the article concedes directly that it may not hold traditional academic weight.

The label follows immediately, and the article does not soften it or leave it implied. It is reported as a description of his own work and as the condition the rest of the piece is written against.

The count, as the article gives it

"In 2023, I had the privilege of seeing 16 of my articles published in the Jakarta Post, a milestone that fills me with gratitude." The LinkedIn figures, 86 articles and over 550 posts, are stated in the following sentence.

The label

"Some academicians have labeled my writings as pseudo-science, a perspective I humbly understand, even though I believe there's value in all forms of expression."

The Response

I decline the argument, because the writing was never for recognition.

The article's answer to the label is a refusal to contest it, and it gives a reason rather than leaving the refusal as composure. The decision not to engage is attributed to contentment and self-assurance in a post-corporate life, which locates the indifference in circumstance rather than in disdain.

The purpose of the writing is restated to make the refusal coherent. If the work were for readership, a judgement about its standing would matter; the article says the value is in the process of expressing the ideas and in centralising his own thinking.

The judgement itself is then met with something other than rebuttal. The article describes viewing such judgements with empathy, on the understanding that everyone's path to success and happiness is unique, which is the position from which the following section can examine the critics' own system without hostility.

Why the label does not bite

"It's not about seeking readership or followers; it's about the personal fulfillment I gain from expressing my ideas."

The Diagnosis

The academy Johansson describes rewards publication and not teaching, and the papers multiplied as a side effect of mass enrolment.

The article reproduces a long passage from Johansson and then restates it in its own words. The account below is Johansson's; the use made of it is the article's.

The starting point is historical. A century ago, on Johansson's account, science was done by wealthy men doing it as a hobby, or by university teachers hired primarily to teach who researched in their spare time. Very few people now do research without being paid to do exactly that.

The hiring criterion moved with it. Universities now select professors primarily on their track record as researchers, and professors worldwide accordingly see research as their prime duty, with teaching carrying a load rather than defining the post.

The volume follows from enrolment rather than from discovery. As university and college enrolment rose in the developed world, a large number of university teachers were hired, all competing for better positions and promotion, and since the main merit in academia is publication, effort went there. Johansson's conclusion is that the increase in papers is to a considerable extent a side effect of the increase in basic academic education.

The article restates this in the register of its own argument, calling modern academia a highly competitive arena where research takes precedence, and asking whether the majority of academic output is truly impactful.

Evidence

Most papers are never read, so writing for the masses is the rational act society needs and the irrational one a career punishes.

This is the passage the article's whole frame depends on, and it is what turns a personal defence into an argument.

FINDING A
The readership claim
"there is strong evidence that the great majority of scientific papers are never read by anyone else than the referees and the journal editor." The support offered is that the majority of papers are never cited, and that of those cited, only around twenty percent are actually read by the citer.
FINDING B
The threshold it proposes
Johansson's inference is that only when a paper is cited five times or more can there be some confidence it has actually been read. The counter-argument, that many papers are read without the reader finding reason to quote them, is raised and answered: if a scientist reads a paper and has no reason to refer to it later, it plausibly had little impact on that researcher's thinking.
FINDING C
The asymmetry at the centre of it
Writing for a broader audience would promote the general level of scientific understanding in society, which the passage calls clearly rational from a societal point of view. From the individual researcher's point of view it is not, because "writing for a broader audience is not valued in academia."
FINDING D
The penalty, named by a journalist
Johansson quotes Nick Kristof in the New York Times of 15 February 2014 to the effect that where peer-reviewed publication is the condition of academic success, academics who spend their time writing for the masses will be penalised. Kristof's phrase for that spending of time is set in his own scare quotes.
What this does to the label

The article never returns to answer the pseudo-science charge directly. It does not need to: if the system issuing the label produces work that is largely unread, the label describes standing within that system rather than value outside it.

Implications

The remedy is not a metric but the mentoring relationship, which is the subject of the piece I published yesterday.

The article's conclusion on publishing is posed as a choice rather than a verdict. Should the focus remain narrowly on advancing individual careers, or should there be greater emphasis on contributing to societal knowledge? It proposes redefining the metrics of academic success and encouraging outreach beyond the academic community as steps worth taking.

Then it declines to stop there. Having asked where the question goes, the article answers that it returns to something the metrics cannot reach, and the answer is a relationship rather than a policy.

It points at its own companion piece for the argument. Bridging Ideals and Reality, published the previous day, is named in the text as the place where the case is made, and the article summarises its purpose as calling teachers to find and fulfil their callings. The two pieces are written as a pair and each names the other's territory.

The turn, in the article's own words

"It will return to the intention and the original formula of learning process: the relationship, the mentoring between the teacher and the student."

Conclusion

Philosophy began when people asked how we ought to live, and that is still the question.

The article ends on a passage from Bryan Magee, introduced as its final note, and the passage's own sequence carries the point.

philosophy begins when human beings start trying to understand the world, not through religion or by accepting authority but through the use of reason.

— Bryan Magee, quoted in Beyond the Ivory Tower, 21 December 2023
Where it starts
With the early Greeks, in the sixth, fifth and fourth centuries BC, and with questions about what the world is made of and what holds it up.
Where it turns
With Socrates, who on Magee's account said that "what matters most is how we ought to live." The subject moves from the composition of the world to the conduct of a life.
Why it closes the piece here
The article has just argued that academic publishing has lost sight of what its output is for. Magee's sequence supplies the older standard: the discipline began by asking how to live, not by asking how to be counted.
What is left standing
Not a rebuttal of the pseudo-science label, which the article never attempts, but a different question about what writing is for, addressed to the same readers who issued it.
