Evolution of Management Thoughts: Very Personal Reflection

The companion piece to the same day's taxonomy, this article arranges management thought by era rather than by scope, and the arrangement produces an argument the taxonomy cannot. Management did not begin with Taylor: craft workshops managed by families, guilds regulating quality and price, and military and monastic orders supplying the first hierarchies and the first record-keeping all precede the factory. Industrialisation made management a separate activity, and every era since has corrected the one before it. Scientific management optimised the task and was criticised for treating workers as machines. Classical theory structured the organisation and was criticised as mechanistic. Human relations restored the person. Systems and contingency added the environment, the strategy era added position, and the modern era added technology and sustainability. Because the article records each era's criticism alongside its contribution, the sequence reads forward as progress and backward as a chain of corrections to the previous overcorrection.

Introduction

The history is written for entrepreneurs, on the argument that old theories are a toolkit rather than a museum.

The article opens on its audience rather than its subject, asking what a student of entrepreneurship gains from the history of management thought, and the answers are practical throughout.

The case is cumulative: "By investing the time to understand the history and evolution of management theories, entrepreneurs can better equip themselves to deal with the challenges and opportunities that come their way."

Ten benefits are listed, each paired with a named framework. Strategic decision-making through Porter's Five Forces. Problem-solving through Maslow or Herzberg. Adaptability through agile methodologies. Human capital management through McGregor's Theory X and Theory Y. Cultural sensitivity through Schein's model of organisational culture.

And the last is the one that justifies a historical survey rather than a current-practice guide: "a historical perspective can sometimes spark innovation. Entrepreneurs might find that combining elements from different theories leads to new, more effective management strategies tailored to their unique business needs."

Framework

Management existed long before industry, in workshops, guilds, monasteries and armies.

The article calls this period "a fascinating and often overlooked chapter in the history of management thought", and the point of recovering it is that the earliest practices look nothing like the ones that followed.

1  ·  Craftsmanship and apprenticeship
Master craftsmen produced goods in homes or small workshops and took apprentices. Management was "informal and mostly family-based", and the craftsmen were "not only skilled laborers but also marketers, managers, and entrepreneurs rolled into one". Training was mentor to mentee rather than employer to employee, with apprentices often living in the household.
2  ·  What that arrangement made central
Resource allocation was "typically a family affair, often including discussions around the dinner table about how to allocate time, materials, and labor." Human relations were decisive, since "Loyalty, family ties, and community standing were all integral to the management process." And because reputation was everything, ethics and quality were not separable from business.
3  ·  Guild systems
Guilds "functioned as early regulatory bodies that set standards for quality and pricing", with membership signalling credibility. They standardised training, with guild examinations as a rite of passage, and decided collectively: "Within guilds, decision-making was more democratic, as members would gather to make important decisions affecting their trade."
4  ·  Military and religious organisations
These "offered some of the earliest examples of hierarchical structures, chains of command, and formalized roles." Monastic record-keeping of "agricultural yields, resources, and labor" is identified as an early form of resource management, and the strict rules of both later shaped administrative and bureaucratic styles.
What was lost when the factory arrived

"the period before industrialization had management practices that were deeply rooted in social and community structures, emphasizing close human relations, quality of work, and ethical considerations." Each of those three is something a later era has to rediscover.

Details

The factory separated managing from doing, and everything afterward follows from that split.

The Industrial Revolution is treated as the structural break rather than as one era among several, and the article covers the European and American versions separately.

Before: the workshop
•Dispersed workshops, production crafted piece by piece
•The craftsman managed production and business together
•Decisions shaped by family, season and local demand
•Quality guaranteed by personal reputation
•Training as a live-in mentor relationship
After: the factory
•Centralised labour and resources, mass-produced identical goods
•Managers specialising in coordination, quality control and supervision
•Division of labour narrowing what any one worker did
•Appalling conditions, child labour, very long hours
•Laissez-faire capitalism leaving business largely self-regulated
The three pioneers the article names before Taylor

Robert Owen, who "experimented with improving working conditions at his cotton mills in New Lanark, Scotland" and advocated humane conditions and education for workers. Charles Babbage, who contributed through specialisation and division of labour. And Andrew Ure, whose work "emphasized the technical aspects of factory production but also outlined the necessity for a new type of management focused on machine operation and process efficiency."

Where the ethics of management begin

"This era ignited discussions around the ethical dimensions of management, especially in terms of labor relations and working conditions." The article notes the American variant diverged, seeing "a relatively earlier onset of government regulation aimed at curbing monopolistic practices and ensuring worker safety."

Evidence

Each era answers the previous one, and the article records what each was criticised for.

This is what makes the survey usable rather than decorative: no era is presented as settled, and every contribution carries its objection.

1900–1930
Scientific management
Taylor's The Principles of Scientific Management (1911), with the Gilbreths on motion studies and therbligs and Gantt on the chart. The criticism is recorded immediately: it faced criticism "for treating workers like machines and ignoring the human elements like job satisfaction, motivation, and group dynamics".
1900s–1930s
Classical management theory
Fayol's Administration Industrielle et Générale (1916) with five functions and fourteen principles, and Weber's Economy and Society (1922) on bureaucracy and rational-legal authority. Both were criticised "for being overly mechanistic and for not accounting for human needs and social influences in the workplace."
1930–1950
Human relations movement
Mayo and the Hawthorne Studies. The article positions it explicitly as a reply: it "emerged as a counterpoint to the scientific and classical approaches to management, which were criticized for neglecting the human and social aspects of work", and it founded the modern field of organisational behaviour.
Mid 20th c.
Systems and contingency
von Bertalanffy's General System Theory (1968) treating organisations as systems affected by external environments, and Fiedler's A Theory of Leadership Effectiveness (1967) holding that leadership effectiveness depends on situational contingencies. Deming's Out of the Crisis (1986) and Hammer and Champy's Reengineering the Corporation (1993) follow in the same tradition.
Late 20th c.
The strategy era
Criticised in turn on two grounds: that "an overemphasis on competitive positioning can lead companies to neglect other important aspects, such as organizational culture and employee well-being", and that its frameworks are "sometimes criticized for their static nature, as they often don't account for the dynamic aspects of today's business environments."
2000–present
The modern era
Mintzberg's Strategy Safari (1998) on emergent strategy, McGrath and MacMillan's The Entrepreneurial Mindset (2000), and Kim Scott's Radical Candor (2017). Technology integration, ethical and sustainable practice, agile and lean management, and distributed leadership. Criticised for "technology for technology's sake" and for agility at the cost of long-term planning.

Implications

A second pass sorts the same history by perspective, and that is where culture enters.

The article's closing section re-reads everything it has just covered through a different lens, organising by perspective rather than by date, which surfaces two traditions the chronological account leaves implicit.

The symbolic-interpretive perspective is the first. Geertz and Michael Pacanowsky are named together for a focus "on culture as a system of shared symbols and meanings", with Geertz's The Interpretation of Cultures (1973) as the work, and Schein placed alongside them.

The second is organisational learning. Gareth Morgan's Images of Organization (1986) supplies metaphors, reading organisations as "Machines, Organisms, Brains, Cultures, Political Systems, Psychic Prisons, Flux and Transformation, and Instruments of Domination".

And Argyris and Schön supply the mechanism, distinguishing "between learning that allows organizations to maintain existing policies and assumptions (single-loop) and learning that leads to a change in policies and assumptions (double-loop)." That distinction is what turns the whole history from a list into an account of how a field corrects itself.

How the article summarises its own four perspectives

Classical: structure, planning and efficiency, through Fayol and Weber. Modern: environmental context, adaptability, quality and process, through von Bertalanffy, Fiedler, Deming, Hammer and Champy. Symbolic-interpretive: culture, language and meaning-making, through Geertz, Pacanowsky and Schein. And organisational learning, through Argyris and Schön.

Conclusion

What the era-by-era structure delivers is a field that keeps correcting itself.

The value of the survey is not the chronology but the pattern the chronology exposes, which is that each era's contribution is the previous era's blind spot.

Overall, this era captures the complexity and dynamism of contemporary management, offering both new tools and new questions for the future of organizational leadership.

— Evolution of Management Thoughts, 5 September 2023
The pattern, stated as the article builds it
Scientific management optimised the task and forgot the person. Classical theory structured the organisation and was called mechanistic. Human relations restored the person. Systems and contingency admitted the environment. Strategy supplied position and was called static. The modern era supplies technology and is warned about technology for its own sake.
Why that matters to the companion piece
The same day's taxonomy sorts these theories by scope into grand, middle-range and low-range tiers. This article sorts the same material by time. Together they give two independent maps of one field, and the theories that appear prominently in both are the ones the author goes on to build his own position from.
The Indonesian source it rests on
Simatupang (2009), writing in Apakah Manajemen adalah Sains, published by Penerbit ITB, is cited for a figure on how management is perceived by various audiences. It is the only Indonesian-language reference in the piece.
The strategy era's lasting effect
"The frameworks and theories developed during this period are foundational to modern business strategy and are taught in virtually every business school around the world." The article adds that they have also informed public policy on competition and innovation.
