LinkedIn Article  ·  Climate · The Tourism Paradox and a Village-Scale Answer  ·  11 November 2023

Tourism and Climate Change

Tourism raises emissions and raises awareness through the same act, and the article refuses to resolve that by choosing a side. Its move is to take the paradox down two levels: first into En-ROADS, where tourism is made tractable as four specific simulation inputs and the author commits to a number; then to the smallest unit where anything can actually be decided, a single village. What arrives there is not a principle but a scheme with a radius, a deposit amount and a returned seed, which is the article's answer to the objection that systems thinking produces no action.

Introduction

Promoting tourism raises the carbon footprint, and the travel that raises awareness is the travel that does the damage.

Situation

Tourism is promoted as economic development, and as more people move around the globe the energy consumption associated with travel rises with them.

Complication

Air travel is a significant contributor to greenhouse gas emissions, so the industry being promoted is one whose growth is measured in the thing that has to fall. The article's cover poses the question directly: what would aggressive tourism with no care for sustainability do to the temperature?

Question

Can tourism be grown and climate action pursued at the same time, or is the pairing incoherent?

Answer

The relationship runs in both directions, and the article treats that as the working condition rather than as a contradiction to be argued away. What follows is a simulation to size the effect and a village-scale scheme to act on it.

The tension, as the article names it

“the tension between traveling to witness the effects of climate change and contributing to the problem through emissions is a real issue.”

The Paradox

Awareness cuts both ways, because tourists who see the impacts first-hand become the constituency for acting on them.

The article sets the two directions against each other and lets both stand.

What tourism costs
✗Increased travel and a higher transport carbon footprint
✗Air travel as a significant emissions contributor
✗Rising energy consumption as more people move
✗Land use change from new resorts and attractions
✗Pressure on carbon sinks such as forests
What tourism supplies
✓Demand for sustainable options as tourists grow aware
✓Pressure on the industry toward carbon offsetting and renewables
✓Educational and transformative experience of the impacts
✓Ecotourism supporting conservation efforts
✓Sustainable development in local communities
Why the right column is not merely consolation

"By traveling to different parts of the world, people can see the impacts of climate change firsthand, which can be a powerful motivator for action." The article treats witnessing as producing political capacity rather than only sentiment.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Eight strategies attack the paradox from transport, policy and consumption at once.

The article lists the approaches being explored, and none of them resolves the paradox alone.

1  ·  Carbon offsetting
Travellers offset emissions by investing in projects that reduce CO2 elsewhere, such as reforestation or renewable energy.
2  ·  Sustainable transportation
Trains instead of planes where feasible, and more fuel-efficient or electric aircraft for the flights that remain necessary.
3  ·  Local and regional travel
Encouraging people to explore closer to home, which reduces the need for long-haul flights rather than offsetting them.
4  ·  Sustainable tourism practices
Tourism that supports local economies and operates within the carrying capacity of the environment, minimising impact on wildlife and ecosystems.
5  ·  Conservation education
Using a portion of tourism revenue to fund conservation and educational programmes that raise awareness of climate change.
6  ·  Policy and regulation
Limiting environmental impact through measures such as carbon taxes, and investing in sustainable infrastructure.
7  ·  Virtual experiences
Using technology for virtual tours that raise awareness and educate without the travel.
8  ·  Responsible consumption
Educating tourists on consumption habits during travel, to reduce waste and promote conservation.
The article's own weighting

"Each action may seem small on its own, but collectively, they can lead to a significant reduction in the overall impact of tourism on the environment." It calls the requirement systemic change to how travel works, plus individual choices that prioritise sustainability.

Evidence

En-ROADS takes tourism through four inputs: transport, energy supply, economic growth and land use.

The article uses the En-ROADS Climate Interactive simulation to make tourism tractable, holding other factors constant and adjusting only what tourism touches.

1  ·  Transport
Tourism involves significant transportation, especially air travel, which is a major source of greenhouse gas emissions. Raising the transport emissions input reflects increased tourism.
2  ·  Energy supply
Tourists consume energy in hotels, restaurants and attractions, raising demand for electricity and heating, which increases fossil fuel use where renewable options are absent.
3  ·  Economic growth
Tourism contributes to growth, and the simulation can show how increased economic activity raises energy demand and emissions where it is not managed sustainably.
4  ·  Land use
New resorts and attractions change land use and affect carbon sinks such as forests, which is simulated by adjusting deforestation and afforestation rates.
What the same four inputs also show

The article notes the simulation works in the other direction too, letting a user explore how sustainable practices mitigate the impact: cleaner transportation, sustainable energy infrastructure for tourist facilities, and preserved natural carbon sinks.

The Finding

My prediction on that simulation is 3.5 degrees.

Having set out the four inputs, the article does something most treatments of this subject avoid: it commits to a figure and publishes the scenario behind it.

The number is presented as a prediction rather than as a finding, and the article offers no confidence interval, sensitivity range or comparison against published projections. It is the author's own run of a public simulator under his own assumptions.

The scenario itself is shared as a parameterised En-ROADS link, which is the reproducible part of the claim. The version and the individual parameter settings are carried in the URL, so the run can in principle be opened and inspected rather than taken on trust.

That combination is the point. A number without the scenario is an assertion; the scenario without the number is an exercise. The article supplies both, which is also what makes it checkable by anyone who disagrees.

The prediction, as stated

"Here is my prediction based on the en-roads simulation: 3.5 degree celsius". The scenario URL printed beneath it appears truncated in the source document, ending mid-parameter, and is reproduced on the Sources slide exactly as printed.

Method

Start with why, then ask who benefits most, then find which loops reinforce and which balance.

The final section changes scale deliberately, from global simulation to what the article calls a simple step at personal or community level, and it supplies a sequence rather than a recommendation.

The first move is ontological, and the article says so: begin by asking why. If the driving force turns out to be purely economic growth and is deemed essential, the questioning does not stop there.

The second question is about distribution, and it is the one that changes what the first answer means. Asking who will benefit the most tests whether the growth being defended is growth for the people being asked to host it.

Only then does the systems tool come out. The article's instruction is to apply the system dynamics model to discern which elements have a negative reinforcing effect and which have a positive balancing one, which is the same apparatus its companion piece on causal loop diagrams sets out in full.

Conclusion

Ban vehicles within a kilometre, take a deposit returned for collected rubbish, and hand back a tree seed.

The article ends with a concrete scheme for a single village, prefaced with its own qualification that this is a complex and multifaceted decision and offered as what the author would do in the reader's position.

1
Draw a vehicle boundary
"I would initially establish a boundary where motorcycles and cars are prohibited, perhaps within a 1km radius of the village." The radius is offered as a starting point rather than as a fixed figure.
2
Take a refundable deposit
Visitors make a deposit of 100,000 Rupiahs, reclaimable in exchange for collecting plastics or rubbish during their walk and visit. The mechanism converts a cost into a task rather than into a charge.
3
Return a seed rather than only the money
In return, visitors are given a tree seed to plant, which turns the transaction's end point into an addition to the place rather than a settling of accounts.
4
Keep the value in the village
"Establish a circular economy in the village, where the food, attractions, drinks, accommodations, and entertainment are all produced by and benefit the villagers." This is the article's answer to its own question about who benefits most.

Each action may seem small on its own, but collectively, they can lead to a significant reduction in the overall impact of tourism on the environment.
