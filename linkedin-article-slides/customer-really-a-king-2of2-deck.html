Is Customer really a King ? - Part 2 of 2

Part 1 proposed the king-like customer; this part tests it on the author. After twenty-nine years with one airline and a decade in its top tier, one small request was refused and an email to the VP went unanswered. The refusal is legible through a customer-value framework that would score him a free rider — but the frameworks also say retention beats acquisition, and what the episode finally illustrates is Kahneman: the remembering self overtook the experiencing self, and the loyalty ended.

Opening

I tested the theory on myself: I asked one airline for one favour.

Part 1 argued for a king-like customer. Part 2 is what happened when the author had to be one.

The recap is brief and self-deprecating: "In my previous article, I wrote about a funny sign “customer is king” and suggested customers should shop like kings—buying a lot and not bargaining. While it’s amusing, this phrase often sets unrealistic expectations that customers should always be treated with utmost deference, regardless of their behavior." The alternative stands as Part 1 left it — "a king-like customer, who embodies dignity, fairness, and respect without acting entitled."

The relationship being tested is long and specific. "I’ve been flying with this airline since 1995 and became a member of their envied super premium lavished membership program 10 years ago." What he believed he had built: "a relationship based on trust and loyalty." As a member, "I took many flights where I felt valued and respected, contributing significantly to their revenue and enjoying their consistent high standards."

The request was small and the article is careful to say so. "Due to unforeseen circumstances, I needed an extension on my membership grace period." And immediately, the disclaimer that keeps the piece honest: "Of course, I’m not a king—obviously, since I fly commercial airlines and had to ask for an extension. My request wasn’t about demanding royal treatment but hoping for recognition of a decade-long relationship built on loyalty and mutual benefit."

The airline is not named

The article withholds the airline's name throughout, and the deck does so too. What is at issue is a pattern in loyalty programmes, not one company's reputation.

The Refusal

The request was denied and the email to the VP went unanswered.

Two facts, and the second is the one the article returns to.

"Their response was disappointing. My request was denied, and it felt like my ten years of loyalty and significant contributions were overlooked." Then the detail that turns a policy decision into a relationship ending: "Even my email to the VP of Customer Service went unanswered, both on LinkedIn and through official channels."

The question he puts is rhetorical and it lands: "Would they treat a “king” (jokingly) like that? Or maybe in their eyes, a member like me isn’t worth the king definition—not strategic enough? Maybe I was just a number to them."

What is being described is not a denied benefit but an absence of reply — the failure of the empathy dimension that Part 1 had made the whole basis of the king-like proposal.

Framework

Their two-by-two may have scored me a free rider, and that would explain it.

The article offers the airline a defence it has not asked for, and marks it clearly as speculation.

"Had they used the Sunil Gupta ’s 2x2 framework (Gupta & Lehman, 2005), perhaps, they found that I might not provide high value (no big revenues, not enough word of mouth), and they think they have provided me generous perks; hence, for them, I am considered a free rider. If that were the case, it explains their attitudes."

Every clause of that is conditional — had they used, perhaps, might not, if that were the case. The article is reconstructing a possible rationale, not reporting one, and it says so. Its verdict on the outcome is separate and unhedged: "Despite my contributions, the airline’s decision showed no real relationship or recognition of my loyalty."

The counter-question is where the analysis bites: "What would they lose over one customer, if I turned out to be "vulnerable customers in the framework above?"

Speculation, kept as speculation

The article never learns why the request was refused. No one at the airline explained, and no document is cited. The free-rider reading is the author's own hypothesis about a framework the airline may or may not use, and it is preserved here as a hypothesis.

Evidence

Retention is cheaper than acquisition, and lifetime value outran acquisition cost.

Three frameworks are brought against the decision, and none of them is about fairness — all three are about cost.

FRAMEWORK A
Retention economics
"Keeping a loyal customer is far cheaper than acquiring new ones (Aulet, 2013). The lifetime value (LTV) of a long-term customer like me far outweighs the cost of customer acquisition (COCA)." The consequence: "Losing a loyal customer not only affects immediate revenue but also increases marketing and promotional expenses to attract new customers."
FRAMEWORK B
Service quality
The article routes the loss through "Parasuraman's SERVQUAL framework (Seth et al, 2005)" — the same framework Part 1 used to define the king-like customer. What failed was not the product but responsiveness and empathy.
FRAMEWORK C
What customers actually want
Dixon and colleagues' finding is quoted at length: "A large scale study of contact-center and self-service interactions, however, finds that what customers really want (but rarely get) is just a satisfactory solution to their service issue."
FRAMEWORK D
What the airline sent instead
"They keep sending my and upgrading the website, notifications, colorful email, but they forget the only thing I asked kindly." The sentence is reproduced as written, including its slip, because it is the whole HBR finding restated as a grievance.

The Turn

What would a king-like customer do? Acknowledge it calmly, then leave.

The theory from Part 1 is applied by its author to his own disappointment, and it holds — up to a point.

"So, I asked myself, "What would a king-like customer do?" I handled this rejection with dignity. Instead of reacting impulsively or with anger, I calmly acknowledged their decision." That is the dignified complaint of Part 1, performed.

But dignity is not the same as staying. "Yet, the experience left a lasting impression. The lack of empathy and flexibility in handling a loyal customer’s reasonable request highlighted a gap in their service approach." And the decision that follows is stated plainly, with the balance still on the books: "I still have around 320,000 miles remaining, and after using them, I decided to look at other airlines that might better appreciate long-term customers."

This is what distinguishes king-like from compliant. The composure is real and the departure is real, and the article treats neither as a contradiction of the other.

Framework

Kahneman names the aftertaste: the remembering self overtook the experiencing self.

The final frame explains why a decade of good service did not survive one refusal — and the article does not claim this is unfair.

The experiencing self
•A class at Harvard Business School in 2019, asked for the most extraordinary customer experience he had had, and this airline was his answer
•"The welcome I received during flight boarding as a member made me feel truly special, like a king"
•Before covid, they "always return with "great exprerience that prompt telling good stories to others""
•He had used the company as his own teaching example of Thomke's framework on emotional transitions
The remembering self
✗"In hindsight, those feelings were superficial and did not touch on real humanity"
✗"my remembering self's perspective on the abrupt departure incident left a very bitter aftertaste"
✗"this is a case of the remembering self overtaking the experiencing self"
✗"I am still very grateful" — the gratitude is recorded alongside the bitterness, not instead of it
The teacher's own case study, reversed

The 2019 class was taught by Professor Stefan Thomke, and the airline was the author's illustration of how firms capitalise on emotional transitions. Five years later the same company supplies the counter-example. The article leaves that irony unstated; the deck notes it because the two moments are the same evidence read twice.

Conclusion

Forget the bells and whistles and just solve their problems.

The article ends without escalation, and the refusal to escalate is the last demonstration of its own thesis.

To really win their loyalty, forget the bells and whistles and just solve their problems ... Conventional wisdom holds that to increase loyalty, companies must "delight" customers by exceeding service expectations.

— Dixon, Freeman & Toman (2010), quoted in Is Customer really a King ? - Part 2 of 2
No campaign, no threat
"My life will go on, and so will the airline. My word of mouth is just a ripple in the ocean. The company is so big that it doesn't need my loyalty or money." The article declines the leverage a public complaint would give it.
The status symbol, set down
"Additionally, I don't need to prove my existence with a super premium membership symbol." Part 1 argued that a king-like customer does not require special treatment to feel respected; this is the author holding himself to it.
The alternatives, named
"I can fly with Qatar Airways or Japan Airlines to get me to the East Coast of US from Asia, even without a loyalty membership, and still find satisfaction, comfort and safety." Two competitors are named; the airline that refused him is not.

References
