What would she have done differently? Is it possible though?

The title asks what a leader should have done differently and then, in its second clause, asks whether doing differently was available at all. The article's answer is that it largely was not, for two compounding reasons: the structure of the system shapes behaviour and bears part of the responsibility usually assigned to the individual, and a leader deciding alone under heavy pressure falls back on neural pathways laid down in childhood and adolescence. The conclusion is therefore not a judgement on one episode but a claim about timing. A moral compass that was not formed early cannot be produced at the moment it is needed.

Introduction

The question in the title presumes a freedom the moment may not contain.

Situation

A leader faces a public crisis over racism and discrimination, and the response is judged afterwards against what a better response would have looked like.

Complication

That judgement assumes the alternatives were reachable at the moment of decision. The article argues they may not have been, because the system had shaped the situation and early formation had shaped the person deciding inside it.

Question

What would she have done differently, and, as the title's second clause asks, was doing differently possible?

Answer

The scope for a different decision was narrower than the question implies. Responsibility is distributed between a structure that shapes behaviour and a set of mental models fixed long before the appointment, which relocates the intervention from the crisis to the childhood.

How the article is built

The piece publishes its own scaffolding first. Roughly half the text sets out how the argument will proceed, section by section, before the line "So here is the post:" introduces the article itself. The deck follows the argument; the preamble is described here rather than given a slide of its own.

Framework

Leaders are a handful and the majority are followers, and it is the followers who choose their leaders.

The article opens its argument with a distinction it says is routinely collapsed, and the order matters: the third category turns out to be the decisive one.

01
The few
Leaders
"There are only a handful of people who are appointed as leaders, a distinction that should not be confused with managers." The article treats the appointment as the beginning of the problem rather than its resolution.
02
Distinct from leaders
Managers
Named only to be separated out. The article makes the point that the two roles are habitually conflated and that the conflation obscures what leadership actually demands.
03
The many
Followers
Described as workers, safe players, those who wait and see, and those who prefer to hide behind the crowd. Operating inside a system with its own structures, cultures and political dynamics, they are the ones who choose their leaders, in formal and informal capacities alike.
The claim it rests on

"Just like it takes a whole village to raise a child, it takes a whole village to raise a leader." The article adds that recognising the role of followers is central to understanding the ascent of any leader, and singles out the toxic case.

Details

The structure of the system shapes behaviour, so a destructive leader's failure is not solely their own.

The article's second move is to assess the system rather than the person. Its integrity is judged on two things at once: the rational organisational model that is written down, and the underlying, often unspoken culture, which the article likens to System 1 thinking. The politics inside the structure are named as a third significant factor.

That framing produces the article's most consequential sentence, which redistributes responsibility without dissolving it. The failure attributed to a destructive leader is held to be, more accurately, a manifestation of broader systemic issues, with the structure bearing a significant part of the responsibility for behaviour it profoundly influences.

The claim is carefully bounded. It says the failure is not solely the leader's, not that the leader bears none of it, and the article's later insistence on unwavering principle depends on the individual share remaining real.

On the system

"The structure of the system is paramount. It fundamentally shapes behavior."

On where responsibility sits

"the failure often attributed to a leader, particularly one who is deemed destructive, is not solely their own. More accurately, it is a manifestation of broader systemic issues."

Evidence

Decisions made alone at the top run on mental models formed in childhood and adolescence.

The article turns to a published case study to connect the systemic account to the individual one, and the mechanism it extracts is the pivot of the whole piece.

CASE A
The case it uses
A Harvard Business Review interview with Franco Bernabè, taken up for its account of how leaders' decisions are shaped by mental models formed in their early years. The article's interest is in the lens, not in the executive's own record.
CASE B
The mechanism
"when leaders find themselves at the top, making decisions alone and under heavy pressure, their brains tend to rely on the neural pathways formed during early childhood and adolescence."
CASE C
Why isolation matters
The article draws out what it calls the often solitary nature of decision-making at high levels. Where there is no one to ask, the fallback is not deliberation but whatever was laid down long before, which is what makes early exposure decisive for a leader who is purpose-driven and unwavering in principle.
CASE D
What this does to the title's question
If the decisive material was fixed decades earlier, then asking what she would have done differently is asking about a range of options that had already been narrowed. The article does not excuse the outcome; it relocates the point at which it could have been changed.

Implications

Adherence to principle admits no 99 percent and no 98 percent.

Having argued that the individual share of responsibility is reduced, the article now presses hard on what remains of it, and the standard it sets is absolute. Straying even slightly from a hundred percent, it says, settling for ninety-nine or ninety-eight, is unacceptable in matters of principle.

The distinction it draws in the surrounding passage is between commitment and performance. Where various forms of racism are present, leaders are required to meet them with understanding and a sincere commitment to inclusive values, and the article names the failure mode directly: treating inclusion as a facade of political correctness maintained to protect a career or an institution.

The two claims fit together rather than pulling apart. The system supplies the conditions, and the leader supplies the line that does not move. The article's argument is that the second is only available to someone in whom it was already installed.

The standard

"Adherence to principles must be unwavering" — and the article immediately quantifies what unwavering excludes, ruling out ninety-nine and ninety-eight percent alike.

The failure mode

"Leaders must tackle these challenges with a profound understanding and a sincere commitment to inclusive values, not merely as a facade of political correctness to protect their career or the institution."

Conclusion

Values cannot be implanted late, so the moral compass has to be there before the pressure arrives.

The article closes by naming when the work has to be done, which is the only answer its own framing allows.

Therefore, the ability of leaders to make informed, independent decisions, guided by a strong moral compass, is imperative.

The timing claim
"This steadfastness in values cannot be an afterthought or implanted late in life; it needs to be ingrained from early childhood."
What happens without it
Where such values are missing from what the article calls fundamental System 1 thinking, the result under a critical situation is compromise. The failure appears at the moment of pressure but was set much earlier.
What the decisions are for
The article ties the whole argument to a purpose: informed, independent decisions of this kind are what effectively address and condemn all forms of racism and discrimination, and what contribute to a more inclusive and equitable environment.
Where that leaves the title
The second clause of the title is the article's real subject. It asks not what should have been done but whether the doing was available, and the answer it reaches is that the availability was decided long before anyone asked the question.
