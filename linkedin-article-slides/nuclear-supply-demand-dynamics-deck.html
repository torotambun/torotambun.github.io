Using Supply Demand Framework to Asses Nuclear Industry's Dynamics with Its Context

A supply and demand diagram is used to answer a question put by nuclear engineering students about whether their education still has a future, in a country the article describes as generally against nuclear power. Its finding is an asymmetry: the supply curve responds to things the industry controls, namely technology, policy and capital, while the demand curve does not, because the resistance is cultural and a cultural objection is not price-sensitive. That asymmetry sets the agenda, moving the work from cost reduction to trust. The career answer that follows is deliberately conditional, and the article's final position is that the foundation transfers whichever way the market turns.

Introduction

In a country generally against nuclear power the supply curve is steep and the demand curve barely moves.

Situation

Global energy demand rises with population, economic development and industrialisation, while the supply side shifts with resource availability, technology and the relative cost of each source.

Complication

Plotted for a country the article names as generally against nuclear power, both curves sit badly. Supply is steep, meaning production is costly; demand is shallow and unresponsive, reflecting opposition rather than price. The equilibrium lands at a low quantity and a high price.

Question

What can actually be moved, and what does that mean for people whose training is committed to this industry?

Answer

Supply can be shifted right by technology, political commitment and capital. Demand cannot be moved the same way, because its resistance is cultural, so the work there is trust rather than tariff, and the career answer has to be conditional on which curve moves.

On the supply side

"The supply curve is relatively steep, suggesting higher costs associated with nuclear energy production in Indonesia." The article attributes this to a lack of infrastructure, expertise or general support in the country.

On the demand side

"The demand for nuclear energy is significantly lower, as indicated by the shallow slope of the demand curve."

Framework

Supply can be shifted right by three levers: technology, political commitment and patient capital.

The article treats the supply curve as the tractable side, and names what moves it. All three reduce cost or risk rather than changing anyone's mind.

01
First lever
Technology
Advances in reactor design, safety mechanisms and waste management. Small modular reactors and advanced fuel cycles are named as the innovations that can lower costs, shorten timelines and improve safety at once.
02
Second lever
Political commitment
Government policy is treated as a market force in its own right: subsidies, research funding and international treaties. Policies targeting environmental sustainability and climate change direct the market towards cleaner sources.
03
Third lever
Patient capital
Limited partners, private equity, venture capital and angel investors, whose appetite for a high-capital, long-horizon, heavily regulated sector is what determines whether the technology is built or stays on paper.
The case the article makes for the sector

Nuclear is positioned as a transitional solution rather than a destination. “It stands as a bridge between the current high energy demand and the need for carbon emission reduction.” It supplies baseload where solar or wind are less viable, and complements renewables rather than competing with them.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Capital

Venture capital is agile while nuclear is high capital, long horizon and heavily regulated, so the money arrives cautiously and mostly for small modular reactors.

The article is direct about the mismatch. Venture capital is drawn to sectors offering high returns for accepted risk, and nuclear presents a profile that fits badly against that appetite on three counts at once.

Different pools behave differently, which is the part the article draws out. Limited partners and private equity firms hold large capital and seek returns over longer horizons, and their gradual shift towards sustainable and socially responsible investment opens an avenue for nuclear projects aligned with carbon reduction goals.

Venture capitalists and angel investors are described as more agile and more open to high risk, provided there is potential for significant disruption. Their interest is concentrated rather than general: small modular reactors and advanced fuel cycles, and specifically the question of whether those technologies reduce construction and operating costs, shorten timelines and mitigate the traditional risks.

The article's conclusion on capital is that the climate is evolving rather than favourable. Recognition of the sector's potential is growing as investors align with sustainability goals and as innovation lowers the risks, and the investment that follows is what shifts the supply curve right.

The mismatch, stated

"Its high initial capital requirements, extended timelines for profitability, and regulatory complexities make it a challenging proposition for traditional venture capital investors."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Harder Curve

Demand is the harder curve, because the resistance is cultural and a cultural objection does not respond to price.

This is the article's central analytical move, and it is what makes the piece more than a survey. Having shown that supply responds to investment and policy, it turns to demand and finds a curve that the same instruments do not reach.

The term it uses is non-elastic demand, and the explanation is not economic. Resistance is described as deeply rooted in societal concern and perception, which forms a barrier that influences the demand elasticity itself rather than sitting alongside it.

Three components are named. Historical incidents have shaped public consciousness; waste disposal and the long-term environmental impact of radioactive material remain unresolved in public view; and safety fear is identified as the single most significant driver of resistance.

The consequence the article draws is a change of instrument. If the objection is not about price, then reducing price does not answer it, so the work moves to education, transparency, engagement and trust. That is why the strategy section that follows contains no tariff, no subsidy and no cost curve.

What set the terms

"The narrative of nuclear energy is often overshadowed by historical incidents such as Chernobyl and Fukushima." The article adds that these events left an indelible mark on public consciousness and continue to influence opinion and policy worldwide.

What dominates the rest

"Safety fears are perhaps the most significant factor driving societal resistance to nuclear energy."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Chernobyl and Fukushima set the terms, so the work is education, transparency and trusted community voices rather than a better tariff.

The article's strategy for shifting demand is a list of six instruments, none of which is financial.

1  ·  Education campaigns
Aimed at demystifying nuclear energy by making complex scientific concepts accessible, covering how nuclear power works, its reliability and low carbon emissions, and the advances made in safety, delivered from school curriculums through to community workshops.
2  ·  Transparent communication about safety
Open communication about design and operational safety protocols, regulatory oversight and emergency preparedness, carried through regular reports, open days at facilities and interactive online platforms.
3  ·  Showcasing programmes that worked
Case studies of countries that have used nuclear energy safely and effectively, presented as evidence of viability and covering contributions to energy security, economic development and environmental sustainability.
4  ·  Community and religious leaders
Named explicitly by the article as advocates. These leaders hold the trust of large groups, and providing them with accurate information is treated as instrumental in shifting perception within their communities.
5  ·  Media and social influencers
Working with journalists, bloggers and social media personalities to spread accurate information and counteract misinformation, which the article says is particularly effective in reaching a younger audience.
6  ·  Public involvement in decisions
Public forums, consultations and feedback mechanisms, so that community concerns are heard. The article's argument is that this builds trust and also gives the public a sense of ownership over the energy choices of their region.
Why the list has this shape

Every item addresses belief rather than cost. The article's position is that trust has to be built through genuine engagement, which means involving communities in decisions and being open about risks, not only distributing information.

Benchmark

Nuclear must price at or below fossil fuels before any of the long-run argument is heard.

Having spent the demand section arguing that price is not the objection, the article is careful to state where price still governs absolutely. The benchmark is set against traditional fossil fuels, and the article treats meeting it as a precondition rather than an advantage.

Its reason is about how the decision is actually made. Consumers and industries base energy choices primarily on cost, so an argument about emissions, stability or energy security reaches them only after the cost comparison has been passed.

The illustrative comparison it gives places nuclear above coal and hydro on price per unit and below oil and gas, with hydro lowest and coal low on abundance and accessibility. The article presents this graph as hypothetical and illustrative rather than as sourced data.

The long-run case is stated separately and deliberately after the benchmark: reduced greenhouse gas emissions and a stable energy supply, which the article argues make nuclear part of a sustainable energy future despite its challenges.

The precondition

"For nuclear energy to be a viable and attractive option, it must achieve a price point that is either on par with or lower than that of fossil fuels."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Implications

Where climate mitigation drives demand the industry grows, and where coal prevails it does not.

The article plots two demand scenarios against a single improved supply curve, and reads the employment consequences off the two equilibrium points.

Demand rises on climate mitigation
✓Expansion in plant operation and maintenance
✓Research and development, safety analysis, regulatory compliance
✓Designing reactors, waste management and safety systems
✓Policy development as regulations and treaties change
✓International collaboration, global safety bodies, multinational energy firms
Coal prevails and demand falls
✗Opportunities within the nuclear sector become more limited
✗Adaptation required towards transferable skills
✗Radiation applications in medicine and industry
✗Decommissioning and environmental remediation
✗Crossover into renewables, energy policy or impact assessment
What remains relevant in both columns

The article names project management, environmental protection and public engagement as areas that hold their value irrespective of which energy source wins, and treats skill in them as a safety net across sectors.

Conclusion

The degree holds either way, because physics, systems engineering and environmental safety transfer.

The article's sub-conclusion answers the question the piece was written to answer, and it answers it conditionally rather than reassuringly.

Therefore, nuclear engineering education can still be considered a worthwhile pursuit, if students and professionals are adaptable and open to applying their skills in a variety of contexts.

— Using Supply Demand Framework to Asses Nuclear Industry's Dynamics, 9 December 2023
The conditional it states first
Whether nuclear engineering education remains relevant depends on the trajectory of the industry. In scenarios favouring growth there is clear demand for the skills; the article does not assert that such a scenario is coming.
What survives the other scenario
The skills acquired "such as a strong foundation in physics, systems engineering, and environmental safety, remain valuable and transferable to other sectors." The foundation is the asset, not the sector.
The condition attached
The worth of the pursuit is made conditional on adaptability. The article puts the requirement on the student rather than on the market, which is the honest place for it given everything above.
Who the article is finally addressing
Its closing passages are written as notes for someone briefing engineering students, asking them to consider how their own roles could shift these dynamics, through technological innovation and through informed contribution to policy debate.

Sources
