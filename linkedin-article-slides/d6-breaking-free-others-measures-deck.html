D-6 Breaking Free: Liberating Yourself from Others' Measures

A client who paid generously spent years measuring my work against yardsticks that were never mine — and the freedom, when it came, was not in winning the argument but in declining the measure.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

Others' expectations are a chain that tightens the moment I step outside the boundary they drew.

Christmas as the season that sharpens it rather than softens it.

"The weight of others' expectations has always felt like an invisible chain, one that tightens the moment I start to step outside the boundaries they've drawn for me."

"Christmas, a time for joy and reflection, is also a time when I'm reminded of the countless ways I have been measured, judged, and found wanting by someone else's standard—someone who never paused to ask what truly mattered to me."

That last clause is the whole complaint, and it is narrower than it first sounds. The objection is not to being judged. It is to being judged against a standard whose owner never established what was being attempted.

The Client

A client who paid generously spent years telling me my work should look like someone else's.

"It all came to a head with one particular client, someone who engaged me with promises of partnership and trust, paying generously, but at what cost to my spirit?"

"His criticisms came quickly, like arrows aimed not at my work but at my essence." The first of them was about slides: "You should use consultant-style slides when teaching," he said.

"Consultant slides? I wasn't selling ideas—I was teaching frameworks, illuminating paths, and helping his employees see possibilities within themselves."

And the objection was not ignorance of the form. "Years ago, I had been a consultant for Schlumberger Business Consulting for a short stint; I knew what those slides looked like, and I knew they had no place in the classroom. Yet, his words lingered like a shadow over my efforts."

Why the payment matters to the account

The relationship is not described as exploitative in its terms. He was paid, and paid well. The cost being counted is a different currency, which is what makes the piece harder than a complaint about a bad client.

The Escalation

A book launched at MIT was met with a question about why I had not written like someone more popular.

"Then came the books. “Why haven't you written a book like so-and-so?” he asked, as though my voice and my ideas needed validation through mimicry."

"Years later, I presented him with one of my books—a work that had been launched at MIT, a pinnacle of intellectual achievement. His response? Another dismissal."

"Why don't you write like Kyai so-and-so? He's so popular. You should make a YouTube channel."

"It wasn't enough to have written; it had to be written in the style he approved, for an audience he deemed worthy. The audacity of it all left me breathless, but I smiled through it, refusing to let his words tarnish what I knew was a milestone."

The structure of the exchange is worth marking. Each time the standard is met, the standard moves — and it moves toward whoever is currently more popular, which is a measure that can never be satisfied by doing the work better.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Tell

When introductions to VIPs became advice to go work for them, the measure was money and I was not using it.

"The introductions I made for him to VIPs, connections cultivated through years of trust and respect, were met with more critiques."

"Why don't you work for them? Why don't you become their advisor? You could earn more."

"His words stung, not because they were true but because they were so profoundly blind to what drives me. Did he think I measured my success by the size of my paycheck or the title beside my name? His questions weren't curiosity; they were judgments masquerading as advice."

The answer given at the time is the piece's clearest statement of the boundary: "I don't need recognition from the government. I don't live for acknowledgment."

"But the critiques never ended. They poured out of him like a flood, relentless and exhausting. It was as though his own discontent with life found its outlet in tearing down others."

Field Notes

The research names the cost: self-worth made contingent on approval produces stress, anger and conflict.

The article stops three times to place its own experience against published findings — not as proof of the account, but as a name for what the account describes.

FINDING A
Contingent self-worth carries a cost
"Research indicates that an over-reliance on external validation can lead to increased stress, anger, and relationship conflicts, as individuals' self-worth becomes contingent on others' approval (Crocker, 2002)." His reading of it: "each critique from him felt less like constructive feedback and more like an attempt to tether my worth to his ever-changing expectations."
FINDING B
Invalidation compounds
"Consistent exposure to invalidation and criticism has been linked to heightened negative emotions and decreased self-esteem." Shenk and Fruzzetti (2011) "found that invalidation can exacerbate emotional dysregulation, leading to a cycle of self-criticism and diminished self-worth."
FINDING C
The cycle was available and not entered
"Reflecting on this, I see how his unrelenting critiques could have drawn me into such a cycle, but instead, they fortified my resolve to define my value on my own terms." The claim is not that the mechanism failed to apply — it is that it was recognised.
FINDING D
What the literature offers as the exit
"Developing an internal sense of self-worth can mitigate the adverse effects of external criticism and reduce the dependency on others' approval for personal validation." With that, "individuals can navigate professional and personal challenges with greater resilience".
The question the findings open

"Why do we let others' measures define us? Why do their judgments pierce us when we know they're rooted in their own insecurities?" The article poses it and does not answer it directly — it answers it by acting.

What I Carry

Freedom is not winning the argument; it is declining the measure.

"This Christmas, I'm letting go of those chains. I refuse to carry the burden of someone else's discontent."

"I will not let their narrow vision confine the expansive, winding path I've chosen. My life is not a consultant slide deck, nor a popular YouTube channel. It is a tapestry of moments, achievements, failures, and quiet joys—woven not for an audience but for myself."

Note what is not in that sentence. There is no rebuttal of the slides, no defence of the book, no counter-argument about audience size. The critiques are not answered; they are set down.

"If this resonates with anyone reading, let this Christmas be the moment you set yourself free. Free from the voices that demand you fit into their molds. Free from the need for validation from those who will never see your worth. Free to be unapologetically yourself, measured not by what you do for others but by the peace and purpose you find within."

"That is my Christmas gift to myself this year: freedom."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Last Word

I hope he stops measuring himself by the yardsticks of wealth, power and recognition.

The piece could have ended on the gift to himself. It goes one paragraph further, and the extra paragraph is directed at the man.

And to the man who taught me this lesson, though unintentionally, I offer no bitterness.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun
What is wished for him
"I hope one day he, too, will stop measuring himself by the yardsticks of wealth, power, and recognition. Perhaps he'll find that the only measure worth living by is the depth of one's own contentment."
The diagnosis inside the wish
The wish reframes everything before it. If the man is himself measured by wealth, power and recognition, then the critiques were never assessments of the work — they were the only vocabulary he had. That is the reading the last line commits to.
Where it ends
"Until then, I walk my path, unshackled and unafraid." Not reconciliation, and not rupture — a separation with the other man's freedom still wished for.
