Meritocracy's Mirage: How Scholarly Excellence May Conceal Inequities and Inequalities?

Meritocracy is usually tested against its principle, and this piece tests it against a room. A scholarship ceremony convened to honour merit turned out to be full of students educated abroad and visibly affluent, and the article treats that as evidence rather than as irony. Passow's ten obstacles supply the mechanism: the starting line is not one line, so an outcome measured at the finish encodes advantages accumulated long before the race. The policy consequence it names is specific and uncomfortable, that public money is funding students whose families could already afford the schools. And its central observation is hedged by the author himself, at length, in a footnote.

Introduction

Meritocracy promises an equal chance based on talent, work and determination.

The article states the promise before testing it, and states it generously. At its core, meritocracy in education holds that every individual, whatever their background or circumstance, should have an equal chance to succeed on the strength of their talents, hard work and determination.

That promise is then tied to something larger than education policy. The article aligns it with democratic principles, emphasising fairness and equality of opportunity, which is what makes a failure of meritocracy a political problem rather than an administrative one.

Having set the standard, the article does not argue with it. What follows is not a critique of the principle but an account of one evening on which the principle and the practice were visible in the same room.

The Scene

The room celebrating merit was full of luxury, and a scholarship winner carried a Chanel 2.55.

The account is dated and located: last spring, leaving a club in downtown Chicago around six in the evening, in an April drizzle, wearing a Navy-issued jacket and a waterproof hat.

What he walked into was a scholarship awardees' assembly, a gathering convened to celebrate young achievers and, in the article's framing, to honour the principles of meritocratic education. The belief in the room was unambiguous: these were students who had achieved prominence through talent, commitment and industriousness.

The scene inside told a different story. Amid the chatter the article records the luxury many students exuded, in upscale attire and refined accessories, and one detail that crystallises the rest.

The article is careful that a bag is not an argument, and it returns to that carefulness in a footnote reproduced below. What it treats as the harder evidence is not the accessories but the biographies.

The detail the piece turns on

"the stark surprise of spotting a Chanel 2.55 bag in the grip of a scholarship winner" — which the article reads, with the attire and accessories, as a telltale sign of privilege.

The author's own case against his own observation

A footnote sets out four alternatives: the items might be the culmination of personal savings or gifts; they could be pre-owned or replicas, and so more attainable than they look; an inclination towards fashion does not preclude academic dedication; and the visible display could be personal style, a commemoration of an achievement, or a deliberate parade of affluence. The article raises all four against itself.

Evidence

Most of them had been raised abroad, so their stories were about exposure rather than adversity.

This is the observation the article rests on, and it concerns provenance rather than appearance. The majority of the students, he learned, had been raised outside Indonesia and had developed their academic skills in foreign universities.

The consequence for the ceremony's premise is immediate. A scholarship framed as recognising achievement against odds was being awarded to people whose accounts contained comparatively few, and the article says so directly.

The connection to Passow follows in the same paragraph. Income disparity, cultural difference, linguistic barriers and geographical isolation all came to mind, and the article's phrase for what happened next is that the essence of meritocracy seemed to unravel in front of him.

What the evening left was a question rather than an accusation. Is meritocracy the academic prowess of an individual, or the totality of a journey including its struggles and the systemic barriers overcome?

The finding

"Their tales were less about overcoming adversity and more about leveraging international exposure and opportunities."

What the room was not

"This wasn't the modest gathering of ardent learners I had imagined."

Framework

Passow's ten obstacles explain why the race was never even.

The article takes the ten disadvantages identified by Dr. Harry Passow (1916–1996) and elaborates each as a mechanism rather than as a category.

1  ·  Poverty
Beyond missing books and tutoring, the article names constant anxiety about food and shelter, absent early childhood education, and schooling made transient by economic hardship, compounded by a digital divide and the psychological toll poverty exacts.
2  ·  Limited development of mental abilities and language
Scarce linguistic and cognitive stimulation in early years: a narrower vocabulary, fewer varied social interactions, limited access to educational media, and parents whose own educational exposure makes the gap hard to bridge.
3  ·  Sharp differences between home and school values
Alienation as a daily condition, a curriculum that feels unrelatable, an internal tug-of-war between two identities, and parents who feel out of place and so disengage from the school.
4  ·  Racial or ethnic minority status, or lower caste
From subtle microaggressions to open discrimination, together with an absence from the curriculum that produces invisibility, and lowered expectations from educators and peers.
5  ·  A native language that is not the medium of instruction
Absorbing academic content while learning the language it arrives in, which hinders comprehension and produces feelings of inadequacy and diminished self-worth.
6  ·  Isolation, geographical, physical or social
Remote or nomadic living, physical disability, schools without the infrastructure to teach effectively, and membership of ostracised groups.
7  ·  Poor health, emotional disturbance or disability
Chronic conditions that make focus exponentially harder, and struggles that remain invisible without specialised intervention while the effect on growth is not.
8  ·  Parents indifferent or hostile to education
A demotivating message from the people whose support most peers have, producing reduced engagement and isolation.
9  ·  An inadequately equipped or staffed school
Outdated materials, absent extracurricular opportunity, missing basic facilities, and potentially untrained or less motivated staff.
10  ·  A self-concept that rules out success
An internalised belief in one's own incapacity, which the article calls a self-fulfilling prophecy: withdrawal from participation, anxiety during evaluation, and avoidance of academic challenge.
Why the list belongs here

The article's reading is that to champion meritocracy in education one must first identify and tackle these barriers, because a system that measures outcomes without accounting for them measures the barriers as well as the merit.

Details

Income disparity buys resources, environments and an early start that compound.

The article separates three channels through which money reaches attainment, and keeps them apart because they operate at different stages.

The first is direct purchase. Where the quality of education is proportional to its cost, high-quality schools, supplementary materials, extracurriculars and tutoring are available to those who can pay, which establishes an edge inside the system.

The second is environmental and sits outside the school entirely. Stable living conditions, healthcare, nutritious food and a place conducive to study on one side; food insecurity, no quiet space, and academic work juggled against a part-time job on the other.

The third is chronological and the article treats it as the most consequential. The divergence begins before formal schooling, in the phase that lays the foundations: early learning programmes and books and educational tools for some, and their absence for others.

Implications

Privilege is self-reinforcing, and the cost is talent wasted at the bottom.

The article names three consequences, and the third is the one it frames as a loss to everyone rather than to the individual.

How the cycle closes
✗Better resources produce better test and evaluation results
✗Those results open scholarships and prestigious places
✗Those places open lucrative careers
✗The status is fortified and the gap widens
✗The outcome reads as merit at every step
What it costs everyone
✗Talented students without access never showcase or hone their skills
✗Their talents remain underdeveloped
✗A reservoir of potential goes untapped
✗Society pays what the article calls a hidden cost
✗Progress that would have followed does not
Where the runners actually start

"many students from lower-income backgrounds start their educational journey at a disadvantage." The article's image is that they do not merely begin a few steps behind but run on an uneven track, laden with obstacles.

The waste, named

"This results in a significant waste of potential that could have contributed to societal progress."

The Policy Question

Public money is funding students who could already afford the schools.

The article's second footnote is where the observation becomes a policy claim, and it is stated as a live question rather than a settled finding. Government scholarships sending students to foreign universities are merit-based by design.

The pattern it reports is that recipients are frequently not undergraduates from Indonesia at all but graduates of universities abroad, from affluent families, now studying at top schools their families could fund themselves.

Having stated the objection, the article argues the other side at length rather than leaving it unanswered. Merit-based awards attract top talent and foster excellence; need-based awards level the field for those who could not otherwise attend; and the tension between them remains under discussion.

It also concedes the complexity of the criteria, which may weigh international relations, the potential for future contribution to the country, and the promotion of particular fields. The article's own position is that the optics of such allocations generate debate and may push for policy reconsideration.

The question, in his own words

"I still have question about my country spending billion of dollars for these rich students, instead of needy students…"

And the case against himself

"Merit-based scholarships can attract top talent and foster excellence, but they may disproportionately benefit students who already have access to quality education and resources."

Conclusion

The question is what each of us does about it in our own sphere.

The article ends by turning the analysis back on the reader and on the motive underneath the system.

the very essence of meritocracy seemed to unravel before my eyes. The chasm between those with privilege and those without seemed to be growing, even in spaces meant to celebrate pure academic achievement.

— Meritocracy's Mirage, 24 October 2023
The question it puts
How each of us addresses and rectifies these disparities in our own spheres of influence. The article offers no policy instrument at this point, which keeps the obligation personal.
What it asks us to examine
The allure of wealth and power, and the underlying obsession with amassing wealth and status, which the article treats as the appetite the system is built to satisfy.
The charge it makes
That in placing undue emphasis on materialism we enthrone ourselves, acting less as humane individuals and more as self-centred masters, and in doing so shackle others and ourselves alike.
What true meritocracy would require
Not only celebrating academic achievement but ensuring every child, whatever their background, has an equal shot at achieving it. The article's standard is the starting line rather than the podium.

Sources

The article cites one work.
