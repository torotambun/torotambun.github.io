Part 2: Brain Formation, Lifelong Journeys and Lessons from Prof. Sri Margono: My Testimony on Entrepreneurship Formation

The article opens as an indictment of well-funded university entrepreneurship programmes and then answers it with a testimony rather than a study. A schoolboy in 1987 walked into a medical faculty with no introduction and left with a mentor, and that single relationship opened a sequence he traces through a national competition, a palace, and a career. The argument is that the decisive inputs are early narratives and guiding figures, neither of which a university can supply to a brain whose foundational circuits are already laid. Its final move is to test the claim against the mentor herself at ninety-two, whose memory now returns to the two figures that formed her.

Opening

Well-funded university programmes promise innovation-driven entrepreneurs and consistently fail to produce them.

The article opens on the gap between funding and output. University programmes promise to cultivate opportunity-based and innovation-driven entrepreneurs, and despite their resources manage only modest results, in a country where a genuine innovation ecosystem is conspicuously absent.

What it holds against the administrators is not the failure but the response to it. Regulators and executors are described as continuing with a business-as-usual attitude and applauding themselves for limited successes, rather than introspecting.

The comparison it draws is with ecosystems that have genuinely produced innovation-driven communities, which the article characterises by an economic yield it puts at eleven trillion US dollars, rivalling the world's eleventh largest economy. No source is given for that figure.

It then distinguishes what it is asking for from what policy has produced. Government policy is credited with an inadvertent proliferation of micro-entrepreneurs, small-scale businesses that sprang up en masse to fill a void in industry, and the article is explicit that these are not the innovation-driven entrepreneurship it means.

The charge

"their consistent failures to deliver become glaringly evident, especially in a country where a genuine innovation ecosystem is conspicuously missing."

What it takes instead

“Much like the old adage, “It takes a village to raise a child,” similarly, the birth and nurturing of an innovation-driven entrepreneur require the active participation and synergy of a robust community.”

The Encounter

In 1987 a schoolboy walked into a medical faculty with no letter of introduction and was handed a mentor.

The formation the article describes begins earlier than the encounter. A writing competition won in the sixth grade, run by a pharmaceutical company; a junior high essay for the Postal Department on the introduction of the postal code; and in high school, the Science Club, with bootcamps before and after school led by alumni and senior students, where research methodology was learned.

The ambition that followed was specific: to reach the national scientific writing competitions run by the Indonesian Institute of Sciences with TVRI Nasional, and by the ministry.

One project required experiments with rats, unusual for a schoolboy, and his biology teacher's advice was to approach the Faculty of Medicine at the University of Indonesia. The article is precise about how little accompanied that advice.

What happened on arrival is the article's point about how such doors actually open. He approached the Department of Pharmacology, was directed to Parasitology, and asked a member of staff for help; on learning which school he came from, the man led him to Prof. Pinardi Hadijaja's office, who introduced him to a colleague in a white doctor's jacket. That was the first meeting with Sri Margono, later a professor, and the connection that made it possible was that her daughters attended the same school.

The whole of the preparation he had

"This was the extent of guidance I received: no formal introductions, no letters of recommendation."

The Turns

What followed was a palace, a handshake with a president and a set of doors that stayed open.

The article gives the sequence in order, and the order is the argument: one relationship produced a chain that ran for decades.

Under her guidance the research matured from inquisitive exploration into something larger, with her monitoring progress and securing access to the resources and personnel of the lab.
1987

A collaboration between the Indonesian Institute of Sciences and TVRI Nasional. The article treats this as the hinge rather than the prize.

Discussions with the Minister of Education Prof. Fuad Hassan, the Minister of Research and Technology Prof. B.J. Habibie, the Minister of Information Harmoko, and officials of the Secretary Office.

What It Revealed

A mentor is not a service but a pathway, and this one held for thirty-six years.

The article's account of what the relationship became is domestic rather than professional. He was invited into her home after office hours, met her mother, a woman in her seventies who traced her lineage to Sultan HB VII and whose command of Dutch and Dutch etiquette sat alongside her Javanese roots.

The detail he returns to is a meal. Despite differences of tribe and religion and his standing as an outsider, he was invited to eat, the table set with sayur asam, tempe and fried fish, and gestured to serve himself. The article says the memory still brings tears.

The household itself is read as an organisation. The long-serving household member, addressed by the Javanese term rather than by name, had become part of the core family, and the article treats her as expressing the household's ethos much as corporate culture expresses an organisation's, through who answers the door and what is said on the telephone.

The relationship's reach is shown rather than asserted: consulted by his parents at an academic crossroads in 1991, present with her husband at his wedding, and reachable during the pandemic when her familiar voice was a comfort. In 2014 she was still sharing a manuscript on flowers and gardening that later became a published book.

The lineage she carried forward

Her ethos is traced in the article to her great-great-grandfather, whom she called Sultan Sugih, or Sultan HB VII. "Noted for his exceptional negotiation skills with the colonialists, he met their expectations while masterfully ensuring the welfare of his people." The article reads her openness to a stranger as that legacy in practice.

Field Notes

At ninety-two her memories return to two figures, a brother and a Sultan, which is what makes them pillars rather than recollections.

The article describes a deliberate attempt to elicit more. He asked about her fondest childhood memories and about any guiding principles left by her parents or relatives, employing every technique he had learned to reach what might be tucked away.

What came back was narrower than expected and, on the article's reading, more revealing for it. Her memories circled repeatedly to two figures: her only sibling and the Sultan.

The brother is remembered in detail: a handsome face and sharp nose, a reserved and diligent nature, ice skating together and his reluctance to swim, and a continuing concern for the state of his family. She recalled his academic journey consistently, placing him at TU Delft though sometimes confusing it with Utrecht.

The Sultan is remembered as a source of values rather than as an ancestor. From the stories of him she took the importance of being benevolent and socially responsible and of extending a hand to the vulnerable, and the article's judgement is that these were not stories for her but guiding principles.

Why the repetition is the finding

"The repetitive nature of these memories suggested that they weren't just mere recollections, but instead, profound pillars that had significantly shaped her life's journey."

The Neuroscience

The aging brain does not revert to adolescence; its myelin degenerates and its pathways wear.

The article poses its neuroscience question directly out of the observation above. As age advances, and particularly for those grappling with dementia, certain memories become more pronounced, which prompts the question of whether a brain in its nineties retraces the myelination phase and revives the neuronal firings most potent in youth.

Its answer is no, and the correction matters to the article's own thesis. Older individuals may appear to regress emotionally toward earlier memories, but that is a matter of memory retention rather than of neural structure.

The actual process runs the other way. Myelination coats nerve fibres through life and enables quicker transmission; with age some of that protective myelin degenerates, which can slow cognitive response. Rather than returning to an adolescent state, elderly brains may show areas of myelin damage, especially where neurodegenerative conditions are present.

The same applies to the pathways themselves. Neural routes formed from experience are efficient highways in a person's prime, and with time, and with conditions such as dementia, they show wear: synapse loss, neuron death, and communication that becomes less efficient.

The article records the signs it observed without qualifying them: memory degradation became evident, and yet she still knew his name in October 2021 and, recently, connected his name to his Manadonese heritage.

The correction it makes to its own question

"The brain doesn't exactly revert to its adolescent state as age advances."

What survives, and what that suggests

The two sets of memories that persist are the earliest and most emotionally charged ones. On the article's own framework, that is consistent with pathways laid during the period of heightened plasticity being the last to wear rather than with any return to it.

Conclusion

Early narratives and guiding figures decide trajectories, and the university arrives too late to supply either.

The article closes by naming the neurological and psychological foundations under everything above, and then drawing the institutional consequence.

University environments, while invaluable, cater primarily to the late adolescent and early adult brain. At this point, much of the neural wiring, especially the foundational circuits, have been established.

— Part 2: Brain Formation, Lifelong Journeys and Lessons from Prof. Sri Margono, 20 October 2023
The neurological half
Early childhood and adolescence carry heightened plasticity, with rapid growth and pruning solidifying frequently used pathways and discarding others. The hippocampus and amygdala make emotionally charged encounters into stronger memories, which are then recalled and re-lived.
The psychological half
Erikson's identity versus role confusion places adolescence as the stage where vocations and values are explored; narrative psychology holds that the stories we recall shape self-concept; and research on mentorship finds that influential figures met early serve as templates for behaviour and ambition.
What the article calls them
Sparkling events and reminiscence bumps: the pivotal experiences that fall inside the window of heightened plasticity and go on to influence neural pathways, identity formation and self-concept.
Where that leaves the programmes
Relying solely on university environments to instil an entrepreneurial spirit overlooks the influence of early life experience. The article's own testimony is offered as the evidence: the decisive input was a teacher's advice, a stranger's help, and a mentor met at seventeen.
