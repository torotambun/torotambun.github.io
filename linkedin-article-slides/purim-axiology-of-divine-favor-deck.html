(6 of 6) Purim: Axiology Question - Responsibility and Weight of Divine Favor in the Book of Esther

The series closes by asking what happens after favor arrives, and answers that favor is not a reward but an assignment. It does not sustain itself, it does not exempt its holder from risk, and it is forfeited by being hoarded. Esther's decisive moment is not receiving favor but spending it — and the book's whole ethical weight sits in that difference.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

The Question

The interesting question is not how favor is granted but what it obliges.

Situation

"Favor in the Book of Esther is never arbitrary, stagnant, or self-serving. It positions individuals for responsibility, testing their ethics, timing, and courage" (Berlin, 2001).

Complication

"But what happens after favor is granted?"

Question

"Does it sustain itself, or does it require action?" "Does it benefit only the individual, or is it entrusted for a larger purpose?" "Is it an end goal, or merely the beginning of a greater test?"

Answer

"The final axiology of favor is found not in its reception but in its application."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Properties

Three properties make favor a liability as much as a gift.

The article states them as claims about how favor behaves, not as counsel.

01
It moves
Favor is never static
"Favor is never static—it shifts according to ethical action." Haman's case is the demonstration: the same favor that elevated him convicted him within a single scene.
02
It is held in trust
Favor is not self-preserving
"Favor is not self-preserving—it is entrusted for communal and divine purposes." The holder is not the beneficiary.
03
It costs
Favor does not shield
"Favor does not shield from sacrifice—it often requires stepping into risk." Esther's position inside the palace is precisely what obliges her to leave its protection.
The pivotal claim

"The pivotal lesson of Esther is that favor does not eliminate the need for choice" — it demands it. "The one who receives favor must decide how to wield it, when to act, and whether to risk everything to fulfill its purpose."

The Test

Esther's decisive act is not receiving favor but spending it.

"Esther’s greatest test is not whether she has favor—but what she will do with it."

ARGUMENT A
Favor alone does nothing
"She is granted favor with the king" (Esther 2:17). "But favor alone is meaningless without the willingness to act."
ARGUMENT B
The warning that forces the choice
"If you remain silent at this time, relief and deliverance will arise from another place, but you and your father’s house will perish. And who knows if you have not come to your royal position for such a time as this?" (Esther 4:14)
ARGUMENT C
Three ways favor is forfeited
"Favor given but not acted upon is favor wasted." "Favor that serves self-preservation alone is favor misused." "Favor used to sustain oppression is favor inverted."
ARGUMENT D
The moment it becomes ethical
"Esther’s ethical moment does not lie in gaining favor but in risking it. She moves from passivity to leadership with her declaration" — "I will go to the king, though it is against the law. And if I perish, I perish." (Esther 4:16) "Here, favor ceases to be a mere condition—it becomes an ethical choice."
The rabbinic instruction attached

"In a place where there are no leaders, strive to be a leader." (Pirkei Avot 2:5, Neusner, 1984) And the conclusion the article draws: "Favor, in its axiological sense, is not a personal security—it is a divine assignment."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Division

Delay tests patience and manipulation tests nothing, because manufactured favor cannot be held long enough to be tested.

Mordechai and Haman occupy the two ends of the same principle.

Mordechai — favor deferred
✓"His act of loyalty—saving the king’s life—is recorded but forgotten" (Esther 2:21-23)
✓"His reward does not come immediately but at the exact moment Haman seeks his execution"
✓"favor operates within divine timing"
✓"Immediate favor is a test of humility." "Delayed favor is a test of patience and purpose."
✓"Had Mordechai’s favor been granted immediately, it would have been personal recognition. Instead, it became the catalyst for Haman’s downfall"
Haman — favor manufactured
✗"It is pursued, manipulated, and ultimately weaponized for destruction."
✗"Favor gained through deceit is inherently unstable."
✗"Favor sought for personal gain is unsustainable."
✗"Favor that serves injustice turns against its bearer."
✗"Haman’s favor exists only as long as deception sustains it. The moment truth is revealed, his power collapses instantly" — and "Haman’s artificial favor proves to be a liability rather than a legacy."
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Favor is not the end of the story but the beginning of the obligation.

"The Book of Esther dismantles the notion that favor is an ultimate reward."

Instead, it reframes favor as a divine responsibility—one that requires action, timing, and ethical grounding.

— (6 of 6) The Axiology of Divine Favor
The three cases, settled
"Esther’s favor is a trust—it is tested through her moral courage." "Mordechai’s favor is delayed—but its timing aligns with divine justice." "Haman’s favor is manipulated—and collapses under its own weight."
The questions it leaves with the reader
"Is favor being used for purpose or self-preservation?" "Is favor a burden we are willing to carry—or an illusion we cling to?" "When favor arrives, will we recognize its ethical weight?"
Where the series ends
The same verse the fifth instalment closed on — "May the favor of the Lord our God rest upon us, and establish the work of our hands." (Psalm 90:17) — and then the sentence that ends the sequence: "Favor is not an end—it is an ethical calling."
