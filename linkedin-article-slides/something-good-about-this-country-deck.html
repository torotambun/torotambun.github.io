There Must Still Be Something Good About This Country

I do not withdraw the criticism. I withdraw its monopoly. An eye trained only on what is broken makes decay the first explanation for everything — and the country has not collapsed, because people hold it together without ceremony. Counting them does not soften the failure. It makes the failure worse.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

Retired field engineers sat in a funeral house for a brother, and nobody staged it.

A scene that troubled me precisely because there was nothing remarkable about it.

Situation

A few retired Indonesian field engineers recently sat quietly in a funeral house for a man they still called a brother. "They were not hungry for validation, or for the civic titles that often pass as achievement in Indonesia: pejabat, staff ahli, deputy, direktur jenderal, anything with a formal chair, protocol line, and nameplate."

Complication

"The scene was ordinary. That was why it troubled me. No banner, no speech, no institutional choreography, no public honour." Part of my work as a cultural researcher and autoethnographer has been to examine Indonesia from the inside, including its failures — weak institutions, corrupt behaviour, short-term leadership, ecological damage, poor education, and the waste of people who should have been formed better.

Question

If the country is as damaged as I have spent years arguing, what accounts for the fact that it has not collapsed?

Answer

Something the criticism was not built to see. "There must still be something good about this country." Not the goodness of official speeches or election billboards, but the remaining good that survives where nothing is staged for recognition.

The Turn

If the eye is trained only on what is broken, decay becomes the first explanation for everything.

The criticism stands. What has to change is its claim to be the whole account.

"I do not withdraw those criticisms. The damage remains visible. Praise becomes false when it hides suffering."

"But criticism also has a danger. If the eye is trained only to name what is broken, decay becomes the first explanation for everything. Anger may sharpen, but judgment can become thinner."

Indonesia cannot be only stunting, hunger, lack of clean water, neglected villages, damaged forests, weak schools, corruption, nepotism, injustice, unreliable law, active repression of human rights, and the numbness that says one death is all right, sixty sailors lost in a submarine is all right, a village erased by landslide or tsunami is all right, 2.5 million hectares of deforestation is all right. "If it were only that, daily life would have collapsed long ago."

What the Fact of Survival Asks For

"It has not collapsed. That fact does not excuse the damage. It asks for another kind of attention."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Engineers

Competence that could travel did not need a nameplate at home.

Many had begun as local graduates recruited into a transnational oil and gas field-service company, out of Indonesian schools and universities, before thirty or thirty-five years of hard technical work.

They became "people who could be assigned across more than thirty countries without needing ceremonial importance at home." They are now in their sixties and seventies, living quietly, reasonably prosperous, and entering retirement with grace. "They had come for a brother in the old working sense of the word. Not necessarily by blood, not by rank, not by title, but by shared rigs, tools, transfers, risks, jokes, fatigue, and ageing."

They belong to a story of honest prosperity. In a country where many assume wealth comes from political access, procurement games, rent-seeking, bureaucratic rank, or proximity to power, "some people still become comfortable through skill, lawful work, mobility, patience, and a long career of delivery." Their achievement was less visible but harder to fake. "But the path was work, not theft."

Part of that came from the technical culture that formed them. "In the field, competence had to speak faster than hierarchy. A wrong decision could injure people, damage equipment, or kill someone. There was less room for excessive sungkan, isin, or wedi." For Indonesian engineers raised inside a society often too deferential to rank, that mattered.

What This Complicates

The complaint about nearly eighty years of independence still has weight; many institutions have failed people who deserved better. "But the retired field-engineer circle does not fit neatly into a story of total failure. Local schools and local universities did produce people who could work across borders, obey the law, keep learning for decades, and retire without stealing from people." That is not a small thing.

The Inventory

The remaining good survives in kitchens, funeral houses, prayer rooms and old work circles.

Not the goodness advertised in official speeches, placed on billboards before elections, or performed in ceremonial nationalism. These are the places where it is actually kept.

1  ·  Moral memory
"People still remember who stood with them before status arrived and after status disappeared." This memory is not always clean — it can be selective, sentimental, unfair, and trapped in old obligations. "But it still keeps some social debt alive, and not all debt is corrupt. Some debts remind us that we did not survive alone."
2  ·  The funeral house
"A funeral house reveals this better than a national speech." People arrive, sit with the family, bring food, speak quietly, and stay longer than efficiency would allow. "A person's death becomes a test of who still remembers the human being beneath the role. The retired engineers passed that test by coming, sitting, and staying."
3  ·  Food as a language of care
"People may not say affection directly. They cook it, carry it, and place it before others." At funerals, after illness, during visits, after childbirth, before travel, food moves between people before words become adequate. "It does not erase poverty. It does not repair injustice. But it prevents many encounters from becoming empty."
4  ·  The stubborn life of group belonging
Some of this group life can be suffocating; it can police difference and exhaust people who need solitude. "But it also resists the loneliness that many wealthier societies now manage through therapy, pets, apps, and scheduled friendship."
5  ·  Family, as a difficult good
"Still, family continues to hold many lives that would otherwise fall through broken institutions. Parents pay school fees beyond their means. Grandmothers raise children when parents migrate for work. Older siblings become second parents too early." Much of Indonesia's social stability rests on these private burdens, many of them unfair, many invisible, many carried without ceremony.
6  ·  Small public decency
There are public servants who refuse to humiliate citizens, working inside badly designed systems with poor pay and angry citizens. "A few still explain forms without contempt. A few still treat the poor as citizens, not interruptions. Their goodness is small only if one has never stood powerless before a desk." The same is true of teachers, nurses, midwives, neighbourhood women and informal helpers whose names rarely enter policy documents.

The Refusals

Every one of these goods can be turned into a weapon, and I will not pretend otherwise.

Moral language remains alive. It is also violated constantly, and most often against the people least able to object.

How Each Word Gets Turned
✗Malu is sometimes used to control the weak rather than restrain the strong
✗Sabar can become a weapon against those asking for justice
✗Syukur can be used to make the poor accept what should not be accepted
✗Amanah is spoken loudly by people who betray trust
Why the Words Still Matter
✓They keep expectations alive: a society that can name shame, restraint, gratitude and mutual help has not lost its inner grammar
✓People still know when a public official humiliates a citizen
✓They still know when a rich person behaves without tahu diri
✓They still know the difference between patience and surrender, even when they cannot act on it
No Romanticising the Family Either

"The Indonesian family can be unfair. It can ask too much from mothers, daughters, older siblings, unmarried relatives, and those who earn a little more than the rest. It can hide violence. It can turn duty into emotional blackmail. It can silence those who need protection. No honest praise should deny this."

What It Revealed

Decent people make institutional failure more painful, not less.

This is the part that surprised me. The inventory does not soften the indictment; it sharpens it.

"These are not decorative stories. They do not cancel institutional failure. They make institutional failure more painful because they show that the country still has people better than many of the systems around them."

"A corrupt system is worse when decent people are forced to spend their lives compensating for it. A weak school is worse when one sees how much a good teacher can still do inside it. A broken bureaucracy is worse when one meets a public servant who proves humiliation was never necessary."

"Counting these remaining goods is not propaganda. Propaganda praises in order to hide damage. This is different. This is an attempt to see the country without lying about either side of it."

What I Carry

Indonesia is not innocent. Indonesia is not finished either.

The Indonesia I need to inventory more carefully is made of people who receive too little honour for the work of holding.

The mother who stretches money without making sacrifice into spectacle. The father who carries debt quietly. The grandmother who keeps the household intact. The teacher who remembers a difficult student. The neighbour who brings food before being asked. The nurse who treats a poor patient with dignity. The small public servant who refuses to humiliate citizens. The retired engineer who becomes comfortable without stealing. The old colleague who still comes to the funeral house.

— The inventory, as the article states it
The verdict, held open
"Indonesia is not innocent. Indonesia is not finished either. It is damaged, unjust, often cruel, often absurd, and still held together by people who receive too little honour for the work of holding."
The criticism continues
"I will continue writing about what weakens Indonesia. But I also need to count our blessings and be fair." There are still ties holding families, neighbourhoods, classrooms, prayer rooms, kitchens, funeral houses, old work circles, and ordinary people together.
Where the ties were visible
"At the funeral house, those ties were visible in retired field engineers who had come quietly for their brother."
