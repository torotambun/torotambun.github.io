From Woods to World-Changing Ideas: Personal Discipline Meets MIT’s Ecosystem

The campus he arrived at in 1973 was cramped, and the crampedness turned out to be the mechanism: proximity forced the crossings that made the ideas. His own life runs on the same principle at personal scale — a mile and a half through the woods every day — and both are the frame for a dissertation on entrepreneurial ecosystems in Indonesia.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

The man beside me had quietly shaped fifty years of energy engineering.

A ten-seat dinner table at the entrance, in the centre of the room, at an MIT Club of Indonesia event in Jakarta.

It was not the most private seating arrangement, and it was the third time our paths had crossed — but sitting next to him, the conversation felt more personal than it had before. We reflected on professional journeys that had both passed through MIT, at different times and with different focuses.

At the start I handed him a copy of my book, Disciplined Storytelling, published in April 2024. It reflects my journey through leadership, innovation and storytelling, and was inspired by MIT's Patrick Winston, who emphasised "storytelling, writing ability, and quality of ideas—in that order." He had not taught me personally; sharing the book felt like contributing to an intellectual legacy MIT fosters among its alumni.

The Path

He reached MIT in 1973 as the first non-MIT alumnus on the chemical engineering faculty.

A route through the places where American energy was being built.

The 1950s
A childhood inside the energy industry
His early years were spent in regions central to the burgeoning energy industry of the decade, surrounded by oil refineries and the development of new technologies. His schooling took place in key energy hubs, shaping an early understanding of how power and industry intertwine.
Undergraduate
Heat transfer, learned in the South
He pursued engineering at a prominent technical institute in the South, refining his understanding of complex systems such as heat transfer — which would later become pivotal to his work.
Graduate
The Midwest, and a widening of the field
At a renowned Midwestern university with a strong engineering programme, the combination of industrial exposure and cutting-edge research broadened his expertise in chemical engineering.
1973
MIT, and a bridge to nuclear energy
He became the first non-MIT alumni faculty member in the Chemical Engineering Department — a pivotal moment that bridged chemical engineering with the emerging field of nuclear energy.

The Gap

The cramped campus he resented turned out to be the mechanism.

Coming from a scenic Midwestern campus, MIT's urban setting was a stark contrast.

The campus felt cramped, the buildings tightly clustered, and the Charles River alongside it was heavily polluted. Back then, he recalled, "the Charles was not the clean, inviting river it is today. Sailing was popular at MIT, but if your boat capsized, you had to be pulled out quickly and taken to the medical center for a tetanus booster."

Over time he saw the advantage of the compact environment. Walking down a hallway meant passing from chemical engineering to chemistry, then biology, and even biotechnology or physics. Those interactions were not merely convenient; they were critical to the interdisciplinary collaborations that sparked new ideas.

The proximity was not a byproduct of the campus layout — it was the foundation of MIT's success as a hub for innovation. Nobel laureates including David Baltimore and Phillip Sharp, whose work in biology often intersected with chemical engineering, were regular collaborators (Boud & Lee, 2005; Kezar & Gehrke, 2017).

Framework

Proximity, recruitment and orchestration are what make the ecosystem work.

Three mechanisms, none of which is a building or a budget line.

01
Distance
Proximity across disciplines
A layout that makes crossing between fields unavoidable produces daily interaction between departments at a level the article calls unparalleled. The culture of interdisciplinary engagement is ingrained in the structure itself.
02
Hiring
Experience gained elsewhere first
MIT's recruitment culture encourages its alumni to gain experience elsewhere before returning to teach, which ensures faculty bring diverse perspectives into the interdisciplinary environment rather than reproducing it unchanged.
03
Coordination
The ecosystem orchestrator
Budden and Murray describe MIT as an "ecosystem orchestrator", integrating academia, industry and government to foster entrepreneurial activity. The orchestration of those relationships, formal and informal, is the transferable part.

Details

Chemical engineering built the first reactors, and hydro made them workable.

The part of the conversation that landed on the author's own first discipline.

1  ·  Where the nuclear industry got its framework
Much of the American nuclear industry's design and operational framework came from chemical engineering principles in the 1950s, especially in heat transfer and reactor design.
2  ·  Why hydro plants sat next door
Early reactors required steady-state operation to maintain efficiency and could not easily ramp power up or down. Hydroelectric plants were often located nearby to manage the load, providing peak power while the nuclear plants ran constant — and recharging during off-peak hours.
3  ·  Why the pairing was a safety matter
Unlike modern designs, older systems depended on a complex relationship between reactor control rods and external energy systems. Adjusting the rods to manage the fission reaction was less precise then, which made the hydroelectric backup critical to overall system safety and stability.
An aside, carried as his

He noted that Pope Francis trained as a chemical engineer in Argentina before his religious calling, and mentioned that President Xi Jinping holds a chemical engineering degree from Tsinghua University in Beijing, earned in the mid-1970s as the country's energy and industrial activities were rapidly expanding. Both points are relayed as his, in the article's own reporting register.

The Parallel

He walked a mile and a half through the woods; I sold all my cars.

Two disciplines arrived at separately, recognised across a dinner table.

His practice
•For nearly a decade he chose not to own a car
•"I walked a mile and a half through the woods every day" — near his home in Lexington, then a commuter bus to MIT
•A student in China was shocked that an American professor did not own a car
•A daily walking and jogging routine, carried with Thich Nhat Hanh recited along it
•His phone opened once in the whole conversation, to show a collection of those books
Mine
•In 2017 I sold all my cars
•After a sustainability course at MIT led by Professor John Sterman in October 2016
•The transition was not easy — there were challenges at home, especially convincing my wife
•A disciplined exercise routine of my own, aligning daily habits with broader principles
•The same shape of decision, reached from a different direction
What he recited on the walk

"I have arrived, I am home. In the here, in the now. I am solid, I am free. In the ultimate I dwell." It was not just about exercise — it was how he approaches life, balancing innovation with personal well-being, and it showed in his being fully present at a table where others would have reached for a phone.

What I Carry

The teaching that lasts is the permission to apply a principle anywhere.

A design course he taught, and what he wanted from it.

I wanted them to see that their principles could be applied to anything

— The professor, quoted in the article
The result he can point to
Many of his students went on to build successful companies — a testament to the open-minded approach, and a mirror of the author's own teaching philosophy of helping students think beyond what is expected.
Fifty years in, still forward-looking
He spoke with excitement about an upcoming study on fusion energy, bringing together experts from multiple disciplines to explore fusion's potential in a deeply decarbonised future. The enthusiasm remained undimmed.
Why this conversation is in a dissertation
Innovation ecosystems arise from diverse, interacting components (Autio & Thomas, 2014), and MIT's blending of academia and industry is the model the author carries into research on entrepreneurial ecosystems in Indonesia. The small deliberate actions — walking miles to cut a carbon footprint, guiding students past traditional boundaries — are the same principle at personal scale.
