Stop 3: Core Concepts in Philosophy of Science

The third instalment narrows from philosophy to philosophy of science and finds a discipline organised around what science cannot settle about itself. Demarcation comes first because it decides what counts as science at all. Reasoning is then argued to be the wrong kind of thing to list beside ontology, epistemology and axiology, since it cuts across all three, and each mode of reasoning binds to particular ontological positions rather than floating free of them. The central section is Kuhn's: theory does not accumulate but breaks, normal science runs inside a paradigm until anomalies accumulate into crisis, and incommensurability means the successor cannot be scored against its predecessor on shared terms. Underdetermination adds that data alone cannot choose between rival theories. What remains open at the end is the realism debate, which asks whether the unobservable entities of successful theories are real or merely useful.

Introduction

Three figures set the terms, and each of them limited what science can claim.

The instalment opens on the people rather than the problems, naming three whose contributions constrain rather than extend scientific confidence.

Karl Popper "Advocated for the falsifiability criterion of scientific theories", which makes vulnerability to refutation the mark of a scientific claim rather than weight of confirming evidence.

Thomas Kuhn "Introduced the idea of paradigm shifts in scientific progress", replacing the picture of steady accumulation with one of periodic rupture. Willard Van Orman Quine "Questioned the distinction between analytical and synthetic statements, impacting the philosophy of logic and mathematics".

The discipline's three stated purposes follow the same pattern of scrutiny rather than support: to provide a rigorous foundation for scientific inquiry, to critically evaluate the methods and findings of various disciplines, and to offer a platform for debating the ethical implications of scientific developments.

The first question, because it decides what counts

The demarcation problem is "The issue of distinguishing between science and non-science or pseudoscience", and the article calls it "Fundamental for understanding what qualifies as scientific knowledge and methodology." Its worked example is astrology against astronomy, separated by empirical testing, falsifiability and methodological rigour.

Gap

Reasoning is not a fourth category beside ontology, epistemology and axiology; it runs through all of them.

This is the instalment's own contribution rather than a summary of the field, and the article flags it as such. Reasoning "is not typically listed as a separate category like ontology, epistemology, and axiology, but it is a critical element that cuts across these categories."

The argument is made by showing where it operates in each. In ontology, reasoning shapes "how we define and categorize entities and phenomena in the world". In epistemology it is "central to the processes of observation, hypothesis formulation, experimentation, and inference". In axiology it "guides us in making value-laden decisions about the conduct of research".

It reaches further down as well, into methodology, methods and sources, where it governs the selection of design and the judgement of which data are credible.

The consequence is that reasoning cannot be bolted on after a position is chosen. "Throughout the entire research process, from formulating research questions to drawing conclusions, reasoning is the intellectual foundation that guides how researchers' approach, analyze, and interpret information."

Why this matters for the instalments that follow

"Different philosophical orientations and research paradigms may emphasize different forms of reasoning, such as deductive, inductive, or abductive reasoning, depending on their epistemological and methodological underpinnings." The article then maps ten ontological positions onto the modes of reasoning each tends to use, which is the connective tissue between Stop 2 and Stop 5.

Framework

Three modes of reasoning, and each binds to a different kind of research claim.

The article gives each a concept, a relevance, an example and, unusually, a location in the logical systems set out at the previous stop.

1  ·  Deductive
"Starting from general principles to derive specific conclusions." Formalised through classical logic, it "provides conclusive proof of its conclusions when the premises are true", and is associated with positivist and post-positivist paradigms. Located in classical and mathematical logic.
2  ·  Inductive
"Using specific observations to form general conclusions." Not formalised through classical logic since it deals in probability. "Inductive reasoning does not guarantee absolute truth but aims to establish patterns and probabilities." Used by interpretivist and constructivist paradigms to build context-specific understandings.
3  ·  Abductive
"Inference to the best explanation." Heuristic and less formal, combining evidence with existing knowledge to propose plausible explanations, and common in qualitative and interpretive research. Its example is the fossil record and evolution as the best available account.
4  ·  Five more the article adds
Analogical, drawing parallels between similar situations. Critical, evaluating arguments and assumptions for validity and bias. Hypothetical-deductive, which "combines deductive and inductive reasoning to refine and validate theories". Anecdotal, which the article marks as "less rigorous than other forms of reasoning". And probabilistic, which "acknowledges uncertainty and incorporates probability distributions into decision-making".

Evidence

Theory does not accumulate, it breaks, and the replacement cannot be scored on the old terms.

The central section is Kuhn's, and the article states the departure from the standard picture directly. "Kuhn argued that science doesn't progress via a linear accumulation of new knowledge, but undergoes periodic revolutions, or paradigm shifts."

The mechanism is attrition. Normal science operates inside the prevailing paradigm; anomalies are "unexpected results that cannot be explained by the existing paradigm", and a crisis comes when they accumulate. The worked case is the precession of Mercury's orbit, unexplained by Newtonian mechanics and accounted for by relativity.

A paradigm is more than a theory: it "dictates the kinds of questions scientists should ask, the methods they should use, and how they should interpret the results". Which is why replacing one is not an ordinary correction.

And why comparison fails at the moment of replacement. Incommensurability is "The idea that theories from different paradigms are often incomparable because they use different sets of terms and concepts", as in the move from Ptolemaic to Copernican astronomy, which changed the basic terms as well as the claims.

The second limit on what evidence can settle

Underdetermination is "The notion that empirical data alone cannot determine the choice between multiple theories." The example given is quantum mechanics and string theory, both explaining certain phenomena while resting on different premises. Falsifiability, Popper's criterion, is offered as the counterweight: "all swans are white" is scientific precisely because one black swan disposes of it.

Implications

Knowing how paradigms work changes what a researcher does, not just what they know.

Seven reasons are given for studying paradigms and paradigm shifts, and most of them are practical rather than historical.

1  ·  It explains why good ideas get resisted
"Paradigms, once established, develop significant inertia." The existence of a dominant paradigm accounts for revolutionary findings being overlooked, since "Scientists, like all people, can be influenced by prevailing norms and biases."
2  ·  It changes the status of an anomaly
"They aren't just odd outliers; they can be signals that the current paradigm might be insufficient." A result that does not fit becomes worth pursuing rather than worth discarding.
3  ·  It positions the researcher's own work
"Paradigms inform what is considered legitimate research within a field. They shape research questions, methodologies, and interpretations." Knowing the prevailing paradigm is how a researcher locates a contribution within it.
4  ·  It applies outside science
The article extends the concept to business, naming the shift from traditional retail to e-commerce as a paradigm shift where recognition "can be crucial for survival and success", and to culture and society, where recognising shifts helps adaptation to changing norms.
The disposition the article recommends

"If history has shown that dominant ideas can be overturned, it encourages current thinkers to remain receptive to new and challenging perspectives." Intellectual humility is presented as a conclusion drawn from the history rather than as a virtue asserted.

Conclusion

What is left open is whether electrons are real or merely useful.

The instalment closes on the realism debate, which is the question the whole journey's ontology sections have been building toward.

Scientific realism
•Theories are, or can be, true descriptions of the world
•Includes unobservable entities such as electrons and quarks
•Justifies science as a means of achieving true beliefs
•Argues from success: the Standard Model implies quarks exist
•Sits with ontological realism at the previous stop
Anti-realism
•The aim is empirically adequate or useful models, not true descriptions
•Unobservable entities need not be committed to
•Critiques the objectivity of science, stressing instrumental value
•The Copenhagen interpretation: the wave function is a predictive tool
•Sits with instrumentalism at the previous stop
Two further distinctions the instalment closes on

The epistemology of science examines "what constitutes scientific knowledge, evidence, and methodology", with statistical significance as its worked case. The metaphysics of science investigates "the ontological commitments and assumptions that underlie scientific theories and practice", and its example is exactly the question above: whether electrons are real entities or useful fictions.
