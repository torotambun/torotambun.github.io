Understanding the Venn Diagram "Paying Customer, Fit-Value Proposition, and Operational Excellence"

The article sets out how one teacher organises an entrepreneurship course, and its structure carries an argument that the syllabus alone would hide. Three circles name three competences, the paying customer, the fit value proposition and operational excellence, and each maps onto a recognised strategy tradition. But the intersection where they meet is not a fourth skill. It is a question about motive, and the article answers it in a way that excludes most of the people who ask: if the reason is money, this is not for you. Behind the diagram sits a competence rubric sorted into Heart, Home, Hands and Heads, and Heart holds what families and early childhood install, which is where the dissertation's argument about formation enters a course syllabus rather than a research paper. The technical contribution is a distinction: the Paying Customer-Based View is narrower than the Market-Based View, because it concerns only the customers who actually pay, and the first five of Aulet's twenty-four steps are read as exactly that narrowing.

Introduction

The centre of the diagram is a question about motive, not a competence.

Three circles intersect, and the article is explicit that what sits in the overlap is the thing it considers most important, and that it is not a skill.

Comprised of 3 circles which has the intersection that is the most crucial component: "Why are you here?"

— Understanding the Venn Diagram, 17 August 2023
The answer that disqualifies
"If the answer is money or wanting to be rich, definitely this is not for you." Stated at the start of a course rather than discovered at the end of one.
The answer that qualifies
"If the answer is “it is my calling that I must be a blessing to others, all races, religion and creatures “Rahmatan lil Alamin or Tikkun Olam” then this may be your cup of tea." The same two traditions appear in the axiology set out eight days later in the Berfilsafat article.
Why the diagram is taught first
The model "is pivotal for my understanding of what I think people need to learn first when they encounter “entrepreneurship”." It is presented as the entry point to the subject rather than as a summary of it.
What the three circles correspond to
Each is matched to a strategy tradition. The paying customer focus belongs to someone who is "an expert of applying “market-based theory”". The fit value proposition focus belongs to someone who "can translate the jobs to be done question". And operational excellence belongs to "an expert of “resource-based theory”".

Framework

The competence rubric sorts into Heart, Home and Hands, and Heart is the one a course cannot teach.

Behind the diagram sits a rubric correlating Morris et al on entrepreneurial competences with Aulet's twenty-four steps, and the ordering of its categories is the argument.

01
What arrives before the course
Heart
Named in the article as "Shared Values from families and early childhood". It holds tenacity and perseverance, resilience and anti-fragility, guerrilla skills described as seeing the unseen, self-efficacy in pitching, and conveying a compelling vision. These are the competences the rubric assumes rather than develops.
02
What the ecosystem supplies
Home
"common language and ecosystem", covering building and using networks, mapped to steps 2, 9, 12 and 14, and resource leveraging at steps 10 and 11. This is the layer a university or an accelerator can actually provide.
03
What the steps train
Hands
Opportunity recognition at step 1, which the article marks as the most time consuming. Opportunity assessment across steps 1 to 10. Risk management at steps 20, 21 and 23. Creative problem-solving at 6, 7, 22 and 24. Value creation across steps 12 to 19. And maintaining focus while adapting, mapped to all the return steps.
The fourth category, and what sits in it

Heads and Hands holds the methods rather than the traits: PMR techniques, reflexive ethnography, the 24 steps themselves, xTeam and 4CAP+ and the Signature Leadership Framework, and embedded project management. The dissertation's own instruments appear inside a teaching rubric.

Details

The Paying Customer-Based View is narrower than the Market-Based View, and the narrowing is the point.

The article makes a distinction it says is deliberate, opening the section by stating that it is talking about PCBV and not MBV.

Market-Based View
•Anchors its philosophy in the firm's external environment
•Competitive advantage from responding to market dynamics
•Covers customer preferences, competitor actions, industry forces
•Recognises that static advantages are ephemeral
•Leads to dynamic capabilities, continuously renewed
Paying Customer-Based View
✓Goes beyond general market trends
✓An intensified form of customer-centricity
✓Zooms in on the segment that directly contributes revenue
✓Not users or consumers but engaged, paying entities
✓Mapped onto the first five of Aulet's twenty-four steps
How the first five steps do the narrowing

Market segmentation establishes the landscape. The beachhead market "embodies the initial set of customers that the firm believes will be its most likely paying customers." The end user profile asks what they need. Total addressable market quantifies the revenue. And the persona profiles "give a tangible face to the paying customer", which the article calls crucial for designing features and campaigns.

Evidence

The resource side is widened well past VRIN, into thirteen capabilities a founder actually needs.

The article accepts the standard resource-based view and then argues that its categories are too narrow for how modern firms actually compete.

1  ·  The standard position, stated first
"RBV contends that a firm's resources, both tangible and intangible, are central to achieving a competitive advantage. These resources need to be valuable, rare, inimitable, and non-substitutable". The framework "assumes that the variation in resources among firms is the primary reason for differences in firm performance."
2  ·  Why intangibles now dominate
"In the digital age, where firms like Apple have built their empire on brand value and firms like Google leverage massive data-driven insights, the ability to manage and monetize intangible assets becomes paramount."
3  ·  Team and leadership as resources
Team dynamics covering "collaboration, diversity of thought, adaptability, and resilience". And Ancona and Bresman's 4CAPS+ model, with sensemaking, relating, visioning and inventing, where "The “+” signifies the noble personal values a leader brings, suggesting that while collective skills are essential, a leader's integrity and purpose remains pivotal."
4  ·  The rest of the thirteen
Storytelling ability, because narrative "can galvanize both internal stakeholders (employees, leadership) and external ones (customers, investors)". Then fundraising and fund management, operational discipline, iterative capability, sales and customer maintenance, marketing across the 4Ps and 7Ps, business model innovation, scaling, supply chain management, corporate finance and accounting.

Conclusion

Alertness spots the opportunity; commitment is a separate thing and it can be irrational.

The closing section separates two ideas usually run together, and is candid about one mechanism that sustains commitment for bad reasons.

while entrepreneurial alertness pertains to the cognitive capacity to recognize opportunities, entrepreneurial commitment relates to the willingness and dedication to pursue and actualize those opportunities.

— Understanding the Venn Diagram, 17 August 2023
What raises commitment
Perceived feasibility, meaning belief in one's ability to execute. Perceived desirability. Resource availability. Personal investment. And social networks, where "Encouragement and support from peers, mentors, and other stakeholders can boost commitment."
The mechanism it names honestly
"As entrepreneurs invest more time, money, and effort into a venture, they might become more committed", and the article adds that this can be irrational, driven by not wanting the investment to go to waste. The sunk cost fallacy is listed as a driver of commitment rather than as an error to avoid.
And path dependence alongside it
"initial commitments and decisions might lead to further commitment due to this path-dependent trajectory." Both mechanisms make commitment grow for reasons unrelated to whether the opportunity is still good.
The attribution it declines to make
On entrepreneurial commitment the article says "it's hard to pin it down to a single author or work, as multiple scholars have discussed the importance of commitment, passion, and persistence", naming Per Davidsson on the nascent entrepreneurial process rather than claiming an originator.

Sources

This article carries the fullest reference list of the batch, with twelve entries including three of the author's own LinkedIn posts cited formally as prior work. The competence rubric it rests on is credited to a correlation between Morris et al and Aulet that the author published separately. Several diagrams, including the Venn diagram itself and the rubric table, are images that do not survive text extraction, so the central visual of the article is absent from the deck. The step numbering in the body skips from 5 to 9 and from 8 to 10 because the steps are grouped by which view they serve rather than listed in sequence.
