(3 of 5 Part 1) To Wish to Return to Childhood: A Philosophical Reckoning

Where the physicist measured the wish and found it impossible, the philosopher refuses to dismiss it and interrogates it instead. Nine frameworks put nine different questions to the same sentence, and none of them answers yes — but the refusal is not the point. What the frameworks establish is that you cannot return to childhood and can return from it, and that knowing why the question was asked is what keeps the whole exercise from becoming a game.

Opening

To say you wish to return to childhood is a philosophical utterance, not a sentimental one.

Situation

"A physicist might measure time’s asymmetry. A theologian might call it longing for Eden."

Complication

"But the philosopher? The philosopher hears in that wish a question about the structure of memory, the weight of becoming, and the desire to return to the origin of questioning itself."

Question

"What is the self across time?"

Answer

"To say “I wish I could return to my childhood” is not naïve. It is a profound philosophical utterance — an admission that time wounds, that memory fragments, and that identity is never whole in the present."

Three Answers

Three traditions agree childhood is not a place, and disagree about what it is instead.

Before the frameworks, the article gives three direct responses.

01
Husserl, Ricoeur
Phenomenological
"childhood is not an object “back there” in chronological space. It lives within us as temporal consciousness". "we are not asking for coordinates in time. We are longing to inhabit time differently". So "the task is not to return to childhood, but to remember how to return from it".
02
Kierkegaard, Heidegger
Existential
"To wish to return is also to flee. It reveals our resistance to the weight of freedom". Childhood "is not merely innocence — it is unconscious being, life before radical responsibility." The question that follows: "Are we longing for what was — or for what has never been fully lived?"
03
Augustine, Bachelard
Metaphysical
"Time, Augustine said, is a distension of the soul. We do not move through time; time moves through us." So "childhood is not a chapter — it is a dimension." Bachelard's early rooms and gardens "are not memories. They are anchors of being."
What wonder has to do with it

"In Plato’s Republic, Socrates doesn’t seek to preserve power — he seeks to protect the soul’s capacity to learn, a journey that begins with wonder (thaumazein) — the same wonder a child experiences before knowledge calcifies into certainty." Hence: "It’s the desire to see the world again as something not yet owned, not yet named."

The Frameworks

Nine frameworks put nine different questions to the same sentence.

Each is stated as a question rather than a doctrine, which is what lets them be compared.

1  ·  Ontology — what is childhood?
"Ontologically, childhood is not "back there" — it's a temporal mode of being we once inhabited and may still carry." Heidegger on being as time-bound; Bergson on carrying a rhythm rather than returning to a moment.
2  ·  Epistemology — can I know it?
"Memory is partial, fragile, and often reconstructed." Ricoeur: "we don't remember the past directly; we narrate it." So "To say "I know my childhood" is to say: I know what I need it to mean, now."
3  ·  Axiology — why does it matter?
"MacIntyre shows us modernity has fractured moral traditions. Childhood might represent a time when values were still whole. The desire to return is the desire for coherence, rootedness, and moral clarity."
4  ·  Continental — language, power, trace
"Derrida says the "childhood" we seek is already a trace — never fully present, always shaped by the language we use to describe it. Foucault challenges our assumption of innocence". Ricoeur returns it: "The longing to return is also the longing to re-narrate."
5  ·  Analytic — is the question coherent?
"Gilbert Ryle would warn against category errors — childhood is not a place you can travel to. Wittgenstein would ask: what language game are you playing?" "The analytic tradition doesn’t mock your longing, but it demands you clarify".
6  ·  Virtue ethics — who desires this?
"Aristotle might say: the desire to return is the desire to flourish. MacIntyre hears mourning in your voice — mourning a broken moral world. C.S. Lewis sees hope: childhood as the place where virtue first stirs."
7  ·  Existentialism — what does it reveal?
"Sartre says: you are fleeing your freedom. Kierkegaard says: you are in anxiety before becoming. Camus urges: revolt against absurdity." "To return is impossible. But to become? That is still open."
8  ·  Pragmatism — what use is the longing?
"James would ask: does the idea help you live better? Dewey: does it help you grow? Rorty: does it help you re-describe your self?" "Not because it is true in the abstract, but because it makes moral, emotional, and practical sense."
9  ·  Theology — is return a sacred act?
"Teshuvah (Judaism) and Fitrah (Islam) both teach: returning is not regression. It is spiritual restoration." The article flags this one as differently constituted, and hands it to a later instalment.

The Method

The article is built in three depths so a reader can choose how far in to go.

It opens with what it calls the easiest chunk — the nine frameworks in a paragraph each — then repeats them at length, then narrows to the three foundational branches beneath them all.

Those three are ontology, epistemology and axiology. On the first: "What is childhood? Is it a stage of biological development? A psychological state? A metaphysical condition of being unburdened?" And the harder version — "Is the self continuous over time? If I “return,” who is returning? The same “I” or a new subject looking back?"

The article's own answer to that branch is careful: "Childhood may not exist ontologically as a location in time, but it may still persist within the structure of the self."

The fourth movement is the desktop exercise itself, which the article begins here and hands to the next instalment to complete.

The Motif

Knowing why the question was asked is what keeps philosophy from becoming a game.

The article says plainly where the question came from, and it is not curiosity.

Knowing the motif is paramount. It turns philosophy from a game into a form of resistance, remembrance, and clarity.

— (3 of 5 Part 1) To Wish to Return to Childhood
What prompted it
"It emerged from a deep unease — a fear rooted in the current collapse of clarity, truth, and shared meaning. The falling Rupiah, the rise of authoritarian nostalgia, and a public sphere addicted to performance and spectacle have made me, once trained in engineering but now seeking grounding, ask: Can I return to childhood?"
What philosophy is being used for
"Not out of naivety — but out of the need to reclaim something that feels lost. And so, I turned to philosophy. Not as abstract speculation, but as a tool for confronting the now."
The answer the frameworks converge on
"You cannot return to childhood. But you can return from it — and bring something back." "You can bring back wonder. You can bring back trust. You can bring back the courage to be unfinished." "And that is not regression. It is recovery."
