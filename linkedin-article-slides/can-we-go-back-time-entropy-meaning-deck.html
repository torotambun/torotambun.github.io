(1 of 5) Can we go back: A Journey Through Time, Entropy, and Meaning

The wish to return to childhood is not sentimental; it is a request for clarity in a year that has stopped supplying it. Physics answers it flatly — four independent reasons close the door — and in doing so reframes the question. The danger was never that we cannot go back. It is that a society can go backward without deciding to.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

The wish to go back is a demand for clarity, not for innocence.

Situation

"They say the future is here. But look around — the Rupiah has hit its lowest point in 27 years, fear is resurfacing, and truth is drowned in a sea of noise, and even our heroes have changed — from those who built quietly to those who podcast endlessly."

Complication

"It feels like we’ve moved forward, only to land in a worse past. We wanted to move forward. But now, it feels like history is looping — entropy in reverse, not by design, but by decay."

Question

"There are some questions we never truly outgrow: Can we go back? Why does time move forward? What is lost — and what can be understood — as the world grows louder and more distracted?"

Answer

"Maybe you’ve felt that too — not for nostalgia, but for clarity." "Is my wish naïve? Maybe. But is it impossible? Not necessarily."

The Physics

Four independent reasons close the door, and entropy is only the first of them.

"physics, that stern gatekeeper of what is and isn't possible, seems to offer four strong reasons why rewinding time may not be in the cards".

1  ·  Entropy
"in an isolated system, entropy always increases. This is not just a trend — it's a law." Local order is always bought: "Even when we create order, we are merely pushing disorder outward." Reversal is "not impossible in theory, just so statistically improbable that the universe has never done it".
2  ·  The arrow of time
"This isn’t a separate law, but more a symptom of entropy. It’s why we see cause leading to effect, not the other way around. It's why we remember the past, not the future."
3  ·  Causality
"In physics, it's the principle that a cause must precede its effect. Without it, science would crumble." Davies and Thorne have explored routes around the paradoxes, "but for now, causality still holds the high ground."
4  ·  The cosmic microwave background
"the universe’s baby photo". "It tells a clear story: the universe has been expanding, cooling, and aging in one direction for 13.8 billion years." Its uniformity gives "not only a timestamp, but a thermodynamic orientation".
What entropy is not

"It's not about rewinding the universe like a movie. It's not a reset button. In reality, entropy is a direction — a cosmic current we all move along." The answer to why we cannot go back is therefore not local: "because the entire universe is going forward."

The Fiction

One film bends physics that exists and the other invents physics that does not.

"Science fiction has always served as a playground for physics."

Tenet — no mechanism
✗The premise is a machine allowing objects and people "to invert their entropy"
✗"Entropy is statistical. You’d need to restore every particle in a system to a highly improbable earlier configuration"
✗Reversing one object inside a forward-running room "would be like unsmashing a glass while the room around it continues aging normally"
✗"That’s not just improbable. It’s computationally absurd."
✗On particles annihilating their own future selves: "This isn’t backed by any current physics." "An electron is an electron, whether it was created yesterday or nanoseconds ago."
Interstellar — grounded, then speculative
✓"Time dilation due to gravity is very real. We’ve measured it with atomic clocks on airplanes and GPS satellites."
✓Miller's Planet, where "one hour on the planet equals seven Earth years" — "extreme, but theoretically plausible"
✓Wormholes "are allowed as solutions in general relativity, but we’ve never observed one" and would need exotic matter
✓The tesseract lets Cooper access "a three-dimensional cross-section of all moments in his daughter’s bedroom" — "Time is no longer a line; it’s a landscape."
✓"In this moment, Interstellar leaves science behind — and embraces storytelling. But it does so with respect for the boundaries of what we know and don’t know."

The Paradox

The grandfather paradox has four resolutions, and each draws the boundary of the possible somewhere else.

"any consistent theory of time must address it."

1  ·  Alternate histories
"When you go back and change something, you don’t alter your own past — you create a new timeline." Aligned with Everett's 1957 interpretation: "causality is preserved, but now it’s distributed across a multiverse."
2  ·  Self-consistency
Novikov and Thorne: "even if you could travel to the past, you cannot change it. The universe would "correct" your actions to preserve consistency." "You are free to act, but your actions must align with the timeline that led to your existence."
3  ·  Time travel is impossible
Hawking's Chronology Protection Conjecture "posits that the laws of physics prevent closed timelike curves (time loops) from forming." "nature simply "protects" history from being rewritten."
4  ·  Causality is not fundamental
"causality might emerge rather than be hard-coded into the universe." "If time itself is emergent, then backward time travel might not violate causality — because causality wouldn't be fundamental to begin with."
What the paradox is actually testing

"physics isn't just about particles and equations — it's about consistency. About asking what kind of universe allows for both freedom and logic to coexist." "It's a stress test for the structure of time — and for the resilience of reason."

The Speculation

Parallel worlds are mathematically serious and empirically empty, and closing that gap needs machines nobody has built.

Three frameworks generate them: Everett's Many-Worlds, where "every quantum event causes the universe to split into all possible outcomes"; string theory, where "other branes — other universes — may exist parallel to ours, separated only by a few Planck lengths"; and eternal inflation, "constantly bubbling off new “pocket universes” in a vast multiverse."

"What unites these theories is that they’re not myths. They’re mathematically rigorous, often derived from attempts to solve deep problems in quantum gravity, unification, or cosmology. But — and this is crucial — none of them have been observed directly."

"No telescope has spotted a neighboring universe. No experiment has confirmed the existence of a parallel brane." "They are not proven — but neither are they disproven."

What testing them would take is instrumentation rather than imagination — colliders beyond the LHC, quantum computers able to simulate quantum gravity, next-generation CMB mapping, and interferometers more sensitive than LIGO. "The scale of these experiments — both in cost and complexity — demands global cooperation."

"Because to detect another universe, you may first need to unify this one."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Turn

The real risk is not that we cannot return, but that we are going backward without choosing to.

"Physics hasn’t taken me backward. But it has taken me deeper."

Maybe this is the real paradox of time: not that we want to go back — but that sometimes, we’re at risk of going backward without realizing it.

What has changed in the present
"We no longer measure truth by its rigor, but by its virality. News has shifted from episteme to doxa — from reasoned knowledge to shared opinion, from verification to noise." And heroes "rise instead through algorithms, through likes, through curated narcissism."
The warning he reaches for
"For the time will come when people will not put up with sound doctrine. Instead, to suit their own desires, they will gather around them a great number of teachers to say what their itching ears want to hear." (2 Timothy 4:3–4, quoted in the article)
Why the question is worth asking anyway
"In a world obsessed with distraction and speed, slowing down to think carefully is rebellious. It’s a way of staying awake and human, when everything else wants us numb." "Because in remembering how to ask “why?” — we remember how to be human again."
