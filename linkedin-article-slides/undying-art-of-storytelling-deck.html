The Undying Art of Storytelling: Navigating 4,000 Years of Human Connection

The article defends storytelling against an age that measures value in data, and it makes the defence structurally rather than sentimentally. Its own argument runs on a three-beat frame, What then But then Then, and the But is the substance: analytical thinking has been elevated and narrative pushed to the periphery, information has multiplied while wisdom has not, unquantifiable knowledge has been marginalised by credential-chasing, and the decline of shared narratives produces social fragmentation rather than merely a loss of charm. Patrick Winston supplies the corrective ordering, placing speaking first, writing second and the quality of ideas third. The article then sets its own frame among nine others, from Freytag's pyramid to Kishotenketsu, which drives a story without conflict at all, and ends on thick description, learned during what the author calls a failed attempt at a doctorate in anthropology.

Introduction

The form is old enough that the sacred texts were told aloud before they were written down.

The article establishes duration before it argues value, on the basis that a form surviving four thousand years is evidence of something rather than an accident of habit.

Storytelling is described as "a fundamental part of human civilization, culture, and survival since time immemorial", working as a compass across epochs, shaping beliefs and traditions and carrying knowledge between generations.

The strongest instance offered is textual rather than anthropological. Before the enduring books were codified, "the stories that compose the were told and retold around bonfires in the desert, night after night, generation after generation." The extraction preserves the gap in that sentence as published.

What those oral traditions did is given a function rather than a mood: they "served as both spiritual guidance and cultural bedrock, capturing the essence of a people and their relationship with the divine", helping people move through moral, social and spiritual terrain.

The epigraph the article opens and closes on

Margaret Atwood, used twice, at the top and as the final line: "You're never going to kill storytelling because it's built in the human plan. We come with it." The claim is that the form is constitutional rather than cultural, which is why the article treats its decline as a symptom rather than a fashion.

Gap

Four forces are squeezing storytelling out, and only the last of them is obviously a loss.

This is the But of the article's own frame, headed The Eclipse of Storytelling, and the four causes are set out separately because they operate differently.

1  ·  The ascendancy of the analytical mind
"In an era that increasingly elevates analytical thinking and empirical data, storytelling finds itself endangered, relegated to the periphery of what society deems valuable." The trend "elevates numbers and facts but often dismisses the nuanced emotional and ethical dimensions that storytelling uniquely illuminates."
2  ·  The tyranny of information overload
"Our digital age is awash with information, but wisdom remains scarce." The internet has made people "voracious collectors of facts, fostering the illusion that hoarding data equates to understanding", which distracts from what narrative does: synthesising complex truths into relatable form.
3  ·  The commodification of knowledge
"In today's market-driven world, unquantifiable forms of knowledge, like the wisdom encapsulated in stories, are often marginalized." What replaces them are "credentials and specific skills that promise immediate economic returns".
4  ·  Social fragmentation and the loss of shared narratives
An emphasis on individual achievement and specialisation leaves fewer stories held in common, and the article is explicit that the cost is collective: the decline "can result in social fragmentation, reduced empathy, and a decline in collective well-being."
The corrective, and the order matters

Patrick Winston, quoted as the response to all four: "Your success in life will be determined largely by your ability to speak, your ability to write, and the quality of your ideas. In that order." Placing the quality of the ideas third is the provocation, and the article lets it stand without softening.

Framework

Ten frameworks exist, and the article's own is a plain version of the oldest.

What, But, Then is offered without a claim to originality: it "doesn't directly correspond to a single universally recognized storytelling model" and resembles several approaches that break a story into acts.

1  ·  The three-act family
The classic structure of Setup, Confrontation and Resolution, which the article says "could be loosely translated to “What, But, Then.”" Pixar's version runs Once upon a time, Every day, Until one day, And because of that, Until finally. The Rule of Threes is named as a simplified form of the same shape.
2  ·  The journey and the pyramid
The Hero's Journey, "developed by Joseph Campbell and later adapted by Christopher Vogler", running from Ordinary World through Call to Adventure and Trials to Return. Freytag's Pyramid, a five-part design of exposition, rising action, climax, falling action and denouement, used for classical drama and applicable more widely.
3  ·  Kishotenketsu, the one without conflict
"This is a traditional Asian narrative structure that doesn't rely on conflict to drive the story. It consists of Introduction, Development, Twist, and Conclusion." The article marks the absence of conflict as its defining characteristic, which sets it apart from every other frame on the list.
4  ·  The technique-level four
Save the Cat!, Blake Snyder's sequence of beats a compelling story should hit. In Medias Res, starting mid-action and filling in backstory later. Minimalist storytelling, which creates emotional impact "through what is left unsaid". And nonlinear narrative, which shuffles chronology for intrigue or emotional depth.

Details

Thick description is the tenth method, and the article names where it was learned.

The final section carries a disclosure most writers would omit, given in the heading itself: thick descriptive style is "What I learned last semester in my failed attempt to pursue a formal doctoral degree in Anthropology".

The method is defined by density rather than by plot. It "involves offering meticulous and elaborate descriptions to create a vivid, immersive world for the reader", and the article adds the warning that comes with it: the style "can be richly rewarding but can also become overwhelming if not done skillfully."

Its origin is attributed correctly, to "anthropologist Clifford Geertz in his ethnographic work", where thick description means "detailed accounts of cultural practices, providing context to the observed behaviors and interactions to understand their meanings within a particular culture."

And its purpose is set against mere reporting. The method aims "to convey not just the facts but the layers of meaning behind human actions", which is why the article treats it as a storytelling technique rather than only as a research method.

The ten steps it gives for writing that way

Choose the right setting and characters. Use all five senses. Show, do not tell. Focus on the details. Use strong verbs and adjectives. Use figurative language. Be mindful of pacing. Vary sentence structure. Be consistent. Revise, revise, revise. The article points to a worked example drawn from a short biography it wrote of Prof. Djumilah.

Conclusion

The form is not defended on nostalgia but on what it does that data cannot.

The Then of the article's own frame is short, and its claim is that the revival is already under way rather than that it is needed.

In a world that often prioritizes data and empirical knowledge, storytelling persists as an irreplaceable cornerstone of human culture and interaction.

— The Undying Art of Storytelling, 12 September 2023
Where the revival is happening
"Defying the modern world's over-emphasis on data and empiricism, storytelling is experiencing a renaissance in various domains like business, social media, and education, asserting its enduring importance."
And how the form adapts
Digital storytelling is treated as continuity rather than rupture: new media provide "a fresh stage, proving that this ancient art form can adapt and flourish in any era."
Denouement, defined at length in a footnote
The article footnotes the term rather than assuming it: the part of a story where "the plot strands are drawn together and matters are explained or resolved", serving "to unravel the complexities of the plot and clarify any questions or mysteries that remain" and to leave the audience with a clear sense of how and why things ended as they did.
What the article says it left out
