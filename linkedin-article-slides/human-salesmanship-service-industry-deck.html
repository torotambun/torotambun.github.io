LinkedIn Article  ·  Sales · Human Salesmanship in the Service Industry  ·  6 August 2023

The Irreplaceable Role of Human Salesmanship in the Service Industry: For Now

The article argues that sales in service industries with long sales cycles and high stakes cannot be handed to machines or third parties, because such services resist commoditisation and clients weigh trust and track record above price. It lists seven elements a seller must understand, sets out the Trusted Advisor equation of Maister et al. (2000) with a fifth term of its own, catalogues eleven tasks where robots and AI assist, and puts the human share at around 70 percent and the machine share at around 30 percent as a rough estimate. It closes on the wisdom and experience that robots, with their current capabilities, cannot replicate.

Introduction

Services with long sales cycles and high stakes cannot be commoditised, so their sales cannot be handed to a machine.

Situation

The article opens on a technological claim: "chatbots and robochats have emerged as potential contenders to replace human salesmanship". It confines its answer to service industries with long sales cycles and high stakes.

Complication

In those settings, the article says, "the human touch remains unparalleled". Services rest on specialised expertise and personalised solutions, so they resist being treated as standardised commodities. Commoditisation turns on interchangeability and price, whereas clients "prioritize factors such as quality, reliability, trust, and a track record of success over mere price considerations".

Question

Can sales in the service industry be outsourced to machines or to third-party entities?

Answer

The article says they cannot. In place of commoditising, successful providers are said to emphasise their unique value propositions and to build strong relationships with clients, which it describes as more valuable than commoditising for short-term gains.

Evidence note

The article gives no sales data, client cases or study for this claim. Its support is argument and the frameworks it cites.

Details

Seven elements underpin a service sale, from the buying center to the industry system around it.

The article says the success of sales is deeply rooted in understanding five elements, then numbers seven, and the deck follows the list.

1  ·  The buying center
The group involved in the decision inside a potential customer organisation: stakeholders, decision-makers, influencers and gatekeepers. A sales team is expected to identify each member's role and to tailor its approach.
2  ·  Internal resources
The features and capabilities of the company's own products or services, the customer support available, the ability to deliver on promises, and any limits. Knowing them lets a team offer solutions that are realistic and achievable.
3  ·  Competition
Research into competitors' strengths and weaknesses, pricing, unique selling points and market position, so that the team can show how its offering outperforms theirs.
4  ·  Suppliers and external logistics
Lead times, inventory, shipping and delivery options, and bottlenecks, so that expectations about availability and timelines are set accurately.
5  ·  Government regulation and the civil servants themselves
Industry regulations, licensing and legal frameworks. On dealing with the actors, the article says stakeholders "may have asymmetric desire of transparencies and efficiencies", then stops: "That's all I can write here."
6  ·  Technical, operational and service expertise
Technical knowledge to answer complex inquiries, operational know-how to offer practical solutions, and exceptional service that the article says "cannot be easily replicated by a novice or ambitious undergraduate".
7  ·  The industry system
The article places the seller in a larger system: stock markets, central banks, geopolitics, the nature of the industry, the interests of indigenous people, and not-for-profit organisations.

Framework

Trust rests on the four Maister qualities plus a fifth term, values held at one hundred percent.

The article takes four qualities from the Trusted Advisor equation (Maister et al., 2000), pairs each with a realm, and adds one term of its own after a period of reflection.

1  ·  Credibility, the realm of words
Expertise, industry knowledge and a proven track record. The client can trust what the seller says.
2  ·  Reliability, the realm of actions
Delivering on promises consistently: setting realistic expectations, following through on commitments and giving support through the sales life cycle.
3  ·  Intimacy, the realm of emotions
Active listening, empathy and genuine care for the client's success, so that a meeting is memorable and personal.
4  ·  Self-orientation, the realm of motives
Interest in the client's well-being ahead of the sale: the seller is there for the client's glory and not for the seller or the company. The article's numbered list calls this term the indirect function of self-interest, and its prose calls it self-orientation.
5  ·  The added term: values held at one
A function of the seller's purpose, an absolute commitment to a universal value such as fairness, justice, compassion, no corruption, no bribe and no shortcut, and to basic values beyond the law. The value must be 1, and the article cites Clayton Christensen: "It's easier to stay true to your values 100% of the time than it is to stay true 98% of the time. The moment you compromise, you're tempted to do it again."
Evidence note

The article writes the fifth term as an integral of a function derivative from 0 to infinity and gives no derivation. The equation figure, captioned as from Maister et al. (2000) and Tambun (2022), is an image that the text extraction does not carry.

Implications

AI can take eleven supporting tasks, about thirty percent by the article's own rough estimate.

The article lists eleven tasks where robots and AI assist, and puts their share of the work at around 30 percent against 70 percent for human sales professionals. It calls the split "only a rough estimation" that "may vary significantly based on the specific context".

1  ·  Lead generation
Analysing data from many sources to identify prospects and to predict customer preferences and behaviour.
2  ·  Outreach and communication
Chatbots and email automation handle first inquiries, follow-ups and appointment scheduling.
3  ·  Sales analytics
Processing large volumes of data on customer behaviour, market trends and sales performance.
4  ·  Customer relationship management
Analysing interactions, purchase history and engagement, and suggesting personalised recommendations.
5  ·  Sales forecasting
Analysing historical sales data and external factors to forecast trends.
6  ·  Pricing optimisation
Weighing demand, competition and customer preferences to set prices that are competitive and profitable.
7  ·  Virtual sales assistants
Real-time information, talking points and follow-up suggestions during client meetings.
8  ·  Performance management
Assessing each representative's strengths and weaknesses and recommending training plans.
9  ·  Chat support
Chatbots answer inquiries on websites and social media and route complex issues to human representatives.
10  ·  Personalised content
Recommending articles, videos or products from customer behaviour and preferences.
11  ·  Administrative tasks and efficiency
Managing project timelines, tracking progress and sending reminders, so that human sellers can focus on client relationships and on their technical, operational and service expertise.
Evidence note

The article gives no method for the 70 and 30 split, and its own text says the ratio depends on the industry, the organisation and the sales process.

Conclusion

The seventy percent that remains is field-earned wisdom that machines lack, for now.

The closing pages ground the human share in connection, expertise and experience, call for a balanced approach that uses the strengths of both humans and AI, and word the machine's limit as a matter of current capability.

Time and energy invested in gaining such wisdom cannot be replaced.

— The Irreplaceable Role of Human Salesmanship, 6 August 2023
Human connection
Human sales professionals, with emotional intelligence and empathy, are said to build trust and rapport, and to read body language, tone of voice and personal interactions.
Two kinds of intelligence
The article pairs fluid intelligence, quick thinking and adaptability, with crystallized intelligence, accumulated knowledge and expertise. Human sellers are said to combine them to understand client intentions, weigh cultural nuances and deal with organisational politics.
What the seventy percent covers
The human share is said to reflect the importance of human skills in building trust, providing personalised solutions and nurturing long-term relationships. The machine share covers repetitive tasks, data analysis and lead generation.
Where machines stop
The limit is worded as one of present capability. The article says robots, with their current capabilities, lack the systems needed to analyse such nuances and so cannot replicate human wisdom and experience.
