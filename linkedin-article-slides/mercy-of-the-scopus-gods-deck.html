You Never Know the Outcome: By the Mercy of the Scopus gods (small g)

Fifty-four submissions, eight published, and a fourth rejection after full peer review — this one on grounds that reverse the first editor's instructions. The deeper objection is not to being rejected but to being read through an ontology that mistakes itself for objectivity, by reviewers who never asked how the data was collected. And then, deliberately, the refusal of self-pity: this is not a tragedy, it is a privilege.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

The Count

Fifty-four submissions, eight published, and this is the fourth full-review rejection.

"“Your manuscript has been denied publication.” That's the sentence I received—again."

RECORDED A
Fifty-four manuscripts from seventeen articles
"I have submitted 54 manuscripts—each a labor of love—drawn from 17 different articles." The ratio is itself part of the account: the same underlying work, resubmitted and reshaped across venues.
RECORDED B
Twenty-seven desk rejections, nine desk transfers, three withdrawn
The majority never reached review at all. Thirty-nine of the fifty-four were resolved before a reviewer read them.
RECORDED C
Eight published, three in the pipeline
"Of these, 27 were desk rejections, 9 were desk transfers, 3 were withdrawn, 8 are published, and 3 are in the pipeline."
RECORDED D
Four rejections after full peer review
"This is my 4th rejection after undergoing full peer review." The familiar line followed: "In view of the criticisms of the reviewers found at the bottom of this letter, your manuscript has been denied publication."
What the numbers do not show

"What remains are the scars. The words that never made it. The months that collapsed into a few reviewer lines. The long hours with students. The heat. The recordings. The belief that this one would finally pass. That this one had paid its dues."

The Reversal

Every concern from the first rejection was addressed, and the second rejected it anyway.

"This version was significantly improved from the original submission. I addressed every major concern from the first editorial rejection…"

What the First Editor Asked For
•"removed the overdependence on appendices"
•"clearly reframed the study as cross-sectional (not longitudinal)"
•"repositioned the 4-Layer Model as the manuscript's central thesis"
•"narrowed the theoretical scope"
•Each point had been highlighted as critical, with a request that any revision be addressed to him
What the Second Set Said
✗"They said I used too many theories, calling it "theory melting.""
✗"They said my model was unanchored, the visuals unreadable."
✗"They said I lacked methodological transparency—even though this was the version where I trimmed the appendices because the first editor told me they overwhelmed the reader."
✗"They wanted Gioia structure. They wanted demographic tables. They wanted assurance I didn't make it up."
✗"They even questioned why the "shared assumptions" block in my diagram was too large."
The answer to the last one

"That is the answer—it is large. Because it predetermines the entire structure, it governs all other layers." The resubmission had passed to a new editor and a new set of reviewers: "It was worth full review. But still rejected."

The Absence

None of them asked how the data was collected.

"All this... from people who never asked how the data was collected."

"None of them were there."

"They didn't walk the campus. Didn't sit with students under the tropical heat. Didn't sleep three hours a night. Didn't teach by day, transcribe by night. Didn't fund this work without a grant."

"Didn't watch a student cry when they found confidence to try again. Didn't write fieldnotes after every discussion. Didn't carry the smell of open-air markets into their coding sessions. Didn't build rapport in three languages. Didn't live it."

"But they judged it."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Paradigm

A different ontology read as objectivity is still a different ontology.

"This is the part we rarely talk about: reviewer paradigms."

"When they read with a different ontology and pretend it's objectivity. When they conflate lack of quotations with lack of rigor." They demand the structures they recognise — tables, Gioia ladders, grounded terms — as if those were the only way to see.

"When a qualitative work written from within culture is interpreted through the lens of someone sitting behind a screen 10,000 kilometers away, believing they are neutral. They're not."

And the instructions do not converge. "In my earlier submission, I was told I included too many full-blown thematic analyses. So I reduced it. Now I'm told I failed to provide transparency. Perhaps next time, a third set of reviewers will read the same manuscript and tell me to go back to square one."

The line the article does not soften

Of the reviewer who could not grasp why the shared-assumptions block was large: "Yet this divine reviewer could not grasp it. They are no different from the detached critics who judge from behind the safety of their windowed screens."

The Admission

I am tired. But I am bitter.

The article names the feeling rather than performing equanimity about it.

"I'm tired. But I am bitter." The correction is the point — tiredness would be the acceptable thing to admit, and it is not the accurate one.

"I am bitter because the reviewers sit behind screens ten thousand kilometers away, unaware of the years of immersive work in tropical classrooms, the hundreds of hours invested in dialogic trust, in building this study from lived fieldwork—and they dismiss it because it lacks their preferred coding ladder."

"I am bitter because they did not come to learn, only to judge, armed with an ontology that masquerades as neutrality. I am bitter because the pain of the field was never read. Only the absence of their templates."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
The Turn

This is not a tragedy; it is a privilege.

"Still, what this long road has taught me is that there are people—right now—whose suffering makes mine look small."

I write. I fail. I eat. I sleep. I live. This is not a tragedy. This is a privilege.

— You Never Know the Outcome: By the Mercy of the Scopus gods (small g)
What the comparison actually is
"Hostages who don't get to submit a manuscript. Abductees who don't get to debate reflexivity. Victims of war who would trade a thousand reviewer comments just to have clean water, a name, a home." The turn is not a retraction of the bitterness. It is a placement of it.
The distinction the article insists on
"Some reviewers use their authority without care, issuing harsh judgment without understanding the context or the work's intent. Others provide thoughtful, constructive feedback that still holds high standards. The difference is not in what they demand, but in how they choose to treat the researcher behind the manuscript."
Who is thanked, and who is not
"I have encountered reviewers who evaluate rigorously but still respect the humanity of the process. I want to thank them. The rest—those who dismiss, diminish, or disregard the labor behind fieldwork—I have no words for, only exhaustion." "So to the reviewer gods, I offer no complaint. Just clarity."
