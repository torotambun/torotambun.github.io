When the Loop Fails Before It Begins: Culture, Feedback, and the Inhibition of Innovation Ecosystems

Innovation ecosystems do not stall for want of structure. Across twenty national systems the university is never the ignition point, and in one signed, funded, staffed partnership no pilot launched in two years — because the culture filtered out the rebel talent that the only reinforcing loop depends on. Activation is not a result of structure. It is a result of permission, and permission is a cultural variable.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

Presence is not activation, and infrastructure is not ignition.

"This is not a speech about policy recommendations. It is a system diagnostic."

Situation

"In academic institutions like ours, especially those that specialize in engineering, design, and innovation policy, we often carry the unspoken belief that if we build the right program, if we launch the right platform, if we train the right student—innovation will follow." "That belief feels safe. It is well-intentioned. It is formal, familiar, and fundable."

Complication

"But it is also wrong." "We praise experimentation but reward compliance. We celebrate innovation but reject the rebel. We assemble platforms but silence feedback." "We submit proposals. We frame partnerships. We redesign syllabi. But we rarely ask: will the loop run?"

Question

"We often mistake the presence of institutions for the presence of circulation. But presence ≠ flow." So what actually starts the motion?

Answer

"Because what activates an innovation ecosystem is not structure—it is circulation. And what governs circulation is not policy—but feedback." "And feedback is not just a technical signal. It is shaped by culture."

The Structural Loops

In none of the five structural loops is the university the initiator.

A causal loop model adapted from Passi et al. (2023), drawn from field research across twenty national innovation ecosystems — advanced, transitional and developing.

1  ·  R1 — Attractiveness (reinforcing)
An attractive ecosystem draws more firms, producing more ideas and variation, more co-development, more pilots — and successful pilots make the ecosystem more attractive still. "But notice the entry point: market attractiveness, not research quality. Universities participate in co-development, but they do not start the cycle."
2  ·  R2 — Research Investment (reinforcing)
More funding enables more joint research, producing knowledge outputs that feed co-development and pilots, which attract further funding. "Again: universities are in the middle. They amplify—but do not ignite." "The trigger is not curiosity or discovery. The trigger is finance."
3  ·  R3 — Visibility and Hype (reinforcing)
Media visibility boosts attractiveness, encourages firm entry, increases visible outcomes and fuels more hype. "This loop is not driven by evidence—it is driven by perception. And the actors who drive it are rarely academic."
4  ·  B1 — Ownership vs Openness (balancing)
Increased private capital reduces willingness to share openly, lowering co-development and pilots, which weakens the case for private investment. "This loop is a control reflex—the more capitalized the player, the less willing they are to engage in open innovation." Universities may argue for openness but are structurally subordinate to the financing entity.
5  ·  B2 — Stakeholder Expectation (balancing)
Failed pilots lower expectations, which lowers attractiveness, firm entry and further pilots, "reinforcing a mood of disillusionment". "This loop is rarely discussed, but it's the reason many ecosystems stall. It shows how one failure—if publicly mishandled—can shut down the entire engine."
The finding across all five

"In all five loops—whether reinforcing or balancing—the university is never the independent variable. It is not the one who starts the motion. It is the recipient of funding, the host of projects, the amplifier of ideas—but it is not the trigger."

The Frameworks

Six influential frameworks describe the system without diagnosing why it stalls.

"If the university is not central, then why do so many theories treat it as such?" The article answers historically, then turns the question on the theories themselves.

Triple Helix (Etzkowitz & Leydesdorff, 2000) frames innovation as triadic co-evolution with the university as knowledge originator — but it assumes equal agency, and accounts for neither power asymmetry nor cultural friction. National Systems of Innovation (Lundvall, 1992) assumes that if all actors are present, interaction will emerge; it says nothing about why those interactions so often fail to materialise.

Technology transfer theory (Bozeman, 2000) assumes transfer will occur if incentives align, and does not diagnose why innovation dies before entering the pipeline. Experiential learning (Kolb, 2015) trains students through immersion — but organisations may not be culturally ready to receive them. Innovation capability maturity (Corsi et al., 2006) benchmarks internal readiness, which is not the same as willingness to deviate. Person–environment fit (Kristof-Brown et al., 2005) explains retention, and equally explains why innovative individuals leave.

"All of these models are structurally valid. But none of them are culturally self-aware." The article does not dispute their value; it argues that structural prescriptions alone do not guarantee system motion. "Presence is not activation. Infrastructure is not ignition. Policy is not permission."

The Case

The lab had everything and launched nothing for two years.

A formal corporate–university partnership from the author's doctoral research, titled The Lab That Never Launched.

RECORDED A
The partnership was signed
"This was a real initiative", and "The partnership was signed." The agreement existed, formally executed between a corporation and a university.
RECORDED B
Leadership, roadmap and budget were in place
"The project had leadership buy-in." "The roadmap and budget were finalized." None of the usual explanations for stalled initiatives — no sponsor, no plan, no money — applies here.
RECORDED C
The facility existed and staff were appointed
"The physical facility existed." "The staff was nominally appointed." Every structural component a framework would prescribe was present and countable.
RECORDED D
No pilot was ever launched
After two years, not one. "This is not a failure of planning. It is a failure of LOOP ACTIVATION. The ecosystem had components. What it lacked were CONDITIONS THAT PERMITTED THE LOOPS TO RUN."

The Cultural Loops

The culture filtered out rebel talent, so the reinforcing loop never ignited.

Three loops from the case explain what went wrong — two that hold the system still, and one that could have moved it.

1  ·  B1 — Rebel Talent (balancing)
Short-term orientation with high uncertainty avoidance produces risk-averse shared values, which suppress a risk-taking culture and reduce the number of people willing to challenge or experiment. That raises preference for stable, low-variance jobs, attracting a conformity-driven talent pool and performance systems "optimized for delivery, not discovery". "In effect, the loop preserves cultural stability by preemptively rejecting innovation—not by accident, but by design."
2  ·  B2 — Corporate Conservatism (balancing)
Uncertainty avoidance produces a risk-averse culture, which selects risk-neutral talent, which reinforces a stable performance system that is difficult to challenge. Such systems perform well against delivery metrics — and as pressure to activate the innovation engine declines, appetite for innovation weakens. "This is a balancing loop, not because it fixes a problem—but because it prevents deviation. It sustains performance by silencing signals of change."
3  ·  R1 — Innovation Alignment (reinforcing, and the only one)
If rebel talent is integrated, internal innovation capability rises, generating pressure to activate the engine and raising appetite for innovation, which reinforces further integration. "This loop does not run by default. It remains conditional and fragile, dependent on the willingness of the system to welcome discomfort." "Without rebel integration, the loop stalls at the gate."
What the two diagrams show together

"What looks like a well-run firm is, in loop terms, a system self-protected from innovation." And of the case: "It wasn't broken. It was working exactly as built." The lab "never failed in the execution phase. It failed in the cultural conditions that precluded the system from ever launching."

Conclusion

Where permission is withheld, feedback dies and ecosystems stall.

"But structure alone does not generate circulation. No loop activates just because the design looks correct."

Ecosystems do not fail for lack of planning. They fail when the system refuses to feel what it needs to learn.

— When the Loop Fails Before It Begins
The pattern that keeps repeating
"The architecture is in place.But feedback is filtered, risk is suppressed, deviance is penalized—and still we revise the framework again." "We keep redesigning the structure. We keep assembling the same actors, funding similar initiatives, hosting the same dialogues. And yet, the outcomes remain unchanged."
Where the diagnosis actually points
"At some point, the issue is not policy or strategy. It is the REFUSAL TO CHANGE THE CONDITIONS UNDER WHICH THE SYSTEM LEARNS." "And if feedback is still blocked, if culture is still filtering the necessary friction, then we are not innovating." "We are rehearsing failure."
The question the speech ends on
"So the question is not: What will we build next?" It is: "WHAT ARE WE STILL REFUSING TO HEAR?" — and until that is addressed "not by documents, but by institutional behavior— no ecosystem, no matter how well designed, will run."
