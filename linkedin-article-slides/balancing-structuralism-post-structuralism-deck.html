LinkedIn Article  ·  Grand Theory Thread · The Capstone, and a Named Objection  ·  27 September 2023

Balancing Structuralism & Post Structuralism: Result of an Open Research

This is the closing article of a seven-piece working day, and what makes it research rather than reading is a named person disagreeing. Professor PM Laksono of Universitas Gadjah Mada criticised the author's linking of Levi-Strauss to Schein, on the ground that structuralism carries a determinism and finality that Schein's model does not share. The article accepts the criticism as insightful and then answers it: Schein's deep assumptions evolve when confronted with challenges, so the two are not the same kind of structure. It then turns the objection into a method. Post-structuralism does not replace Schein's shared values but interrogates them, asking how they were constructed, whose voices dominated and whose were silenced. The conclusion is not to choose a lens but to scope each one explicitly, which is what an open research produces when it is conducted in public.

Introduction

A question about national trajectory turns into a question about which theory is allowed to answer it.

Situation

The standing question is the one the whole thread serves. "In answering my query on the trajectory from a “lazy native” to an “innovative nation,” one could argue that it's the culmination of these anthropological evolutions." Culture is treated as mobile: "culture is not static; it is an evolving entity, shaped by countless forces and moments of inflection, both subtle and monumental."

Complication

The case the author actually faces is a mismatch between expectation and condition. "I am facing a case where a nation without traits of innovation is expected to be an innovative nation (by Western's definition)." Japan, Norway and Israel succeeded, and the question is whether shared values built across generations explain it.

Question

"If I borrow this stand, will I be a structuralist? or more like post-structuralist or simply Geert's stand?"

Answer

None of them alone. The position turns out to draw on structuralism for the attention to deep values, post-structuralism for the refusal of an imported standard, Geertz for the insistence on national particularity, and Hofstede for the link between values and outcomes.

Framework

Eight inflection points move a national culture, and none of them is a programme a ministry can run.

Before the theory, the article lists what actually shifts a culture, drawn from the corpus it has been analysing.

1  ·  Technology and globalisation
The printing press for knowledge dissemination, the internet for communication. Interconnection through trade, travel and communication produces both homogenisation, "where global norms become standardized", and hybridisation, "where unique cultural blends emerge".
2  ·  Visitors, and deliberate exchange
"Travelers, explorers, missionaries, and colonizers have historically played significant roles in introducing new beliefs, practices, and institutions to indigenous cultures", producing assimilation, resistance, or both. Student exchange and cultural festivals are the deliberate version of the same mechanism.
3  ·  Economic and political change
Booms, recessions and the shift from agrarian to industrial economies alter values, family structures and social norms. Revolutions, regime changes and the propagation of ideologies carry cultural consequences of their own.
4  ·  Disaster, and intellectual movement
"Events like earthquakes, tsunamis, or pandemics can lead to cultural shifts as societies re-evaluate their values, beliefs, and practices in the wake of significant upheaval." And philosophical movements from the Renaissance to postmodernism, which the article treats as inflection points in their own right.
The author's own metaphor, and the risk it names

On "jumping too much or too high": "Rapid or forced cultural change can lead to a sense of dislocation, loss, or identity crisis." Bypassing stages risks "a veneer of change without the deep-rooted transformation that genuine cultural evolution entails", leaving adopted elements "in constant tension with deeply held values and beliefs."

The Objection

A professor of anthropology objected that structuralism is deterministic where Schein is not, and the objection was accepted.

This is the event the subtitle refers to, and it is the only place in the seven articles where a named scholar pushes back in person. "PM Laksono Ph.D (Cornell University, a prominent Anthropologist Professor from Universitas Gadjah Mada (UGM) criticized my notes of Lévi-Strauss and structuralism concerning Schein. I found his input is insightful."

The substance of the criticism is precise. Levi-Strauss's structuralism "often tends to be deterministic, suggesting that human thought processes are governed by universal structures", which lends those structures an air of finality and stasis.

The answer the article gives does not dispute the characterisation of Levi-Strauss. It distinguishes the two models instead: Schein's "acknowledges the dynamic nature of organizational culture, focused on change, evolution, and the interplay between different levels of culture", and his deep assumptions "can evolve, especially when confronted with challenges or anomalies".

That distinction is the same one the day's opening article reached independently, twelve hours earlier. The difference is that here it arrives as a response to criticism rather than as an assertion, which is what converts it from a claim into a finding.

Why this belongs in the record

The article's subtitle calls it the result of an open research. The open part is that a working hypothesis was published, a specialist objected to it by name, and the objection and the response both appear in the same text rather than the hypothesis being quietly revised.

Gap

Post-structuralism does not replace Schein's shared values; it asks who made them shared.

The article argues that a purely structuralist reading of shared values would flatten exactly what matters about them, and sets out the questions the other lens supplies.

What a structuralist reading gives
•Universal patterns identified beneath observable behaviour
•Organisations treated as sharing similar foundational cultural structures
•Deep assumptions described as inherent and largely unconscious
•Comparability across contexts, since the structures are held in common
•Limited purchase on unique histories or contexts
What a post-structuralist reading adds
✓How have these shared values been constructed?
✓Whose voices were dominant in this construction, and whose were marginalised?
✓How do power dynamics decide which values are promoted as shared?
✓How are these values reinforced through practices and discourses?
✓Values as contingent and changeable rather than benign and agreed
The conclusion the article draws from the pairing

"Using post-structuralism to explore Schein's “shared values” pushes us to recognize that these values aren't just benign elements everyone agrees upon." What follows is the licence to "question, challenge, and deconstruct taken-for-granted assumptions, leading to richer insights and more inclusive organizational practices."

Implications

Keeping both lenses is a scoping decision, not a failure to choose.

The article gives four reasons for retaining structuralism alongside post-structuralism, and one condition attached to doing so.

1  ·  Comprehensive analysis
Structuralism identifies the universal patterns; post-structuralism interrogates their origins, power dynamics and nuances. The article treats these as complementary operations on the same object rather than as competing accounts of it.
2  ·  Historical context
"Structuralism played a significant role in the development of anthropology and cultural studies." Retaining it acknowledges its influence on what followed, post-structuralism included, which cannot be read without it.
3  ·  Different questions need different theories
Identifying universal patterns within organisational cultures is one question, and understanding how those patterns were constructed and maintained is another. The choice of lens follows the question rather than preceding it.
4  ·  The condition attached
"While it's feasible to include both theories in your framework, it's essential to clearly define the scope and purpose of each within your research." Consistency and coherence are required, so this is a discipline rather than a licence to mix freely.

Details

Six scholars, one shared object: what each means by an underlying assumption.

The article's base-camp section lines up Hofstede, Levi-Strauss, Geertz, Schein, Foucault and Derrida on the same four questions. All six address underlying assumptions, and no two mean the same thing by them.

01
Measured, and interpreted
Hofstede and Geertz
Hofstede works by quantitative cross-cultural analysis from corporate surveys, assuming "National cultures can be defined and differentiated based on measurable dimensions of values and beliefs". Geertz works by thick description, assuming the opposite: that understanding a culture requires going deep into its specific contexts "rather than seeking universal structures".
02
Universal, and layered
Levi-Strauss and Schein
Levi-Strauss analyses myth, kinship and language for deep structures of the mind, assuming "All human societies share universal cognitive structures". Schein dissects organisations into artifacts, espoused values and basic assumptions, and his application is practical: helping leaders and consultants understand and shape organizational culture.
03
Power, and language
Foucault and Derrida
Foucault analyses discourse, assuming "There's no single underlying truth or objective reality" and that knowledge is constructed through discourse under power relations. Derrida deconstructs texts, assuming "Meanings are not fixed or stable; they are contingent, constructed, and always in flux."
What the line-up establishes

"While all six scholars touch on “underlying assumptions” in some way, they do so with different methodologies, foci, and applications". The shared vocabulary across the six is what made the conflation possible in the first place, and naming the differences is what dissolves it.

Conclusion

The result of the open research is a scoping rule, not a verdict on which theory wins.

The closing position is modest in form and consequential in effect, because it is what the dissertation's theoretical framework has to stand on.

it's not about choosing one theory over the other but about leveraging the strengths of each to enhance the depth and breadth of your analysis. Including both can enrich your research, provided they're applied thoughtfully and purposefully.

— Balancing Structuralism & Post Structuralism, 27 September 2023
What the day produced
Seven articles in fourteen hours, from the opening defence of Schein at 04:23 to this piece created at 17:40. The sequence runs from a single theorist outward to two disciplines and back to a rule for using both.
What the criticism changed
Professor Laksono's objection is the pivot. Before it, the thread is assembling a framework. After it, the thread is defending one against a specialist, which is a different and more demanding activity.
The position, in four lines
Deep-seated values carry shades of structuralism; questioning Western narratives resonates with post-structuralism; the focus on unique national trajectories aligns with Geertz; and recognising cultural values shaping outcomes has parallels with Hofstede.
What remains open
The article does not say which lens governs which chapter, only that the scope of each must be declared. The allocation itself is left to the dissertation.
