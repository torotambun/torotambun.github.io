Reflecting on the Books That Shaped My Intellectual Journey Prior to entering Nuclear Engineering - An autoethnography attempt

Asked which three books he would go back and read, the answer turned out not to be a list. Each book arrived through a particular encounter — a state library, a competition, a second-hand complex in Pasar Senen — and each was bought again decades later. The route the book took is as much of the education as its contents.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

Students at the Dies Natalis asked which three books I would go back and read.

The 47th Dies Natalis of the Nuclear Engineering and Engineering Physics Department at Universitas Gadjah Mada, where I was one of the speakers.

A handful of students approached with the same question, put by Rajendra Salhudar: "If you could go back to your undergraduate years, what three books would you have read that had a significant impact on your life?"

The question sent me on a reflection that was not really about the books. It was about the broader context in which I met them, and how that context shaped who I became.

Background

My father's answer to money was books, and I had read hundreds before university.

Jakarta, good schools, and a household philosophy that made the rest possible.

I attended SD PSKD for my elementary years and SMA Negeri 8 Jakarta, where everyone — if not all — seemed to be a bit of a nerd. That environment, together with my father's belief in the power of knowledge, shaped my early intellectual development. He often said: "I don't provide you money, but I provide you knowledge through books." It became the cornerstone of my upbringing.

Before university I had read hundreds of books. I was the kind of nerd who spent hours in libraries, borrowing and reading wherever it was possible — while queuing, sitting or standing on buses and trains, listening to TV news, eating, and even during boring high school classes. The habit was not a pastime. It was how I made sense of the world.

The Three

Each book arrived through an encounter I can still date.

Einstein, Newton and Kagawa — and where each of them was standing when I found it.

1  ·  Out of My Later Years — Albert Einstein
Borrowed as a young student from Perpustakaan Pusat LIPI, eager to reach the thoughts of one of the greatest minds in history. What those pages gave me was not science but the ethical and philosophical implications of scientific progress — the broader responsibilities that come with knowledge, and how science fits the moral landscape of a society. Years later, stationed in Perth by Schlumberger, I found a copy and finally added it to my own library.
2  ·  Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica — Isaac Newton
Prof. Andi Hakim Nasution mentioned it during the 1987 LIPI-TVRI National Youth Scientist Research Competition, and a door opened onto a new dimension of the natural world. It was not until my AFS exchange at Tachikawa Senior High School in 1988 that I found a copy in a second-hand bookstore in Kanda, Tokyo. Holding it felt like holding a piece of history — and, for a student far from home, a reaffirmation that curiosity travels across cultures and borders.
3  ·  Kagawa — William Axling
Found in 1987 during one of many visits to the second-hand book complexes of Pasar Senen and Kwitang, first in Indonesian translation. Toyohiko Kagawa's life — compassion joined to practical action — resonated with a growing interest in social justice and human dignity, and shaped the interpretivist orientation I later developed. It was not about understanding; it was about doing. In 2019 I acquired the 1932 original from an auction site.

What I Carry

The story of how a book reached you is as much of the education as the book.

Three books, set against hundreds read before university and tens of thousands of sources since.

The stories of how I encountered each book remind me that the journey of learning and growth is often just as important as the knowledge gained.

— Reflecting on the Books That Shaped My Intellectual Journey, 1 September 2024
What the three have in common
None was assigned. Each was borrowed, stumbled upon, or hunted down in a second-hand market, and each was bought again decades later — a physical manifestation of a journey that had begun much earlier.
What they supplied
Not a mindset alone but the tools to make sense of the world in a meaningful way — the ethical weight of knowledge from Einstein, the foundations of inquiry from Newton, and from Kagawa the demand that understanding turn into action.
Why the answer is not a reading list
The question asked what to read. The reflection returns something else: that reading is a lifelong journey of growth rather than an academic exercise, and that the encounter is part of what the book did.
