LinkedIn Article  ·  Strategy · Creating Shared Value in a Disaster  ·  25 April 2020

Applying the "Creating Shared Value (CSV)" Concept During a Pandemic or Natural Disaster.

The article argues that donations made to help during a pandemic or natural disaster can create new human-made disasters, harming recipients, communities and the small businesses whose goods free supplies displace. Drawing on two Harvard Business School courses, it sets out seven modes of corporate response, from cautious charity in order of nearness to operational expertise, partnerships with universities, corporations and small suppliers, corporate venture investment and spin-outs. It closes on a disaster manager's reply, printed in full, which agrees that excess aid damages local economies, blames where aid enters and a faith-based habit of giving, and calls for three-way partnership and a professionalised aid sector.

Introduction

Well-meant donations in a pandemic can create new human-made disasters, so companies need a different way to help.

Situation

People are trying their best to help others during the pandemic. The article says past events show that this noble intention has ended up prolonging, or even creating, new forms of human-made disaster that did not originate in the pandemic or natural disaster itself.

Complication

Too much donation, the article says, will paralyse the individual in need, the recipient community and the existing small and medium enterprises whose products and services are outnumbered by free supplies flooding the market. It adds, without prejudice, that audits of how funds were distributed probably produced many disappointing findings on conformity and accountability.

Question

How can a company help during a pandemic or natural disaster without creating new harm?

Answer

The article offers seven values from two Harvard Business School courses, "Developing a CSR Strategy" taught by Professor Kasturi Rangan and colleagues and "Creating Shared Value" taught by Professor Michael Porter, Mark Kramer and colleagues. They run from cautious charity to partnerships and spin-outs, and the article closes with a disaster manager's feedback.

Evidence note

The article cites no study or audit for the harm that donations cause.

Details

A company should help through its own operations and partners, and give charity only cautiously and in order of nearness.

The article numbers seven modes of response. Its eighth point carries the disaster manager's feedback, taken up later in this deck.

1  ·  Cautiously resurrect the philanthropic theatre
Time-critical needs must be attended to. Companies should serve the bottom of Maslow's pyramid first, for their employees, then their immediate value chain, then the neighbourhood, starting with short-term needs such as those of senior citizens and small businesses. A charity foundation might offer grants to help their children pay for what is probably a more expensive online education platform.
2  ·  Focus on the operational theatre
Use the company's strategic intents, its core and functional expertise. The example is 3M, creator of the N95, which used surge capacity built nearly 20 years earlier, during the SARS outbreak, to double its N95 output.
3  ·  Partner creatively with a university
The product developed need not relate to the company's core product. The example is a team from UCL and the Formula One engine maker Mercedes-AMG HPP making a CPAP breathing device.
4  ·  Partner creatively with another corporation
The example is 3M and Ford raising production of 3M's powered air-purifying respirators, built from off-the-shelf components of Ford's F-150 truck.
5  ·  Assign the corporate venture arm to outside start-ups
Start-ups that rely on their agility to serve the most marginalised may need capital to meet confirmed pains of captive paying customers. A corporation has leverage and access to datasets or IP from partners such as health organisations, to see where the pandemic is growing and which neighbourhoods need the most support.
6  ·  Commit to buy from small and medium enterprises
Invest in strategic partnerships that commit to buy and absorb SME products in line with the company's core business, to meet spike demand or to distribute necessities through existing channels. The scenario, labelled hypothetical, is a textile company such as Sritex ordering extra non-medical masks or medical workers' PPE from many tailors or fashion SME centres.
7  ·  Spin out new companies
Spin out and spin off the required numbers of new companies from the R&D pipeline or from excess capacity, whose products and services are deemed most needed during the pandemic.

Evidence

Cited 2020 cases show the modes at work: 3M doubled N95 output, and a university and Mercedes built a breathing aid in under ten days.

The article takes three cases from reporting by FSG (Iyer, 2020) and the World Economic Forum (Elwell, 2020).

CITED A
3M doubles N95 production (mode 2)
3M used surge capacity built nearly 20 years earlier to double its N95 output. Of the 35 million respirators it was making per month in the U.S., more than 90 percent were designated for health care workers, as the article cites Iyer (2020).
CITED B
UCL and Mercedes-AMG HPP build a CPAP device (mode 3)
The device can support patients with severe breathing problems and free up ventilators for the most critically ill. As the article cites Elwell (2020), the team reached full-scale production in under 10 days, Mercedes was to make 1,000 units a day at Brixworth, and the designs were released free and shared with more than 1,300 teams in 25 countries within a week.
CITED C
3M and Ford make respirators (mode 4)
The two firms partnered to raise production of powered air-purifying respirators, using components from the F-150's cooled seating with 3M's existing HEPA filters. As the article cites Iyer (2020), they could offer advantages over N95s, being battery-powered and filtering airborne virus particles for up to eight hours on one swappable battery pack.
Evidence note

The article prints the Elwell passage with an opening quotation mark that is never closed. The figures are the cited authors' and have not been checked here.

Gap

A disaster specialist of more than twenty years agrees that excess aid damages local economies, and blames where aid enters and a faith-based habit of giving.

The article prints a personal WhatsApp message from Dave Hodgkin as point 8. He says he has worked in disasters for more than 20 years, agrees with many of the points and disagrees with a few, and does not say which.

01
Where aid enters
The false point of injection
He agrees that excess donated aid can damage a local economy, mostly because of a false point of injection and not the aid itself. The commonest is handing a family a physical product, as when agency rice or instant noodles put the local rice shop out of business. Cash aid fails when households buy the poor goods on offer or spend on the aid industry's priorities.
02
How aid is given
The faith-based aid paradigm
He traces the habit to a faith-based paradigm in which aid is a photo opportunity and visibility comes first. In its Christian form givers want to be seen as good Samaritans and actors answer to each other. In its Islamic form giving is for Allah and actors answer to Allah. Both expect 80 to 90 percent of donations, if not all, to reach recipients directly.
03
Who does the work
An unprofessional aid sector
He says the paradigm keeps the industry from professionalism. People can volunteer only so long before they burn out or quit for a paid job, young enthusiastic volunteers with little experience are in endless supply, and seasoned professionals are scarce.
Evidence note

The message is personal and gives no data. It does not say which points of the article it disputes, and this deck does not reproduce its remarks on the health and finances of individual aid workers.

Implications

Smarter interventions work through existing markets and three-way partnerships, and they require a professionalised aid sector.

The message says a small, well-embedded team of trained disaster specialists could perhaps stop the mad rush to sew a million face masks, and design interventions that form lasting partnerships, are net positive and speed recovery.

1  ·  Hotels as quarantine sites
Government and non-government actors partner with the hospitality industry to use empty hotels and guesthouses for self-isolation and quarantine, in place of schools that were not built for it and may raise the risk of transmission.
2  ·  Restaurants cooking meals
Instead of giving rice, government funds flow to restaurants to cook meals for people in need, who buy them at a much reduced price with their own money or, for the most vulnerable, with help from non-government actors.
3  ·  Cyclone tie-down straps
Government halves the price of the straps, the private sector markets the idea and explains their use, hardware shop staff are trained by non-government actors to advise, and NGO vouchers discount labour and materials for the most vulnerable, such as the newly widowed.
4  ·  Earthquake assessment and building materials
Phone-based self-assessments designed with telecom companies let households report damage by SMS within hours or days, for private engineers to assess remotely. Government works with suppliers to make 10mm deformed steel bar cheaper than 6mm plain bar, NGOs help enforce the existing building code, and funds go to contractors to take on apprentices.
5  ·  Professionalise the aid sector
The sector needs permanent staff with ongoing professional development and long-term relationships with government, not short-term volunteers in a boom-and-bust cycle. It needs better engagement among the private, public and third sectors, and a lower share of funding going directly to beneficiaries, with a much higher share going to cleverer, hands-off tripartite interventions.
Evidence note

The message frames these as things that could perhaps work and reports no results from them.

Conclusion

Aid cannot rest on capitalism, on faith-based giving or on state direction alone, so it needs partnership among all three sectors.

The message closes on what aid cannot be based on, and on what it needs instead.

01
Not capitalism alone
Profit and greed
Aid cannot be based purely on capitalism, because capitalism is based too largely on a paradigm of profit and greed.
02
Not goodness of heart alone
Faith-based giving
It can no longer rest so heavily on giving and receiving out of goodness of heart, which the message says creates aid dependency, reduces professionalism and slows recovery.
03
Not the state alone
Predefined answers
It cannot rest solely on a nanny state ordaining predefined answers for all citizens, with military interventions and dogmatism. The message asks for a more professional, more nuanced aid and much bigger discussions and partnership among the three sectors.
