Tithing Through the Years: A Reflexive and Theoretical Journey of Faith, Culture, and Neuroscience

An act of unprompted generosity among students triggers a reflection on decades of tithing practice, and four theoretical frameworks plus the neuroscience of giving explain why the practice persists even under financial strain, even as the piece closes on tithing's own ethical risk when misused by leaders.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Two team members bought a colleague new shoes the night before demo day, without being asked.

Without anyone asking, two senior team members stepped up when a colleague's shoes, sandals and pants mysteriously disappeared.

They didn't hesitate or wait for instructions — they just saw what needed to be done and did it. I didn't even know about their kind gesture until later, and when I found out, I couldn't hold back the tears. Not out of sadness, but out of pure joy and pride in seeing the "home" we've been building together — where acts of kindness come naturally, even when we don't have much to give ourselves.

What made this act of giving even more meaningful was that it happened during a time when none of them had much to give. While I may not have the backing of venture capitalists or external funding, what I do have is a community — a "home" where people step up for one another, even when we ourselves are struggling.

Gap

This moment recalled a lesson learned in elementary school: tithing.

Rooted in Malachi 3:10, tithing was taught as routine — but its meaning evolved through decades of financial hurdles and cultural context.

"The true test of one's financial character is the will and act of tithing (read: giving)" my father would say. But over time, my understanding of tithing evolved. During a particularly tough financial period, I began questioning the practice of giving when money was tight — conventional wisdom suggested saving over giving.

Yet the promise from Malachi, "Test me in this," alongside New Testament teachings, echoed in my mind. Despite my doubts, I tithed — even when it didn't seem logical. This wasn't just an act of blind faith; it was part of a larger cultural and theological framework that went beyond financial calculations, fostering both spiritual and communal connection.

Framework

Four theoretical frameworks explain why tithing persists even under financial strain.

Each supplies a different lens on the same behavior.

THEORY A
Social Capital Theory (Bourdieu, 1986)
Networks, norms and social trust facilitate cooperation for mutual benefit; tithing functions as a form of social capital, investing in a broader system of trust and reciprocity within the religious community.
THEORY B
Social Exchange Theory (Homans, 1958)
Human relationships form through cost-benefit analysis; people give based on the perceived benefits, rewards, or social approval they might receive in return.
THEORY C
Self-Determination Theory (Deci & Ryan, 1985)
Human behavior is motivated by autonomy, competence and relatedness. Tithing aligns with the core need for autonomy — the choice to give is mine — and fulfills competence and relatedness through community connection.
THEORY D
Moral Foundations Theory (Haidt & Graham)
Human morality is built on foundations including care, fairness, loyalty, authority and sanctity. Redirecting tithes to a foundation supporting precarious students resonates with the care and fairness foundations directly.

Details

The neuroscience of giving supplies a biological mechanism beneath the theology.

Acts of giving activate the brain's reward systems, particularly dopamine, reinforcing the pleasure associated with helping others.

The biological release of dopamine, oxytocin, and serotonin during acts of generosity provides a neurological explanation for why giving is fulfilling, reinforcing the importance of autonomy and relatedness as key motivators in giving. The oxytocin released during acts of generosity fosters feelings of connection, both with those who receive and with the broader community — a biological reminder that giving strengthens social bonds (Oppenheimer & Olivola, 2011).

Endorphins and serotonin also play a significant role, particularly when giving involves sacrifice or personal cost — the body's natural response to difficult but meaningful actions is to release endorphins, which reduce the discomfort associated with the sacrifice, while serotonin provides a sense of long-term satisfaction and control.

Implications

A shift from church tithing to funding precarious students resolved a cognitive dissonance the author names directly.

Festinger's Cognitive Dissonance Theory explains the discomfort between automatic giving and a widening gap between rich and poor.

I started to feel a growing dissonance between the automatic act of tithing to a church and seeing the gap between the rich and the poor keeps widening despite the glamour of the institution, and my deeper understanding of what tithing should accomplish. The decision to shift my giving toward the foundation resolved this dissonance, allowing my actions to align more closely with my ethical and spiritual values.

This shift aligns with the broader concept of Tzedakah (charity) in Jewish law, which is not just about giving but about ensuring that the giving fulfills a moral obligation to care for others — inspired by Rabbi Joseph Soloveitchik's emphasis on the ethical dimensions of giving.

Conclusion

Misused by leaders for personal gain, tithing inverts its own purpose entirely.

"giving should not be done reluctantly or under compulsion but should be a voluntary and cheerful act" (2 Corinthians 9:7).

In some religious communities, tithing has been misused by leaders for personal gain, exacerbating the gap between the rich and poor — particularly concerning when leaders encourage tithing but fail to address the needs of the poor. Deuteronomy 14:28-29 was clear that tithes were to support not just religious leaders but also the most vulnerable members of society; the misuse of tithing distorts this principle and erodes trust within religious communities.

In this autoethnographic reflection, Social Capital Theory, Social Exchange Theory, Self-Determination Theory and Moral Foundations Theory together explain how tithing functions as community-building, reciprocity, intrinsic motivation and moral obligation all at once — with ethical leadership and transparency essential to ensuring tithing remains true to its original intent.
