Examining the Phrase 'Rakyat Jelata': through McLuhan’s and Derrida’s Framework

A cabinet spokesperson's apology for the phrase "rakyat jelata" is not a lesson about a bad word choice — read through McLuhan and Derrida, it shows that meaning is never fixed in a term itself, only in who is authorized to say it, so the ethical burden falls on the speaker's office, not the dictionary.

Introduction

A spokesperson's apology for 'rakyat jelata' turns a dictionary defense into the case study.

The controversy is small and recent, but it hands the piece a live test of who actually controls what a word means.

A high-ranking spokesperson for the Presidential Communication Office of Indonesia faced public backlash for using the term rakyat jelata to describe citizens. The term literally translates to "common people," but its perceived connotations of hierarchy and marginalization sparked the criticism.

The spokesperson's reference to the dictionary to justify the use of the term revealed significant room for improvement in communication skills, as well as in demonstrating compassion and accountability. The defense assumed the word carries one fixed, literal meaning that a dictionary entry can settle. Nothing that follows accepts that assumption.

Framework

McLuhan's medium is the message: the office amplifies the word before its content is even weighed.

Marshall McLuhan's dictum offers the first lens.

McLuhan emphasized that the medium through which a message is delivered shapes its impact, often more than the content itself. The spokesperson's role as a high-ranking official, and the public nature of the statement, served as the medium that amplified the perception of the words.

"Regardless of intent, the term rakyat jelata—spoken by someone in authority—was interpreted as reinforcing societal hierarchies. This interplay between medium and message set the stage for public backlash." The word did not have to be novel or malicious. The office speaking it was enough.

Details

Derrida's eight tools agree on one point: meaning is deferred, historically loaded, and set by power, not the dictionary.

Applied one at a time, Derrida's apparatus dismantles the spokesperson's own defense rather than excusing it.

1  ·  Deconstruction of meaning
"Words do not carry a singular, objective meaning; rather, they acquire significance through their interaction with other words and the circumstances of their use" (Derrida, 1978). Rakyat jelata may read as neutral in isolation but gains complex connotations in a specific socio-political context.
2  ·  Binary oppositions and hierarchies
Derrida's critique of pairs like elite/common reveals how such structures embed power. Words like rakyat jelata might be interpreted as positioning the speaker above the addressed group, even if unintended.
3  ·  Iterability and the trace
"Trace refers to the lingering effects of past uses of a term, which shape its current understanding" (Derrida, 1982). Rakyat jelata evokes class hierarchies and marginalization by historical and cultural memory, independent of the speaker's intent.
4  ·  Challenging the accusation of defamation
Because the term's meaning depends on who interprets it and how, the defamation claim could be reframed as a misreading rather than a clear-cut offense — an argument for humility, not for the dictionary defense the spokesperson actually used.
5  ·  Power and positionality
"A speaker's authority amplifies the potential impact of their words" (Derrida, 1998). A government official using rakyat jelata may inadvertently emphasize societal divisions, regardless of intent.
6  ·  The role of the audience
Meaning is co-constructed. "Meaning arises from interaction, and gaps (aporia) in understanding are inevitable due to differing contexts and interpretations" (Caputo, 1997).
7  ·  The ethics of language
"Ethical communication involves minimizing harm and avoiding language that perpetuates stereotypes or alienates individuals" (Critchley, 1992) — speakers are responsible for anticipating how their words might land.
8  ·  Differance
Meaning is constantly deferred, never fully settled, as words derive meaning through their relational network, which is what creates room for reinterpretation and dialogue rather than a single verdict.

Implications

Five disciplines follow from the same admission that meaning is never fully in the speaker's control.

The piece turns Derrida's diagnosis into a working practice for teachers, speakers and preachers.

1
Language sensitivity
Assess a term's connotations within the relevant cultural and social context before using it — a preacher addressing diverse congregations should avoid language that might unintentionally alienate certain groups.
2
Feedback mechanisms
Build two-way interaction: teachers can use classroom discussion to clarify ambiguous terms, and preachers and speakers can create opportunities for audience engagement to address misunderstandings.
3
Power awareness
Recognize that authority shapes reception. High-ranking officials or educators should strive for humility and inclusivity to counterbalance hierarchical perceptions.
4
Critical reflection
Regularly reflect on language choices to keep communication aligned with ethical principles — using collective pronouns like "we" instead of "you" can foster a sense of unity.
5
Flexibility and adaptation
Misinterpretation is inevitable. Communicators must remain open to clarifying or adapting their language, demonstrating a commitment to mutual understanding.

Conclusion

Weigh words carefully — the reminder is a discipline, not a verdict on the spokesperson.

The piece closes on two scriptural cautions rather than a ruling on the controversy.

Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger

— James 1:19
A second caution
"In the multitude of words, sin is not lacking" (Proverbs 10:19). By integrating these spiritual insights with Derrida and McLuhan, the argument mitigates the temptation to treat a dictionary definition as the last word on what a phrase means.
