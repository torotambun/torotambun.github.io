Stop 5: Research Methodology

The fifth instalment is where the philosophy of the previous stops becomes a set of decisions, and its opening move is to separate two things researchers routinely merge. A research paradigm is practical, a framework for conducting studies; an epistemological position is philosophical, a stance on knowledge and reality. Confusing them produces methods chosen by habit and then justified afterwards. From there the chain is explicit: methodology is the framework, design is the blueprint, methods are the tools in the box, and a paradigm chosen at the top propagates all the way down to how the research question is framed. Mixed methods turns out to be eleven distinct designs rather than one, from concurrent triangulation to fully integrated. Descriptive research describes what exists without touching it and therefore cannot establish causality. And ethics closes the instalment not as a compliance checklist but as a set of philosophical principles: autonomy, respect for persons, non-maleficence and justice.

Introduction

Research paradigm and epistemological position are not the same thing, and the difference decides what gets justified when.

The instalment opens on a confusion rather than a definition, which tells the reader what it thinks the common failure is. "Research Paradigm are often confused with Epistemological Positions/ Perspective. While there are similarities between the two, they are not the same."

The distinction it draws is one of function. "Research paradigms are often more practical and applied, providing researchers with a framework to conduct their studies, whereas epistemological perspectives delve into broader philosophical questions about knowledge and reality."

Methodology itself is defined as procedure rather than philosophy: "the specific procedures or techniques used to identify, select, process, and analyze information", serving as the overall framework for the research.

And it is distinguished from the scientific method of the previous stop by flexibility and by scope: "Unlike the more rigid structure of the scientific method, research methodology allows for a wider range of approaches and techniques, tailored to fit the research question." It can also take in non-empirical approaches such as theoretical analysis, literature reviews and philosophical argument.

The five elements a paradigm commits a researcher to

Ontological beliefs about whether the subject matter is objective and external or socially constructed. Epistemological beliefs about what counts as valid knowledge. Methodological preferences. The human-nature relationship, meaning how the researcher views the interaction between people and what is studied. And ethical considerations, covering treatment of subjects, data integrity and disclosure.

Framework

Seven paradigms sit at the top of the chain, and the choice reaches all the way down to the question.

The article notes its own awkwardness here, heading the section as types of research paradigm and then observing that the list "looks like Epistemological Perspective", which is the confusion it opened by naming.

1  ·  Positivism and post-positivism
Positivism "Focuses on empirical, observable data and the use of statistical or computational analysis. It seeks objectivity and generalizable results", with clinical trials as its example. Post-positivism "accepts the existence of an external reality but emphasizes that our understanding is always influenced by our perspectives", stressing reflexivity and triangulation.
2  ·  Interpretivism and constructivism
Interpretivism "contends that reality is not objective but is socially constructed and understood through human experiences and interpretations", with anthropological study of cultural practice as its case, and phenomenology, hermeneutics and symbolic interactionism as parallel frameworks. Constructivism "Focuses on how knowledge is socially constructed".
3  ·  Critical theory and critical realism
Critical theory "Aims to understand, expose, and change social inequalities", exemplified by research on gender wage gaps that analyses the systemic causes as well as documenting the disparity. Critical realism "argues that there is an objective reality, but our understanding of it is always mediated by human interpretation and social context".
4  ·  Pragmatism
"Focuses on solving practical problems and may employ a mix of methods depending on what is most suitable for the research question." Environmental science is the example, combining field observation with laboratory experiment and theoretical modelling on a problem like climate change.
Why the choice is not a formality

"Your choice of research paradigm will shape your research design, including your choice of research methods, data collection and analysis techniques, and even how you frame your research questions." The framing of the question is downstream of the paradigm, which is why it cannot be settled afterwards.

Details

Methodology is the framework, design is the blueprint, methods are the tools in the box.

The instalment's clearest contribution is a set of metaphors that keep three levels apart, and it is worth having because the three are used interchangeably in most writing about research.

Design is the plan rather than the doing: "the overall strategy or blueprint for how to conduct a research study", covering what data to collect, whether the study is longitudinal or cross-sectional, experimental or not.

The building metaphor carries the relationship. Design "lays out the plan for how to proceed, what kinds of materials (data) are needed, and how to put them together (analysis) to create the final structure (findings). Empirical research is like gathering and using those materials as specified in the blueprint."

And the toolbox metaphor separates design from method: "Think of the approaches/designs as a toolbox, and the specific methods as the individual tools in that box. For example, qualitative research (the toolbox) might use interviews, document analysis, and participant observation (the tools) to collect data."

The flexibility the separation buys

"The approaches/designs often dictate which methods are appropriate, but there's flexibility. For instance, a longitudinal study could be conducted using either qualitative or quantitative methods or both (mixed methods)." Keeping the levels distinct is what makes that flexibility visible rather than accidental.

Evidence

Mixed methods is eleven designs, not one, and they differ in what the second method is for.

The article enumerates the ways quantitative and qualitative work can be combined, and the distinctions turn on sequence and on which strand leads.

1  ·  The four purposes
Triangulation, using one strand to confirm or validate the other. Expansion, where quantitative data gives broader context to qualitative findings or the reverse. Exploration, where qualitative work chases unexpected quantitative results. And explanation, where "Qualitative data might be used to explain patterns or relationships identified through quantitative analysis."
2  ·  Concurrent designs
Concurrent triangulation collects both at once and compares them for convergence or discrepancy. Concurrent embedded gives one strand the primary role with the other embedded "to provide additional insights". Concurrent transformative examines the same problem from both perspectives to transform the understanding of it.
3  ·  Sequential designs
Sequential exploratory starts qualitative and follows with quantitative to confirm or extend. Sequential explanatory starts quantitative and follows with qualitative "to explain or provide context for the quantitative findings". Sequential transformative uses the second strand "to transform or challenge initial understandings".
4  ·  Fully integrated
"Researchers collect both types of data simultaneously and use them to inform each other throughout the entire study. This approach allows for ongoing dialogue between quantitative and qualitative components." The most demanding of the eleven, and the only one with no fixed order at all.
Eleven empirical designs sit alongside them

Experimental and quasi-experimental, the latter lacking random assignment. Descriptive and correlational, which "identifies patterns and associations but can't prove causation". Cross-sectional and longitudinal. Case-control and cohort, drawn from epidemiology. Plus mixed-methods, case studies and observational design.

Implications

Descriptive research describes what exists without touching it, which is its strength and its ceiling.

One design gets a section of its own, and the reason is that its limits are the ones most often ignored in practice.

The definition is built on a negative: descriptive research "seeks to describe “what exists” without affecting it. It provides a snapshot of the variables under study at a specific point in time." Variables are observed and recorded rather than manipulated.

Its advantages follow from the same restraint. It provides "background information that can be used for hypothesis formulation in future research", its structured methodology makes replication straightforward, and a large representative sample supports generalisation.

So do its limits, and the article states the decisive one without softening: descriptive work "Can describe a situation but can't establish cause-and-effect relationships." Depth is limited, and in qualitative descriptive work interpretation "may be influenced by the researcher's perspectives".

Where it is used, and why it still matters

Demographic surveys, public opinion polls, market research, naturalistic observation, case studies and longitudinal studies. "Descriptive research plays an essential role in academic fields, public policy, and business, providing foundational knowledge upon which further research can be built."

Conclusion

Ethics is not a closing checklist; every item traces back to a philosophical principle.

The instalment ends where the journey began, tying each of eleven ethical requirements to the doctrine it comes from rather than to a regulation it satisfies.

Each section would not just describe what these considerations are but would also tie them back to the philosophical principles that underpin them.

— Stop 5: Research Methodology, 11 September 2023
The four principles named
Informed consent "stems from the philosophical principle of autonomy". Confidentiality and privacy involve "the principle of respect for persons". Risk and harm minimisation is "grounded in the principle of non-maleficence". And the principle of justice requires that "the benefits and burdens of research are fairly distributed among all groups in society".
Where integrity sits
Reporting data honestly, without fabrication or falsification, is placed under "the philosophical domain of epistemology and ethics" rather than under compliance, which follows directly from Stop 1's argument that a method has to justify itself.
What else the section covers
Dual-use research, rooted in consequentialist theories. Animal ethics and the three Rs of replacement, reduction and refinement. Institutional Review Boards and the ethical review process. Data management, and publication ethics covering authorship, duplicate publication and plagiarism.
The handover to the final stop
The instalment closes on validity, reliability and generalisability, and on a bridge: "Research design informs the types of tools used, ethical considerations, and how research is evaluated. Philosophy of science often critiques these elements." Saunders' onion, at the next stop, is the model that puts all of it in order.
