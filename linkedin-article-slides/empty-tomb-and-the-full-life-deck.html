The Empty Tomb and the Full Life: What Still Rises Today

The urgent question is not whether the tomb was empty but what difference resurrection makes to someone struggling to resurrect their own life. Seven readings answer it from incompatible ontologies — Loke's abduction, Freke and Gandy's awakening, Lancaster's covenantal rhythm, Borg's vision, Yancey's repaired relationship, Spong's re-entry into life, and a grace-centred synthesis of the author's own. What unites them is a refusal to reduce. Resurrection is not what ends argument. It is what begins again after collapse.

Dr. Toronata Tambun

Introduction

The question is not whether the tomb was empty but whether something still lives in the space it left behind.

"The resurrection remains Christianity's most disruptive claim."

Situation

For many modern readers — "atheists, agnostics, cultural Christians, and those raised among literalist threats of damnation" — it is also its most unbelievable.

Complication

The article displaces the usual question. The more urgent one, for "a generation navigating unemployment, academic burnout, visa limbo, and spiritual exhaustion", is this: "What difference does resurrection make if I'm struggling to resurrect my own life?"

Question

"The question, then, is not whether the tomb was empty, but whether something still lives in the space it left behind. Something that reanimates memory, reclaims courage, restores vision."

Answer

"The aim is not to harmonize contradictions, nor to defend orthodoxy against skepticism. It is to make resurrection plausible again—for the modern reader who may have never disbelieved in God, but long stopped expecting life to come back from death." "What rose that morning may not only have been Jesus—but the capacity to believe in purpose again, without fear."

The Standpoint

The author writes as someone shaped by collapse, not as a theologian.

"I write not as a theologian, but as someone shaped by a long and layered spiritual disorientation." The biographical note is not decoration; it is the warrant for everything that follows.

"I was raised within Protestant fundamentalism, particularly the Southern Baptist tradition, where the dividing line between belief and unbelief was sharp and unquestionable. I learned early that holiness was performance, that faith meant certainty, and that resurrection was a future prize for those who stayed doctrinally correct. That was my starting point—not my destination."

"In time, the system that gave me structure also began to suffocate inquiry. What cracked it open for me was not rebellion, but encounter." Toyohiko Kagawa, discovered in a secondhand bookstore in Kanda, Tokyo, "offered the first rupture: that Christian love must be seen in labor, in justice, in proximity to the poor." "I began to see theology not as what one affirms, but how one shows up."

Henry Blackaby's Experiencing God, introduced by a Southern Baptist missionary in Singapore in 1995, "shifted my focus from doctrine to discernment." "It was the first time I encountered a God not trapped in propositions, but met in decisions." Philip Yancey arrived in September 1999 at a Baptist World Alliance conference in Interlaken: "His writing didn't fix my questions—it gave them shelter." "I credit him for showing me that grace isn't the end of suffering, but the decision to return while still limping."

Stott and Piper "formed the backbone of my earlier certainty" — and then broke it open. "If God is sovereign, what space is left for those who collapse? If grace is real, why is so much of our theology built on fear?" That question brought him to John Shelby Spong, encountered in Perth in 2008 through talks and writings: "He gave me language I didn't yet know I needed—not to reject resurrection, but to recover it from coercion."

Three semesters of Hebrew through the Hebrew University of Jerusalem grounded the vision. "It welcomed ambiguity. It treated questions as sacred. It helped me understand that resurrection need not be defended to be believed. It can be enacted—through memory, through practice, through refusing to let the story end at the tomb."

"This manuscript is not a product of systematic theology. It is the long aftermath of a life shaped by collapse and reorientation." "What follows is not a proof. It is a witness."

The Ontologies

Four readings disagree about what kind of thing rose.

The first four frameworks contest the nature of the event itself, and they are mutually incompatible by design.

1  ·  Loke (2020) — resurrection as the best available inference
"Andrew Loke (2020) offers one of the most analytically structured defenses of the resurrection. His method is explicitly abductive". "Yet Loke's most important move is epistemological: he argues that a bodily resurrection is the most coherent explanation given a theistic worldview. That is, Loke does not pretend to argue neutrally. His conclusion presupposes a metaphysical framework where divine intervention is possible. This is not a flaw—it is a clarification of boundaries." "In this sense, Loke's argument is structurally circular, but logically valid. It does not aim to convert a materialist. It aims to confirm that, for a theist, belief in the resurrection is not only consistent—it is rational." What he leaves untouched: "how such belief moves from inference to transformation."
2  ·  Freke & Gandy (2001) — resurrection as awakening from the ego
"Resurrection is not an event that happened to Jesus but a process that happens within each initiate." Drawing on Gnostic and Pagan mystery traditions, they hold that "taking the resurrection literally was the “faith of fools.”" "Philosophically, their model renders resurrection immune to critique by historiography. There is nothing to prove or disprove." "The resurrection is not an event to be documented" — "it is an identity to be recovered." "The tomb is not in Jerusalem. It is in the ego. And it is empty when one awakens." The article names the cost as well as the gain: "It rejects historical claims outright, and in doing so, it risks severing resurrection from its ethical consequences."
3  ·  Lancaster (2006) — resurrection as covenantal rhythm
"Where Loke relies on metaphysical coherence and Freke on mystical unveiling, D. Thomas Lancaster (2006) brings the resurrection back to earth—specifically, to the rhythms of Jewish tradition." "He roots the Gospel resurrection accounts in Torah logic, post-Sabbath ritual, and midrashic consistency." On the divergent Gospel accounts: "His argument: only a real event would have been told this many ways." Reading Matthew's epiphōskō as to draw on rather than dawn, "Lancaster argues that the resurrection may have occurred Saturday night, not Sunday morning." "If true, Jesus rose not with the sunrise, but with the first flicker of post-Sabbath freedom." "Where modern readers look for contradiction, he sees covenantal layering. Where critics see legend, he sees liturgy." "Not only did Jesus live again. The covenant did too."
4  ·  Borg (2007) — resurrection as vision, and as vindication
"Marcus Borg (2007) brings the resurrection debate into sharp focus by reminding readers of an uncomfortable fact: the earliest Christian witness to resurrection—Paul—never met the historical Jesus." "Borg argues that Paul's experience on the Damascus road was not a postmortem sighting of a physical Jesus, but a visionary disclosure of divine presence." Since Paul uses one verb for every appearance, "If Paul's encounter was visionary, then so, Borg contends, were the others." "For Paul, and for the apocalyptic imagination of the first-century Jew, visions could be as real as mountains." "Resurrection, in this view, is the vindication of Jesus' mission by the God who raised him—not because death was reversed, but because meaning was restored." "Importantly, Borg emphasizes that resurrection cannot be proven—but it also cannot be reduced."

The Repairs

Two more stop asking what rose and ask what it repairs.

The register changes. Neither framework argues about the event; both ask what it did to the people who could not recover from its absence.

01
Framework V
Yancey (1997) — the quiet return of trust
"Resurrection, for Yancey, is a slow realization. It is not a moment of spectacle but of internal alignment. It comes not with angelic trumpets, but with silence, memory, and touch." "Yancey is struck by what the resurrection accounts do not include. There are no heavenly choirs, no divine epiphanies on mountain peaks, no mass appearances to hostile unbelievers." "Instead, there are whispered names in gardens, scarred hands extended to doubters, and shared fish on an unfamiliar beach." "What Yancey finds extraordinary is the ordinary." "The stories do not attempt to overwhelm doubt. Instead, they invite it in. Jesus accommodates skepticism—not through argument, but through presence." "Resurrection, in this model, is not about victory over enemies. It is about reweaving relationship after rupture." The appearances "were pastoral: targeted to each person's wound." "Resurrection, then, is not the removal of doubt—but the quiet return of trust."
02
Framework VI
Spong (2016) — resurrection as permission, not proof
"For Spong, resurrection is a transformation in human consciousness—a re-entry into life, not after death, but within it." "Spong emphasizes that the Gospels portray no eyewitness to the resurrection moment itself. The tomb is discovered empty; Jesus is later experienced. This interpretive gap, he argues, is essential. It reflects that resurrection is not a visible event but a perceptual shift. It does not occur in time—it occurs in recognition." Peter, broken by grief, returns to Galilee and breaks bread: "Jesus did not come back in body. He returned in meaning." "Spong critiques the Church's tendency to weaponize resurrection as a doctrinal threat: believe this or be damned. For him, this is theological abuse. Resurrection was never intended to enforce belief. It was meant to invite freedom." "Resurrection, then, is not proof—it is permission." "What rose on Easter morning was not Jesus' anatomy, but the disciples' willingness to live as if love had won."
The Spong lines the article sets apart

"To live fully. To love wastefully. To be who we were created to be. That is resurrection." — John Shelby Spong. Two days before this essay, on Good Friday, the author had published a rendering of Spong's crucifixion sermon carrying the same phrase.

What the disciples actually found

"The tomb may or may not have been empty—but what the disciples found was not evidence. It was fire."

The Synthesis

The seventh reading is the author's own: resurrection as permission after the collapse of holiness.

Section VII builds from the theology developed in Hallowed Be Thy Name (Tambun, 2025). "The doctrine of resurrection, often framed through metaphysical assertion or empirical defense, rarely appears in tandem with the experience of moral collapse."

"In the theology of Tambun (2025), resurrection is not conceived as the undoing of physical death, but as the re-permission of life after the collapse of holiness. It is not the reversal of Friday's crucifixion, but the reinterpretation of it." "In this model, the crucifixion is not understood as divine punishment but as the visible end of a broken moral system. And the resurrection is the quiet announcement that even in that failure, grace still holds."

"The miracle, in this reading, is not located in a tomb's vacancy but in the permission to speak one's own name again after betrayal, shame, or theological exile." "It is not a reward for the righteous. It is an opening for the disoriented."

The move rests on a grammatical observation. Hallowed be Thy name "is a divine passive. The one praying has already failed to bear the name rightly." On the cross, bearing the name faithfully under distortion, "The holiness of the name is revealed not by distance from sin, but by unwavering mercy within it." "The one who yielded completely is the one God raised. Resurrection, then, is not divine reversal—it is divine vindication. It confirms not just that Jesus was alive, but that His path was right."

"Grace, as argued in Tambun (2025), is not leniency. It is divine initiative where collapse should have ended speech." "If the Cross enacts surrender, resurrection enacts permission. It signals that the prayer was not futile. The name was not abandoned. The speaker was not discarded." "Resurrection, in this model, is not proof of life after death. It is the confirmation that grace survives distortion."

The scene that carries it is John 21. Peter, who denied three times, is met at a charcoal fire, and "Jesus offers him breakfast—not as reward, but as restoration." "Resurrection, in this scene, becomes deeply practical: it is breakfast before mission. It is fire without accusation. It is memory reconditioned by grace."

The neurocognitive sequence

"Under collapse, the Default Mode Network deactivates; narrative control is relinquished. The ACC, registering moral failure, does not escalate anxiety but integrates it into confession. The insula enables interoceptive awareness of exposure without expulsion." "The brain does not initiate resurrection. It becomes available to grace."

Convergence

Seven incompatible ontologies converge on a refusal to reduce.

"These perspectives, divergent in ontology and method, converge on one insight: resurrection is not reducible to physicality, nor to metaphor. It is a living grammar that reorders memory, identity, and meaning."

"What unites these readings is their resistance to reduction. Loke argues from coherence but stops short of coercion. Freke and Gandy remove history but reintroduce self. Lancaster affirms narrative without forcing uniformity. Borg redefines vision as eschatological rupture. Yancey rehumanizes belief through sorrow. Spong destabilizes religion to recover life. Tambun reweaves collapse into a grammar of restored permission."

"Each insists, in different registers, that what rose on Easter was not merely a body—but a possibility. A claim on how to live. A refusal to let death define meaning."

"At stake is not simply the truth of an ancient claim, but its capacity to speak now. For those navigating vocational loss, institutional failure, or the quiet erosion of faith itself, the resurrection must become more than a doctrinal boundary. It must become a grammar of survival."

"What rises is not certainty. What rises is the courage to reenter meaning after it has broken down. What rises is not moral superiority. What rises is mercy that still holds memory." "It does not erase scars, but transfigures them. It does not require metaphysical assent, but permits ontological courage."

Conclusion

Resurrection is not what ends argument; it is what begins again after collapse.

"This essay has not sought to deny the resurrection. It has sought to reframe its theological urgency."

Resurrection, then, is not what ends argument. It is what begins again after collapse.

— The Empty Tomb and the Full Life: What Still Rises Today
Who the essay is written against
"For many, including those raised on the threat of damnation, the resurrection was once the most violent idea ever told: believe this or be lost. But perhaps the deeper witness of the empty tomb is that even those who fled, denied, or despaired were still included in the story God continued to tell."
What the emptiness was for
"If the tomb was empty, it was not to prove a miracle. It was to make space—for memory, for grace, for new grammar. What the disciples carried was not evidence. It was transformation."
What still rises
"What rose was not merely Jesus. What rose was trust. What rose was memory. What rose was a community daring to love again—wounds visible, shame disarmed, and breakfast still warm on the beach." "And that is what still rises."
