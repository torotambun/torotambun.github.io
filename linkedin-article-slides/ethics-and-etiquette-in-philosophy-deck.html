LinkedIn Article  ·  Philosophy · Ethics, Etiquette and the Choice of a Leader  ·  26 January 2024

Wanted: Leaders who Uphold Ethics and Implement Etiquettes

The article separates two things that everyday language treats as one. Ethics belongs to axiology and concerns moral value; etiquette belongs to social convention and concerns conduct. Keeping them apart matters because the failures are independent: a leader can violate ethics, breach etiquette, or do both, and only the last is what the article calls a double jeopardy. Having built the distinction across philosophy, the article declines to apply it. It hands three scenarios back to the reader as questions, and that refusal is the instrument: the reader is asked to make the judgement rather than to receive it.

Opening

A society can end up led by someone unethical and uncouth at once, and that is a double jeopardy.

The article opens by placing itself in a conversation already under way. A reader arriving from a companion LinkedIn post has been given three scenarios and asked to sort them into ethics cases and etiquette cases, and this piece is the apparatus for doing that sorting.

The stake is stated before any philosophy is introduced. The two failures are separable, so a leader may fail on one axis and hold on the other; the compound case, failing on both at once, is the one the article singles out.

The article then does something unusual for a piece of this kind: it defines its own key term formally, in a set-off box, before proceeding.

The stake, in the article's own words

"It may be a double jeopardies if a society ends up to be lead by those who were BOTH unethical (do not uphold ethics, tend to violate ethics) AND who were at the same time UNCOUTH."

The term it defines

Uncouth is glossed in the source as a formal English term for someone who lacks good manners, refinement or grace, covering a range of behaviours from simple rudeness to more offensive or vulgar conduct, and used where a more sophisticated level of discourse is expected.

Framework

Ethics sits inside axiology beside aesthetics, and both ask what is worth valuing.

The article picks one branch of philosophy deliberately, saying it chooses the branch it is familiar with, and locates ethics within it.

01
The parent branch
Axiology
The study of values, concerned with the nature of what is valuable or worthy. It contains two distinct areas of inquiry that are united by that focus rather than by subject matter.
02
First area
Ethics
Concerned with moral values, principles and judgements: what constitutes ethical behaviour, and how moral values are formed and justified. The article notes these principles can be deeply personal, shaped by culture, religion, philosophy or societal norms.
03
Second area
Aesthetics
Concerned with beauty, art and taste, and with the nature and appreciation of beauty whether in art, nature or elsewhere. Named here to show what ethics is being distinguished from within its own branch.
Why the placement matters

Locating ethics in axiology fixes it as a question about values. That is what later allows etiquette, which turns out to be a question about conventions, to be told apart from it rather than folded into it.

Details

Meta-ethics asks what “good” means; normative ethics prescribes what to do.

Ethics divides once more, and the article is careful that the two halves answer different kinds of question.

Meta-ethics
•Explores the nature, scope and meaning of moral judgements
•Asks what it means to say something is good
•Asks whether moral values are universal or vary between cultures
•Examines the origins and definitions of morality itself
•Prescribes no specific behaviour
Normative ethics
•Determines what is right and wrong
•Establishes how things ought to be and how to value them
•Judges which actions are right and which character traits are good
•Supplies frameworks such as utilitarianism, deontology and virtue ethics
•Provides guidance for deciding what to do in a specific situation
Where this leads

The distinction is doing structural work. The article will later place etiquette under normative ethics in the broad sense, because it prescribes conduct, while insisting it is concerned with social convention rather than with moral dilemma.

The Paradox

Morality is either objective like natural law or a human creation varying by culture, and the choice decides whether a universal framework is possible.

The article sets out the disagreement under its own heading as a paradox. On one side, moral truths are held to be constant and to apply universally to all people regardless of time or place, existing independently of human beliefs or actions, so that justice and charity are inherently good and greed and theft inherently bad.

On the other, morality is a human creation and therefore subjective, varying with opinion and context. The evidence offered for this position is variance itself: moral standards differ significantly across cultures, and the article's example is that some societies consider alcohol consumption immoral while others regard it as acceptable.

What hangs on the answer is named explicitly. If moral truths are objective, a universal ethical framework can be built on them. If they are culturally shaped, the article notes, moral principles are not grounded in objective facts at all, and the prospect of one framework for everyone goes with them.

The article does not settle the question. It presents both positions with their supporting reasoning and moves on, which is consistent with how it will treat its own scenarios at the close.

The first position

"Some philosophers believe that morality is objective, akin to natural laws, and exists independently of human beliefs or actions."

The second

"On the other hand, moral relativists argue that morality is a human creation and thus subjective, varying with opinion and context."

Application

Facial recognition is where ethics meets philosophy of science, because biased training data turns a question of method into a question of fairness.

The article addresses this section to researchers and uses it to show the two fields meeting on a live case rather than in the abstract. Facial recognition has advanced sharply through artificial intelligence, and the philosophy of science, which studies how scientific knowledge is developed and validated, has a direct interest in the methods behind it.

The ethical questions come first and are about use: privacy, consent and surveillance. The article asks how the technology can be used without infringing individual privacy rights, and what follows ethically from deploying it for mass surveillance.

Then comes the question that joins the two fields. If the data used to train these systems is biased, the system may behave in a discriminatory way, which makes fairness and equality a consequence of methodology rather than of intention. The article's own framing is that this puts the focus on the methods used to develop the systems and on the validation of their outcomes.

The conclusion it draws is a balance rather than a prohibition. Scientific progress in artificial intelligence offers considerable benefit, and the article's requirement is that it be developed and used in a way that upholds ethical standards and reflects societal values.

The test it proposes

Whether the methodologies are robust enough to ensure fairness and objectivity, and how a developer establishes that a system is ethically sound and scientifically valid at the same time. Both are posed as open questions in the source.

Framework

Read through seven traditions, etiquette is an expression of virtue and a display of power at once.

The article gives etiquette the same treatment it gave ethics, running it through seven philosophical perspectives. The first six read it as formation; the seventh reads it as stratification.

1  ·  Philosophy of social practices
Etiquette is examined for its part in shaping customs and norms, tracing how politeness and decorum evolve, how they vary by culture, and what they signal about a society's values and structures.
2  ·  Philosophical anthropology
Etiquette is a key cultural characteristic that distinguishes and defines human societies. The article's phrasing is that it is more than manners; it concerns identity and the subtle ways people communicate who they are.
3  ·  Axiology
From the axiological view, etiquette represents a practical application of ethical principles. Respect, kindness and consideration embedded in social rules are treated as manifestations of foundational ethical principles rather than as pleasantries.
4  ·  Moral philosophy
Etiquette is an expression of virtue. Respect, civility and courtesy become tangible through it, shaping interaction and, in the article's terms, building the social world.
5  ·  Philosophy of language
Etiquette is part of how language is used. Speech act theory is cited to show how choices of politeness and formality construct and reflect the social environments people inhabit.
6  ·  Philosophy of education
Etiquette is part of moral and social education, teaching how to conduct social interaction with grace and respect and how to read the implicit rules of social engagement.
7  ·  Critical theory
Etiquette reflects and enacts societal power dynamics, stratifications and cultural dominances. The article describes it as a subtle expression of power carried through everyday courtesies and norms.
Where the article places it

Etiquette is held to fall under normative ethics in the broad sense, because it prescribes how individuals ought to behave. The qualification is that its rules facilitate interpersonal relations and social harmony without necessarily deriving from deeper moral principles, which is what keeps it distinct from ethics proper.

Evidence

Zephyria, Avalonia and Eldoria stage the distinction in three invented countries, and the invention is deliberate.

The worked examples are set in fictional states. The article says so in each case, and the deck preserves that framing exactly as written; nothing has been identified or attributed on its behalf.

SCENARIO A
Zephyria, a meta-ethics case
"Imagine a fictional country, Zephyria, where its President is accused of influencing the judiciary to amend the constitution for personal gain." The amendment lowers the age requirement for the Vice Presidency so that the President's son, currently underage for the role, becomes eligible. The article is explicit that a meta-ethical reading examines what ethical leadership in a democracy means and how cultural perception shapes the understanding of political corruption, "rather than judging the action itself."
SCENARIO B
Eldoria, a normative ethics case
A fictional nation launches a programme giving advanced educational opportunities to students from underprivileged backgrounds, presented as a step towards rectifying historical inequities in access. The article evaluates it against justice, equality and the right to education, and endorses the principle that access should not depend on socioeconomic status.
SCENARIO C
Avalonia, an etiquette case
"During a televised national debate in a fictional country, Avalonia, a young, up-and-coming political candidate repeatedly uses mocking gestures and names to belittle their more experienced opponents." The article classifies this as a breach of socially accepted conduct in a formal setting, where the focus is on manners rather than on deeper ethical principles.
SCENARIO D
Why three and not one
The set is constructed so that the three cases land in different categories. One is a question about the meaning of ethical concepts, one is a moral judgement, and one is a matter of conduct. A reader who cannot tell them apart is the reader the article is written for.

Conclusion

The article ends by handing the three scenarios back unanswered, and the refusal is the argument.

Having supplied the distinction across eight sections, the article declines to apply it. The closing move returns the original three cases as questions and stops.

Ethics, with its focus on moral judgment, and etiquette, with its cultural expressions, together form a complex picture of human interaction and social order.

— Wanted: Leaders who Uphold Ethics and Implement Etiquettes, 26 January 2024
The first question
"A ruler manipulates the justice system for family gain. Is this an issue of ethics or etiquette?"
The second
"In a debate, a presidential candidate is impolite and disrespectful. Does this concern ethics or etiquette?"
The third
"During a debate, participants use lies, derogatory remarks, and even mock physical gestures and facial expressions. Are these actions ethical violations or etiquette breaches?"
What the refusal does
The article states its purpose for gathering the material as helping a reader make a rational decision when choosing a leader. Supplying the answers would make that decision for them, so the titles above describe what the piece establishes by its method and not verdicts it withholds.
