Who Thrives in Today's Competitions?

Five years of moving between precarious local students and Ivy League alumni shows a gap that culture explains but does not excuse — and the longest study of its kind says the trait that actually separates them is emotional regulation, which is teachable.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Five years of alternating between two worlds of the same generation.

"Over the past five years, my routine has alternated between two vastly different worlds."

Situation

Through Yayasan Mens et Manus he works "with local students who are precarious and underrepresented, often coming from challenging socio-cultural environments." The mission is "to bring the world to these students" — "exposing them to knowledge, opportunities, and perspectives they might otherwise never encounter."

Complication

The other half of the routine is Jakarta, Singapore, Boston and Bandung, "engaging with Harvard and MIT alumni, Ivy League graduates, or even current students from these esteemed institutions". And: "The contrast is striking and, at times, difficult to reconcile."

Question

"Why do some individuals excel while others struggle, even when opportunities are available?"

Answer

The answer offered is not talent, wealth or schooling. It is emotional regulation — and the evidence for it comes from a study that has followed the same thousand people for forty years.

The First World

Wedi, sungkan and isin are culturally significant and they block the three things competition rewards.

"many of these students are raised in a collective and hierarchical culture deeply rooted in wedi (fear), sungkan(deference), and isin (shame)." The article is careful to grant the values their standing before naming their cost.

1  ·  Wedi — fear of making mistakes
Fear of error, in a competition that rewards attempts. The article's own framing: these values, "while having their own cultural significance, can create significant barriers to personal and professional growth in a rapidly changing global landscape."
2  ·  Sungkan — excessive deference to authority
Deference that makes disagreement with a teacher, a supervisor or an institution feel improper — and so removes the moment where an idea would have been tested.
3  ·  Isin — an overpowering sense of shame
Together the three "often lead to a hesitance to express ideas, take risks, or even embrace failure—all qualities that are essential for growth, especially in today's competitive and unpredictable world."
What the competition actually rewards

Success, the article argues, "hinges on three critical factors: storytelling, writing ability, and the quality of ideas" — "revised from Patrick Winston's original framework." Each of the three requires exactly what wedi, sungkan and isin discourage: putting a claim in public and defending it.

The relationship this is written from

"I am so blessed to know many of them from 2018, until this semester where things change. All these years my students mostly become my co-learners." The judgment that follows is made about one cohort by someone describing six years of the opposite.

The Exception

This semester was the exception, and a fourteen-week bootcamp ended with the demo day cancelled.

"This odd semester 2024-2025 is a an exception. The only time, I adjusted my delivery to meet their very fragile mentality."

The characterisation is blunt and it is his own: "These are students who think the world revolves around them and they were the best in the world by their mothers' eye, very fragile, demanding but lack of hard working and delivery and the institution try to protect them as if they were crème de la crème, ehemmmm, really ?"

He places them in a named framework: "Dunning Kruger framework will categorize them under “Unconsciousness Incompetence” quadrant. They are unaware of their lack of competence, as they don't have enough understanding to even see where they fall short."

And a sharper charge, stated once: "These are students who are infamous bullying their teachers behind their “mental health shield”".

Then the consequence, which is the part that cost him something. "Despite participating in a structured 14-week bootcamp designed to prepare them for real-world challenges, some showed little to no progress, resulting in the cancellation of a demo day—a painful and humbling decision I had to make."

"This outcome highlighted how the cultural traits of wedi, sungkan, and isin can undermine even the best-designed educational interventions if left unaddressed." The design was not the variable that failed.

The Second World

The other room articulates, questions and connects — and was trained to, not born to.

The comparison is drawn plainly, and the article does not pretend the two groups started level.

What the second world was given
•"These individuals are shaped by privilege, exceptional resources, and access to the best education and networks in the world"
•Years of "rigorous education, mentorship, and exposure to diverse ideas"
•Environments "of high expectations, competition, and opportunity"
•Training that goes past technical knowledge
•Networks that carry them past their own limits
What that produced in them
✓"They exude confidence, possess a remarkable ability to articulate their thoughts"
✓"an impressive understanding of business, industry, and innovation"
✓Training that "develops their critical thinking, adaptability, and ability to connect ideas to broader, global contexts"
✓"They have been trained to question, innovate, and push boundaries"
✓"Their confidence and preparedness stand in stark contrast to the struggles of the students I teach"
The claim being made, and the one being avoided

The second world's advantages are attributed to training and resources, not to ability. "skills that are nurtured through years of rigorous education, mentorship, and exposure to diverse ideas" is the article's phrase. That is what keeps the comparison from being a verdict on the students in the first world.

The author's own position in the comparison

"As a local graduate in nuclear engineering, my path was far from linear or guaranteed. By grace and perseverance, I navigated challenges and opportunities to reach where I am today. I am not alone in this experience; many other locally educated individuals have risen to global prominence, breaking through barriers with determination and grit."

Evidence

Dunedin followed a thousand people for forty years and found emotional regulation beats IQ, grades and family wealth.

"The answer to this question may lie in research, such as the groundbreaking Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study from New Zealand".

FINDING A
The study
"This longitudinal study has followed the lives of over 1,000 individuals born in 1972–1973 for more than 40 years, resulting in over 1,000 published papers that explore the factors shaping human development and success. It is one of the most comprehensive investigations of its kind".
FINDING B
The finding
"emotional intelligence—particularly the ability to manage emotions constructively—is a stronger predictor of long-term success than academic achievement, IQ, or family wealth."
FINDING C
Which traits
"Traits such as self-control, emotional regulation, and resilience play a critical role in determining both financial stability and personal fulfillment later in life."
FINDING D
Why it changes the problem
"The good news is that these traits are not fixed; they can be cultivated through effective parenting, education, and structured interventions." A cultural barrier that cannot move is a verdict; a trait that can be taught is a design problem.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Implications

Those traits are not fixed, which is the only reason the gap is addressable at all.

The article closes on 2045, and on what both worlds owe it.

Both groups I interact with—local students facing cultural and systemic challenges and accomplished alumni from world-class institutions—must ultimately contribute to the vision of "Golden Indonesia 2045."

— Dr. Toronata Tambun
What the finding asks of educators
"This finding underscores the importance of equipping students with skills that go beyond technical knowledge or academic achievement." The intervention point moves from curriculum to character.
The size of the task
"Bridging the gap between these two worlds is no small task. It requires a long-term commitment to fostering emotional intelligence, adaptability, and critical thinking in the next generation."
The constraint that makes it hard
"The challenge lies in designing educational and cultural interventions that empower students to overcome the limitations imposed by deeply ingrained norms while honoring their cultural heritage." Not replacing the culture — working with it intact. That is the harder brief and the article names it as such.
Where it leaves him
"These reflections come from a place of both concern and hope." And: "The stakes are high, but so is the potential. With intentional efforts, Indonesia's journey toward 2045 can become a story of transformation, resilience, and shared success."
