A Ruthless Audience Diagnosis for the Thought Leadership Writer

Thought leadership fails not for want of intelligence but because it begins with the writer. Eleven audience segments each carry a visible struggle and a hidden one, and only the hidden one is worth writing about. The writer's job is to say what a group privately knows and publicly cannot afford to say — which means the work begins away from the keyboard.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A writer told to know the audience has been given advice too vague to use.

This reflection grew out of a homework exercise in a Harvard Alumni Entrepreneurs workshop series organized by Jamie Nies and led by Rhea Wessel.

Situation

Thought leadership is frequently mistaken for the ability to produce strong ideas and express them clearly. That matters. A writer may be intelligent, experienced and articulate.

Complication

And still fail to make any lasting contribution. Most writers start from what they know, what they have experienced, or what they feel compelled to say — which produces sincere writing, sometimes impressive writing. But sincerity is not the same as usefulness.

Question

If the ideas are good and the prose is clear, what exactly is missing from writing that earns a polite nod and is never thought about again?

Answer

The diagnosis. "Thought leadership begins not with display but with diagnosis." Not with the writer's need to speak, but with the audience's need to be understood accurately.

The Reframe

Thought leadership begins not with the writer's ideas but with the audience's fracture.

Segmentation is the harder task hiding inside the vague instruction.

Different groups do not merely want different content. They live inside different fears, different illusions, and different forms of pressure. A student may be trapped by borrowed ambition; a job seeker may be wounded by repeated external judgment; an executive may speak of transformation while protecting institutional comfort.

This is why generic thought leadership fails. It may sound intelligent, but it does not enter any one group's real struggle deeply enough. "The writer who says 'my audience is everyone' usually ends up helping no one in particular."

A serious writer must therefore begin not as a communicator but as a diagnostician — asking who exactly this is for, what the group struggles with beneath the surface it presents, what false strengths it mistakes for progress, and what it does not easily admit even to itself.

The Eleven

The surface problem is never the entry point; the unnamed one is.

Eleven segments, each examined for the hidden fracture rather than the obvious characteristic. The line given for each is the one the group rarely says out loud.

1  ·  Undergraduate and graduate students
Visible: pressure. Hidden: borrowed identity performing as conviction. "what looks like drive is often compliance wearing a motivational t-shirt" — and the false strength is intellectual fluency, which is not judgment. What they rarely admit: that they do not know what they actually want.
2  ·  Job seekers
Visible: transition. Hidden: what sustained external judgment does to identity. "Repeated rejection does not only wound confidence. It quietly rewires how a person reads their own worth." The false strength is resilience rhetoric, which asks them to perform optimism and mask their actual state.
3  ·  Early-career professionals
Visible: inexperience. Hidden: being systematically trained out of judgment. "The person who gets promoted early is not the most original thinker. It is the person who most accurately models what their manager considers good work." The false strength is confidence — velocity without direction.
4  ·  Founders wannabe
Visible: not having started. Hidden: attachment to the identity of being an entrepreneur. "They have rehearsed the story of the venture far more carefully than they have examined the reality of what building it would actually require." The false strength is vision — imagination with an audience.
5  ·  Entrepreneurs and startup founders
Visible: resources. Hidden: identity fused with the company until accurate perception is impossible. "Resilience without recalibration is stubbornness." What they rarely admit: that some pivots called strategic learning were retreats from a judgment they were afraid to call wrong.
6  ·  Mid-career managers and emerging executives
Visible: execution pressure. Hidden: a broken feedback loop filled with self-confirming narrative. "Their experience has become a curated mythology rather than an accurate empirical record." They read the absence of contradiction as validation. It is social deference.
7  ·  University lecturers and educators
Visible: institutional constraint. Hidden: the slow corrosion of intellectual courage. "The educator who has not genuinely changed their mind about something important in five years is not demonstrating mastery. They are demonstrating intellectual stagnation behind the shield of credential."
8  ·  Corporate leaders and institutional decision-makers
Visible: complexity. Hidden: a system designed to prevent accurate information reaching them. "The higher you rise, the more information flows toward you — and the less of it is true." The most suppressed insight: the transformation announced is almost never the one delivered.
9  ·  Investors
Visible: risk and return. Hidden: pattern recognition rewarded until it is indistinguishable from prejudice. "The investor who has been right several times in a row is in significant danger — not because success corrupts, but because it teaches the wrong lessons." The deepest distortion is asymmetric attribution.
10  ·  Retirees and the recently retired
Visible: the transition out of work. Hidden: the collapse of identity infrastructure the job was quietly providing. "The crisis of retirement is not primarily financial. It is ontological." What they rarely admit: that they miss being needed.
11  ·  Newspaper and public-interest readers
Visible: information overload. Hidden: high consumption mistaken for understanding. The false strength is critical thinking — often motivated skepticism instead: "Identity defense wearing critical thinking's clothes." The media environment is built for engagement, not understanding.

The Sharpest Tool

The false strength is what the reader is proud of, and the sharpest tool.

Of all the diagnostic categories, this is the one that produces the most useful writing — because it is what the reader would cite in a performance review or on a conference panel.

01
Why it works
It honours what they built
Writing about a false strength is not an attack. It honours the intelligence of what the reader constructed while asking whether it is still serving them. That is a generous and precise act, and the one most likely to produce reflection rather than defence.
02
What it looks like
Fluency, resilience, experience, judgment
The student's intellectual fluency. The job seeker's resilience rhetoric. The manager's accumulated case library. The investor's hard-won judgment. Each is real, each is celebrated, and each is doing quiet damage where it substitutes for the thing it resembles.
03
What it is not
A character diagnosis
The false strength is produced by an environment that rewards it. Naming it as a personal flaw converts a structural observation into an insult and loses the reader in the same sentence.

The Silence

What matters most in each segment is not the feeling but the silence.

Name the silence, not just the struggle.

The executive who cannot admit the transformation will fail. The educator who cannot say the institution is not working. The founder who cannot call a retreat a retreat. The investor who cannot admit that access, not insight, drove their best returns. The retiree who cannot admit they miss being needed.

The writer's job is to say the thing that everyone in the room privately knows and publicly cannot afford to say. This is not recklessness. It is the specific service that good public writing provides: it gives a community permission to think something it has already been thinking.

Writing that gives the reader language for something they have felt but never named is the only kind that gets remembered. Addressing the visible struggle only reaches the part of the reader that already knows what the problem is.

The Door

One idea must enter through a different door for every segment, or it is broadcast.

Take one core idea — that most development confuses performance with growth — and watch where it has to enter.

Same Door for Everyone
✗One framing, issued to all eleven segments at once
✗Language chosen for what the writer finds interesting
✗The reader has to translate it into their own situation
✗Agreement without recognition; a share, then nothing
✗"A thought leadership writer who uses the same door for every audience is not practicing thought leadership. They are practicing broadcast."
The Segment Chooses the Door
✓For the student: the language of grades and approval
✓For the founders wannabe: the gap between the rehearsed story and the unglamorous reality
✓For the manager: the language of feedback loops and organizational silence
✓Same idea, entering where that group already feels the pressure
✓The segment determines the entry point, not the idea

Structural Honesty

Locating the struggle in the system, not the character, is more honest and more useful.

Structural honesty beats individual psychology — and the reason is practical, not merely polite.

The weakest version of audience-specific writing tells the reader they feel this way because of their personality or their mindset. The stronger version says they feel this way because the system they are inside is designed to produce exactly this feeling, and that not having named it is partly a function of that system's success.

Two things then happen. They feel less alone, because the struggle is not their private failure. And they feel more empowered, because a structural problem can be named, examined and sometimes resisted, whereas a character flaw feels fixed.

This is also why the diagnosis in this piece is operational rather than academic. Every observation about the eleven segments is a direct instruction about how to write for them: what to name, what to resist, where to enter, and what to refuse.

Conclusion

A diagnostic piece opens rather than resolves, and the work begins away from the keyboard.

The temptation at the end of a piece like this is to tie it up with a framework and a set of steps. The article resists that, and so does this deck.

Find that. Then write it.

— A Ruthless Audience Diagnosis for the Thought Leadership Writer
Why there are no recommendations here
"A good diagnostic piece does not resolve. It opens." The reader who has just been given precise language for something they have felt but never named does not need steps. They need space. "You are not writing a self-help manual. You are writing a lens."
What generic writing costs
Generic writing performs at the level of the nod: readers agree, share out of social alignment, and forget within a week. Diagnostic writing produces discomfort, recognition, and the specific feeling of being seen with precision. That feeling is what people return to.
Where the work actually starts
Not at the keyboard. It begins when you sit with a specific human group, with real patience and some ruthlessness, and ask what is true about their situation that they are not yet saying out loud.
