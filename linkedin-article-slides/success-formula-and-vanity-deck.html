Success ?

Success is written twice as an equation, and both times the equation is an argument about order rather than a calculation. The first accumulates speaking and writing over a quality term made of knowledge, practice and talent, with constraints declaring which outweighs which. The second makes dissatisfaction the catalyst, strategy the response, and luck a function of opportunity, preparation and action that fails if any one of them is zero. Having formalised what can be controlled, the article ends where formalisation stops: Ecclesiastes calls all of it vanity, and names the one thing that is not.

Introduction

Success written as a formula, and the formula is an argument about order.

The article opens on an equation and immediately says the equation is simpler than it looks.

"My first argument features a rather sophisticated look formula, but actually very simple, for the probability of success, P(s), (small “s”) which intricately links several variables to illustrate their combined effect on the likelihood of achieving success."

The choice to formalise is deliberate. An equation forces a writer to declare which terms multiply, which accumulate, and which outrank which — commitments that prose about success normally avoids making.

The accumulation is the first claim: "The integral sign ∫ suggests that success is an accumulation of efforts over the domain x, which can represent time, effort, or other resources. This implies that success is not a singular event but a cumulative process that unfolds over time."

And progress is granular: "dx represents a small increment within this domain, emphasizing that progress toward success is often achieved in incremental steps."

Framework

P(s) accumulates over speaking, writing, and a quality made of knowledge, practice and talent.

Each term of the first equation, in the article's own reading of it.

1  ·  S — the ability to speak
"S stands for the ability to speak, which might symbolize storytelling skills. This component acknowledges the power of oral communication and narrative in conveying ideas and engaging audiences, which is vital in many paths to success."
2  ·  W — the ability to write
"W symbolizes the ability to write, indicating literacy or the capacity to express oneself through written word. Writing proficiency allows for the articulation of complex ideas and extends one's influence beyond immediate verbal interactions."
3  ·  K — knowledge
"Knowledge, suggesting the depth of understanding and information one has acquired. It forms the substantive basis of what one communicates."
4  ·  π — practice
"Practice, implying the amount of training and experience one has engaged in. Practice refines and sharpens skills, enhancing the quality of output."
5  ·  τ — talent
"Talent, or the natural aptitude one possesses. Talent can give an initial advantage but requires nurturing through practice and learning to be fully realized." Talent is present in the model and is the weakest term in it.
Where the hierarchy comes from

The order — speaking, then writing, then the quality of ideas — is Patrick Winston's, and it recurs across this run of the author's articles. Here it is not asserted but built into the constraints of an equation.

Details

The constraints are the claim: knowledge outweighs practice, practice outweighs talent.

Two inequalities carry more of the argument than the equation they qualify.

The constraints are stated together: "The constraints, denoted by α>β>γ and ωK > ωπ > ωτ, establish a hierarchy and relationships between these variables".

The first ranks the modes: "α>β>γ likely compares the relative importance or impact of speaking (α), writing (β), and the quality (γ) of ideas or communication."

The second ranks the sources of quality: "ωK > ωπ > ωτ specifies that knowledge (ωK) when combined with the weight ω, has a greater impact on success than practice (ωπ) and talent (ωτ)."

Read together they are a strong and falsifiable position: what you know matters more than how much you have rehearsed, and both matter more than what you were born with. A model that ranked talent first would be a different argument about who can succeed.

Evidence

Luck enters with a minus sign, and strategy turns the setback into the story.

The second model replaces ability with dissatisfaction, and makes room for what cannot be controlled.

The catalyst is discontent: "At the heart of this model is the premise that a fundamental dissatisfaction with the status quo (Δ) acts as a catalyst for change. It's the discomfort with the present that propels individuals to strategize (M) and develop a robust framework to address their discontent."

And the strategy is required to move: "This strategy isn't static; it's an adaptable model designed to overcome obstacles and optimize outcomes."

What the model concedes is unusual for a success formula. It allows "the recognition of 'luck' or 'God's intervention' (ξ), acknowledging that despite one's best efforts, there are factors at play that can neither be predicted nor controlled. In this model, such humility doesn't negate effort but complements it".

Luck is then given a structure of its own — "The function of luck (ξ(θ, ρ, κ)) is defined as the interplay between opportunity (θ), preparation (ρ), and action (κ)." And it is conjunctive, not additive.

Why all three terms must be nonzero

"For luck to be actualized, all three elements must be present and nonzero. Opportunity without preparation or action is squandered potential, preparation without opportunity or action is stagnant readiness, and action without preparation or opportunity is directionless energy." And on the minus sign: setbacks, "with a strong strategic framework, these are not merely overcome; they become integral to the story of success, transforming disturbances into strengths."

The Turn

This is the fourth time I have written about success, and I am not a fan of the subject.

The article turns on its own topic, and the reason is worth keeping.

"This is I think the 4th series of write-up about success, which I am not fan of talking about it. I am more proud of my failures, because I learn more from them."

The reasoning is not modesty but epistemics: "Success for a naturally prone to one is like a magnet, hence failures are more interesting to learn from after overcoming and in hindsight."

What he says changed his view: "What relieved me in 2019 are the learnings that human are irrational, teams / other people are necessary to be around, and there is this “unintended effect” or “higher order feedback” in our life."

And the scale the article finally sets the question at: "In the whole measure of universe, we are a paradox: nothing but something in His eye" — a phrase the author immediately qualifies in the source as an anthropomorphic way of speaking.

Higher order feedback

"This saga of success invites us to embrace the full spectrum of our experiences, recognizing that while we may steer our ship with intent, the currents of “higher order feedback” are partners in our voyage, sometimes challenging, sometimes rewarding, but always integral to the odyssey of life."

Conclusion

Ecclesiastes calls all of it vanity, and names the one thing that is not.

Having formalised what can be controlled, the article ends where formalisation stops.

The end of the matter; all has been heard. Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man.

— Ecclesiastes 12:13, quoted in “Success ?”, 3 March 2024
The verse the argument is measured against
"Ecclesiastes (Kohelet) 1:2: Vanity of vanities, says Kohelet; vanity of vanities, all is vanity." The article reads it as "a philosophical reflection on the meaning of life and the best way to live, often emphasizing the fleeting nature of worldly pursuits."
What survives the verdict
"Thus, according to Ecclesiastes, fearing God and keeping His commandments is not vanity. This reflects the book's ultimate message that finding purpose and meaning in life comes from recognizing and living in accordance with divine principles, which transcends the temporary and often unsatisfying nature of worldly pursuits."
Why the ending does not cancel the equations
Both models already contain a term for what the author cannot control, and both already rank effort above endowment. The scriptural close does not overturn the formalisation; it states the boundary the formalisation was always inside.
