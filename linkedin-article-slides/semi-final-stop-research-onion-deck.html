Semi-Final Stop: Saunders' Research Onion

The journey's last stop reaches the model that arranges everything the previous five covered, and the arrangement is the argument. Saunders' onion is peeled from the philosophical stance at the core outward to the practicalities of data collection at the skin, which means each choice constrains the next and a researcher cannot legitimately begin at the outside. Philosophy comes first, then approach, then methodology, strategy, time horizon and collection methods. Each layer is set out with strengths, weaknesses and application, and the treatment is even-handed enough that the weaknesses are as specific as the strengths. But the instalment's own layer numbering does not hold: research philosophy is Layer 1 in the summary and Layer 2 in the elaboration, research approach is Layer 2 and then Layer 3, and Layer 3 Methodology never appears in the summary at all. For a model whose entire claim rests on the order of its layers, that is the one thing that has to be right.

Introduction

The onion is peeled from the philosophical stance outward, which is what makes the order load-bearing.

The model is introduced by its method rather than its content, and the direction of travel is the whole point: it "helps researchers methodically design their research projects by peeling back the layers, starting from their philosophical stance, and working outwards to the practicalities of data collection."

That ordering is the answer to the problem the previous five stops have been circling. A researcher who begins with a questionnaire has committed to a philosophy, an approach and a methodology without examining any of them.

The core layer is research philosophy, "the systematic source of beliefs and the nature of knowledge", and the article is explicit about what follows from it: "Different research philosophies suggest different methods, techniques, and procedures."

Three philosophies are given. Positivism "Assumes that reality is objective and singular, separate from the researcher" and aligns with quantitative work. Realism "Holds that reality exists independent of the human mind", in direct and critical forms. Interpretivism "Stresses the difference between people and the objects of the natural sciences" and aligns with qualitative methods.

The distinction inside realism, which the later stops rely on

Direct realism is "the belief that what you see, hear, touch, etc., represents the world exactly as it is". Critical realism holds that "what we experience (the images, sounds, etc.) are sensations or representations of the external world, not the direct things themselves", and that "our senses and perceptions can deceive us".

Framework

Six layers run from belief to instrument, and each one narrows what the next can be.

The layers are given here in the order the model intends, from the core outward, with the article's own numbering set aside for the moment.

Core
Research philosophy
Positivism, realism in its direct and critical forms, interpretivism. The article notes what each costs: positivism draws the criticism that it "can be limiting, especially when studying social phenomena, as human behavior can't always be reduced to simple cause-and-effect relationships", while interpretivist work is charged with lacking rigour and being hard to replicate.
Second
Research approach
Deduction, the top-down route, which is "systematic, structured, and can yield clear yes/no outcomes" but "can be seen as too rigid". Induction, the bottom-up route, which builds theory rather than testing it. Abduction, which starts from "a surprising observation or phenomenon that doesn't fit existing theories".
Third
Methodology
Quantitative, qualitative or mixed. The article ties the choice back to the researcher rather than the topic: it "is contingent upon one's research question, the nature of the data, the intended audience for the findings, and the researcher's personal epistemological and ontological stances."
Fourth
Research strategy
Experiment, survey, case study, grounded theory, ethnography, action research. Each is given strengths and weaknesses: experiments "May lack real-world validity because of the controlled environment", case studies produce results that "may not be generalizable", action research "May lack the detachment and neutrality of other research forms".
Fifth
Time horizon
Cross-sectional, a snapshot with "No Temporal Depth", efficient but unable to track change. Longitudinal, with repeated measurement of the same subjects, giving "a stronger basis for implying causality" at the cost of time and the risk of participants dropping out.
Skin
Data collection methods
Sampling, secondary data, observation, interviews, questionnaires. The outermost layer and the one most researchers start from, though by the model's logic it is the last thing that should be decided.

Gap

The article's own layer numbers contradict each other, in a model whose claim is that the order is what matters.

This is worth stating plainly because the onion's entire contribution is the sequence. If the layers can be renumbered, the model is a list rather than a method.

In the opening summary the sequence runs Layer 1 Research Philosophy, Layer 2 Research Approach, and then jumps directly to Layer 4 Research Strategies, Layer 5 Time Horizons and Layer 6 Data Collection Methods. Layer 3 is absent from the summary entirely.

In the elaboration that follows, the numbering shifts. Methodology appears as Layer 3, filling the gap. But research philosophy is now Layer 2 rather than Layer 1, and research approach is now Layer 3 rather than Layer 2, which puts two different subjects at Layer 3 within the same article.

The table labels inherit the confusion: the methodology section is headed Table 3 Layer 3, and so is the research approach section, so a reader following the tables cannot recover the intended order either.

Recorded, not repaired

The deck reproduces the layers in the order the model intends rather than in either of the article's two numberings, and states the discrepancy here instead of silently choosing one. Nothing in the substance of the six layers is affected; what is affected is a reader's ability to use the numbering as the article gives it.

Details

Deduction and induction pull in opposite directions, and the approach follows from the philosophy rather than the topic.

The second layer is where the model's constraint becomes visible, because each approach is tied to an epistemology rather than to a subject area.

Deductive, the top-down route
•Starts from a generalised theory and narrows to a testable hypothesis
•Theory-driven: the theory exists before the research question
•Structured, often quantitative testing methods
•Results generalised from sample to population
•Commonly used in natural sciences, associated with positivism
Inductive, the bottom-up route
•Starts from specific observations without a clear theory in mind
•Observation-driven: theory is the outcome, not the precursor
•Qualitative methods gathering rich, contextual data
•Findings may be less generalisable and harder to validate
•Common in social sciences and ethnography, associated with interpretivism
Abduction, and why it is neither

"Abduction seeks to find the best explanation for specific observations. It doesn't just generalize from specific instances (like induction) or test a generalized theory against specific instances (like deduction)." Its process is iterative, its strength is that it is "Highly creative and can lead to breakthrough insights.", and its weakness is that the best explanation "is often subjective and might change as more data becomes available." The example given is a doctor facing symptoms matching no known disease.

Evidence

Six strategies, and the article gives each its weaknesses as specifically as its strengths.

The fourth layer determines how data will be collected and analysed, and the even-handedness here is what makes the section usable for choosing rather than for justifying.

1  ·  Experiment and survey
Experiments manipulate variables under control, can demonstrate causality and are replicable, but raise ethical questions "especially if participants are unaware they are being studied". Surveys reach large samples with standardised questions, but "Responses might be superficial, especially with closed-ended questions" and can suffer response bias.
2  ·  Case study and grounded theory
Case studies focus on the why and how, going "beyond the surface to understand the underlying factors", at the cost of generalisability and with subjective interpretation. Grounded theory "Starts without a preconceived theory and develops one as the research progresses", with theoretical sampling, though it "Can be time-consuming".
3  ·  Ethnography
Immersion in the environment, with participant observation where "The researcher often lives with or spends significant time with the subjects." The article adds an example of its own beyond the standard one: "a researcher living with a group of young entrepreneurs to understand their decision making process and coping mechanism."
4  ·  Action research
"Practical research strategy aimed at solving a problem and bringing about change." Collaborative and iterative through cycles of planning, action, observation and reflection. Its weaknesses are stated frankly: it "May lack the detachment and neutrality of other research forms" and "Results might be too context specific."

Conclusion

The last layer is the one most researchers reach for first, which is the journey's closing point.

Sampling, secondary data, observation, interviews and questionnaires sit at the skin of the onion, and the model's order says they are the last decision rather than the first.

Knowing which methodology to apply is contingent upon one's research question, the nature of the data, the intended audience for the findings, and the researcher's personal epistemological and ontological stances.

— Semi-Final Stop: Saunders' Research Onion, 12 September 2023
Sampling and secondary data
Probability sampling gives every member a known, non-zero chance of selection; non-probability sampling does not, and "can introduce biases if not carefully designed". Secondary data saves time and cost but "Might not perfectly fit the current research questions", and the original collection method may not meet current standards.
Observation and interviews
Observation in a natural setting "Can provide more authentic and unbiased data" and captures behaviours that self-reports miss, but risks the researcher's bias in interpretation. Interviews offer depth and the flexibility "to probe deeper based on responses", against time cost and bias on both sides.
Questionnaires
Scalable and consistent, since "Every respondent gets the exact same questions". The limits are depth and response rate: "Not everyone may return the questionnaire or fully complete it."
Why this is the semi-final stop and not the last
The series ends here. No Final Stop was published, so the journey that began by distinguishing science from philosophy of science closes on the model that orders the whole design, with the destination itself left to the dissertation.
