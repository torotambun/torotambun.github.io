Using En-ROADS Simulation to see whether a corporate innovation initiative can be a savior to bring the country to the "richest"?

GDP per capita is the wrong test of whether a country is rich. The test is whether growth delivers what families actually feel — child growth and dry-season water — and En-ROADS is the instrument for finding out whether corporate innovation can carry that load within twelve to twenty-four months, or whether it is a slogan. There is no single lever, and no panacea.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

The Question

The question is whether corporate innovation is savior or slogan for Indonesia.

"Can corporate innovation be the path to real strength in Indonesia, or not?"

Situation

Corporate innovation is routinely offered as the mechanism by which a country becomes strong, and GDP growth is the number by which the claim is checked.

Complication

But growth that does not reach households is not strength, and a simulation makes that testable rather than assertable. "Decide whether corporate innovation is savior or slogan for Indonesia. Prove it in EN-ROADS and in household outcomes."

Question

Can a firm move levers that reach two degrees while growth is held high — and would that show up where families would feel it?

Answer

That is exactly what the exercise asks participants to establish for themselves, rather than accept. The simulation is the argument's instrument, not its illustration.

The Shift

Move leaders from GDP-first to delivery-first.

"Move leaders from GDP-first to delivery-first."

"Use EN-ROADS to link growth choices to what families feel, then design actions a firm can start within 12 to 24 months." The twelve-to-twenty-four-month window is the discipline: it excludes anything a company could promise without having to begin.

The reframing is what makes the question answerable. GDP-first asks whether the number rose. Delivery-first asks whether anything reached a household — and those can diverge for a decade without the first measure noticing.

The article's companion column supplies the frame: Indonesia at its richest: Strengthening governance for sustainable growth, published in The Jakarta Post in March 2025.

The Run

Set growth high, then reach two degrees with levers a firm can pull.

The simulation sequence, as the exercise sets it out.

1  ·  Baseline
"Note temperature for 2100." Everything afterwards is measured against this, so the starting point is established before any lever moves.
2  ·  Growth
"Set Near-term and Long-term Economic Growth to the high band. Leave “Climate change slows economic growth” ON. Note what happens to energy demand and temperature." Growth is deliberately not treated as the villain — it is held high and the question becomes what else must move.
3  ·  Population
"Test higher and lower. Note the effect size." The instruction is to observe the magnitude, which is the point: population is frequently invoked as decisive and the simulation lets that be checked rather than argued.
4  ·  The constraint that makes it corporate
"Now reach ≤ 2.0°C while keeping Growth high by moving levers that companies can drive in 12–24 months". Not levers a government could legislate over a decade. Levers a firm could start on Monday.

The Test

What should count as rich is whether growth delivers basics people feel.

The section heading reads: "Why this links to “Richest? Check the Child and the Tap Short”".

"What should count as rich is whether growth delivers basics people feel. Two measures matter most in this class: child growth and dry-season water. Use them as your reality check."

Both are chosen because they are difficult to fake and immediate to experience. A household knows whether a child is growing and whether the tap runs in the dry season, regardless of what the national accounts report.

The exercise's reading list sets Indonesia against comparators — Sri Lanka, Morocco, Egypt and Kenya — using national stunting figures, SDG 6.4.2 water-stress snapshots and GDP per capita, so that the child-and-tap test can be applied across countries at similar income levels rather than asserted for one.

What this deck does not supply

The comparative figures live in the article's reference list and its appendix, not in its body. No stunting percentage, water-stress score or GDP-per-capita value is stated here, because the article states none. The sources are recorded so the numbers can be looked up.

Multi-Solving

Multi-solving replaces the contest over who is most at fault.

The exercise "moves beyond the dichotomy of “most righteous” or “most at fault” groups, encouraging a multi-solving approach" and an equity-stakeholder framing.

01
The first prompt
How does solving my problem help you solve yours?
The question that converts a negotiation into a design problem. If the answer is nothing, the two parties are competing for the same lever rather than looking for a shared one.
02
The second prompt
Who needs to be here?
"not only me, me me, but multi stakeholders, right?" The composition of the room is treated as a variable, not a given — and getting it wrong bounds every solution the room can reach.
03
The third prompt
How can we strengthen the connections between us?
Described as "the only force that is strong enough to overcome the inertias of our silos, our departments, our industries, our jurisdictions is the power of human-to-human connection".
What the tool is credited with doing

"This transformative tool, notable for its balanced and humble approach, has gained recognition for inspiring young minds, as well as national and corporate leaders. It promotes a mindful and compassionate methodology in addressing climate challenges." It "empowers them to think critically & innovative, being mindful and compassionate in tackling climate change".

Conclusion

It takes many seeds to plant a garden, and there is no panacea.

The exercise's own summary of what the simulation reliably demonstrates.

it takes many seeds to plant a garden

— Using En-ROADS Simulation, quoting the multi-solving framing
What the run keeps showing
"no magical fix", "no one big solution", "no panacea". Participants arrive expecting a decisive lever and the model declines to supply one — which is the finding, not a failure of the exercise.
Why that answers the opening question
If no single lever closes the gap, then corporate innovation cannot be a savior in the sense the word implies. It can be one seed among many — which is a smaller claim, and a defensible one.
What the exercise is finally for
"This exemplifies the power of collective action and inclusive thinking in addressing the urgent challenges of our times." The simulation's purpose is not to settle the answer but to make participants derive it under constraint.
