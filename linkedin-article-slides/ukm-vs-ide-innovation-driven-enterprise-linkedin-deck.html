LinkedIn Article  ·  Entrepreneurship · SMEs and Innovation-Driven Enterprises  ·  31 October 2019

UKM vs “Usaha Berbasis Inovasi (IDE-Innovation Driven Enterprise)”

The article, written in Indonesian, argues that small and medium enterprises (UKM) and innovation-driven enterprises (IDE, also Usaha Berbasis Inovasi) are different kinds of business that policy makers routinely fail to tell apart. An SME is a livelihood in a local market, whereas an IDE risks large capital over a long wait for a large upside, and its usual problem is finding the buyer. On the equation of innovation as invention multiplied by commercialisation, a zero on either side leaves nothing. The article concludes that government should run two programmes toward one goal, stop capital programmes that preserve SMEs, and teach SMEs to sell to paying customers and to partner with technology groups.

Introduction

Fashionable vocabulary hides the difference between an SME and an innovation-driven enterprise, and the difference matters.

Situation

The article opens in the office of a CEO friend, whose walls are lined with business books. The friend owns a top-10 company, the first in its industry to list on the country's stock exchange, and complains that people repeat whatever term is current: 4.0, disruption, platform-based apps, innovation, digital, entrepreneurship. The friend sets brick-and-mortar business against the fashion for digital platform matchmaking, and the author adds that brick-and-mortar is the blood running through the veins of platform business.

Complication

The article says local discussion treats platform matchmaking as digital business and forgets that most goods made and sold are physical products. Speakers who press small and medium enterprises as a way out of economic trouble are said not to know who SMEs are, because they never studied entrepreneurship seriously and never practised it. The article cites Mark Twain as printed: "It ain’t what you don’t know that gets you into trouble. It’s what you don’t know for sure that just ain’t so".

Question

Are small and medium enterprises and innovation-driven enterprises the same kind of business, and does the difference matter for policy?

Answer

The article says they differ and that the difference matters. It calls the pair complementary, and it treats the failure to tell them apart as a critical error, because the policy that supports each must differ.

Evidence note

The opening scene is one conversation with an unnamed friend. The article gives no survey or data on how widely the fashion for these terms runs.

Details

An SME is a livelihood in a local market, while an innovation-driven enterprise is a long, high-risk venture with a large upside.

The article sets the two apart by market reach, jobs and risk profile. It uses IDE and UBI (Usaha Berbasis Inovasi) for the same category.

SME (UKM)
•More than 62 million in Indonesia, selling traditional goods and services in local and regional markets: shops, home industries, warteg and gyms, the mom-and-pop kind
•The least affected by the 1998 Asian crisis, and useful as work for founders with basic skills and limited schooling when large companies cut staff
•Typically a founder, a spouse and a few employees, so the jobs are few and small in pay, many of them perhaps below the minimum wage, with no insurance, overtime or health allowance
•Vital as a livelihood, yet neither strong enough nor powerful enough to be the growth engine of the whole economy
•Defined differently for tax purposes: revenue below 4.5 billion rupiah a year
Innovation-driven enterprise (IDE / UBI)
•Capital that can be lost before break-even, possibly very large depending on R&D
•A long time to reach break-even, summed up in the article's phrase "higher risk, higher return"
•A very large chance of growth in revenue and market reach
•A problem that is usually not the invention but making sure who the buyer is, as in biotech companies and global pharmaceutical companies
•Staff with advanced education and training, joined as the firm grows by lab technicians, factory workers and hospital workers; it is said that each high-tech job needs five employees with basic vocational training

Gap

Policy makers fail to tell the two apart, and SMEs alone supply only a fraction of the jobs an economic transformation needs.

The article says that people in general and policy makers alike often fail to distinguish the jobs created by SMEs from the jobs created by innovation-driven enterprises. It calls the failure a critical error, because the two are very different and so the policy that supports each must be different.

SMEs do create work, but the article puts it as only a small part of what a large-scale economic transformation requires. Innovation-driven enterprises are the ones that should create more of the jobs, export, and lead the region. If job creation and economic prosperity are the state's goals, the article says, innovation-driven enterprises should be the focus and a major element of the strategy of government and policy makers.

Preserving SMEs merely because they already exist may create a vicious cycle, the article suggests, in which SMEs are the last to leave the middle-income trap, or even a low-income trap. Its simple proposal is to pair SMEs with activities that create a highly knowledgeable and highly skilled generation ten to fifteen years ahead. It adds that this should have started fifteen years ago, or perhaps must start now, and that it is not a digital economy proposal.

Evidence note

The article gives no data on the share of jobs that SMEs supply or on the middle-income trap. The trap claim is hedged in the source itself with the Indonesian word barangkali, meaning perhaps.

Framework

Innovation is invention multiplied by commercialisation, so a zero on either side leaves nothing.

The article opens and closes on this equation and glosses commercialisation as whether the product sells, that is, whether anyone buys it.

01
The equation
Innovation = Invention × Commercialization
The discussion is anchored on this formula. The article says the problem of innovation-driven enterprises is usually not the invention but the ability to make sure who the buyer is.
02
Zero on the invention side
Commerce without invention
Trading alone, acting as an agent alone, brokering alone and a rent-seeker mentality are commercialisation with nothing beneath it, so innovation is zero. The article says an invention need not be technology: it can be a business model, a process improvement or a social focus.
03
Zero on the market side
Invention without a buyer
High-tech work that nobody buys also yields zero innovation. The article says this often happens in Indonesia too, and adds that it is not the topic of this piece.

Implications

Government should run two programmes toward one goal, and build disciplined entrepreneurs instead of handing out fish.

The article says both types are needed for the living economy that government and voters want. It puts the aim as making the fishing rod, a robust and dynamic framework that teaches entrepreneurs to be "paying customer oriented" because their capital is limited, and not supplying the fish.

1  ·  Two departments in the ministry
Departments whose mindsets and programmes differ greatly, aimed at the same goal.
2  ·  Different training
Training programmes that differ from the start, with different education, training and recruitment for the technology-based capability that innovation-driven enterprises need.
3  ·  Tax incentives for accelerators and mentors
Tax incentives for the providers of accelerator and mentoring services, for SMEs and for innovation-driven enterprises alike.
4  ·  Support designed differently
Programmes that support entrepreneurs, designed very differently for each type. The article warns that mixing the regulations, or preserving SMEs without giving them technology-based capability, leaves no legacy and only fills time while something exists for now.
5  ·  Disciplined entrepreneurs, on an Akabri model
Creating disciplined entrepreneurs should be the theme now, through an Akabri-style academy for entrepreneurs that produces discipline and shared value. Akabri was the Indonesian armed forces academy. The article says a tool and framework exist and calls them proven.
6  ·  The record claimed for that framework
It is said to have created an ecosystem of entrepreneurs equal to the tenth-largest economy in the world at 1.95 trillion dollars, with alumni forming 200 to 400 new companies a year and 25,000 alumni whose innovation-driven companies still exist. The article adds that about 40 million businesses created in one superpower over 25 years were of the innovation-driven type.
Evidence note

The article gives no source for the 1.95 trillion dollar economy, the 200 to 400 companies a year, the 25,000 alumni or the 40 million businesses, and does not name the framework, the ecosystem or the superpower. One word in the 25,000 sentence prints as hides and is not repaired here.

Conclusion

Stop funding SME preservation, and teach SMEs to sell to paying customers and to partner with technology groups.

The article ends on three messages.

1
Stop preserving SMEs that are identified with tight budgets and poverty, and stop capital programmes
The article says the issue is not capital, so programmes that supply capital miss it.
2
Teach SMEs to build the business around the buyer
The article's terms are "paying customer oriented" rather than "product oriented", and it says the aim is not merely to pile up products in the village or the cooperative. It asks for simple "primary market research" to establish who will pay for the product and how many buyers there are.
3
Teach SMEs to befriend other village groups and technology-based groups
The article's examples are a vocational electronics school (SMK Vokasi Elektronik) and residents who once worked in manufacturing abroad.
