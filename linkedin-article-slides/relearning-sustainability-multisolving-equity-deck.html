Relearning Sustainability: Multisolving and Equity as Core Tenets for Lifelong Educators and Students

A room of new engineering physics students put a single lever at 1.5 degrees when the simulator gives it one-tenth of one, and the gap is not arithmetic but sense-making. Multisolving is the correction, and equity is what decides whether it works — but neither survives educators who stopped learning or students whose sustainability ends at a reusable bottle.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

I offered 500,000 rupiahs for the right answer and the room went silent.

A session at the Dies Natalis of the Nuclear Engineering and Engineering Physics Department, hosted by KMTNTF at Universitas Gadjah Mada, in a room of new students.

Situation

The discussion was on reducing global temperatures — a challenge sitting at the core of their studies in engineering physics, and one on which there is no single, one-size-fits-all solution.

Complication

I put two questions to them, each carrying a 500,000 rupiah reward: how much the temperature falls if we focus solely on renewables and spend trillions, and how much if we focus solely on electrifying transportation, again spending trillions.

Question

The room stayed silent — not from disinterest, but because the weight of the questions left them unsure. So I began to cold-call.

Answer

Many suggested renewables alone could reduce global temperatures by 1.5 degrees, and electrifying transport by 1.2. The answers came with confidence, and they revealed a significant gap in understanding.

The Gap

They had 1.8 degrees of room and believed one lever could take most of it.

The arithmetic underneath the guesses is what makes them worth writing about.

The students had only 1.8 degrees Celsius left to manoeuvre before the critical 1.5-degree target, and their answers assigned 83.3 percent of that budget to renewables alone and 66.7 percent to electrification alone. Assumptions of that shape would likely have disqualified them from passing a rigorous consultancy entry test, where sense-making is a requirement of precise and realistic problem-solving.

The reality, as demonstrated by the MIT Sloan Sustainability Initiative and Climate Interactive's En-ROADS simulation, is stark: even with enormous investment in renewables or in electrifying transport, global temperature falls by a mere 0.1 degrees Celsius.

I ended the session with the questions still in the air, not to emphasise their mistakes but to ignite a drive to seek greater understanding. Later that day one student wrote to me on LinkedIn — thoughtful, reflective, genuinely wanting to learn from the experience — and that is the part worth being proud of.

Framework

Multisolving pays for itself because it recruits constituencies that do not otherwise collaborate.

This section is credited in the article to Elizabeth Sawin of the Multisolving Institute and to Climate Interactive, led by Andrew Jones.

A multisolving policy or project generates multiple benefits at once — across climate mitigation, public health, economic development, equity, disaster resilience and more. The breadth of benefit means multisolving initiatives typically generate more savings than costs, and by paying out across different categories they win over constituencies who would not normally collaborate.

The approach lets us design the communities we want to live in even as we prevent and prepare for climate change. Its effectiveness, however, depends heavily on how equitably the benefits are distributed. Policies that pursue environmental benefit without addressing social and economic dimensions can inadvertently widen existing inequalities.

That is not only a matter of social justice; it is a condition of the strategies working at all. Where low-income, marginalised or indigenous communities are disproportionately burdened by climate policy or excluded from its benefits, the social fabric that broad-based climate action depends on frays. The Equity Considerations cheat sheet sets out eighteen areas where climate actions land unequally.

Details

On the supply side, every transition bills the workers and neighbours of what is retired.

The first six of the eighteen equity considerations, each pairing the multisolving benefit with who carries the cost.

1  ·  Coal
Benefit: moving away from coal cuts air and water pollution, improving community health and ecosystem stability near plants and mines. Equity issue: the same transition falls hardest on low-income people who rely on coal jobs — who suffer most from coal's effects and are then squeezed again by high energy costs.
2  ·  Renewables
Benefit: less air and water pollution, better public health and productivity, wider energy access, jobs and savings. Equity issue: renewable subsidies and programmes are often inaccessible to poorer communities, particularly those who do not own their homes, deepening the inequality the transition was meant to relieve.
3  ·  Oil
Benefit: reduced dependence lowers the likelihood of spills, protects marine and coastal ecosystems, and improves national security by cutting the military cost of protecting oil resources. Equity issue: ending industry protections costs jobs, and displaced workers need new pathways or the disparity widens.
4  ·  Nuclear
Benefit: stronger safety measures reduce exposure to radiation from meltdowns and hazardous waste, protecting uranium miners and surrounding communities. Equity issue: plants, mines and waste sites are frequently sited in low-income areas whose residents lack the resources to press for stricter regulation.
5  ·  Natural gas
Benefit: moving away improves water security and quality by reducing contamination, and protects wildlife and biodiversity. Equity issue: extraction and burning disproportionately affect poor communities and communities of colour, who often lack the political power to demand better protection.
6  ·  New zero-carbon technologies
Benefit: new job opportunities and applications beyond climate mitigation, potentially driving innovation in other sectors. Equity issue: unknown risks and consequences, particularly for the communities where they are deployed, which can deepen existing inequalities if not carefully managed.

Details

On price and efficiency, the cost lands on the households least able to move.

Considerations seven to twelve.

1  ·  Bioenergy
Benefit: sustainable production can free land for food, improve water and air quality and protect habitats. Equity issue: expansion can drive deforestation and shift agricultural markets, harming small-scale farmers and indigenous communities who depend on the land.
2  ·  Carbon pricing
Benefit: better air quality, healthcare savings, higher worker productivity, renewables made more competitive, and potential funding for social programmes. Equity issue: higher energy costs fall disproportionately on low-income households, and loopholes or exemptions can hollow the policy out.
3  ·  Energy efficiency in transport
Benefit: lower energy costs, less congestion and noise, more physical activity through cycling and walking. Equity issue: high-quality pedestrian and cycling infrastructure is concentrated in wealthier, predominantly white communities, leaving lower-income and minority areas with fewer safe options.
4  ·  Electrification of transport
Benefit: jobs in EV manufacturing and sales, better air quality at source, lower fuel costs. Equity issue: electric vehicles are not affordable or accessible to everyone, and lithium and copper mining for batteries severely harms ecosystems and the communities depending on them, often in less wealthy regions.
5  ·  Energy efficiency in buildings and industry
Benefit: lower demand and cost, better indoor air quality, jobs in weatherisation and retrofitting. Equity issue: the high upfront cost excludes low-income renters, creating a split between property owners who can invest and renters who cannot.
6  ·  Electrification of buildings and industry
Benefit: less noise pollution from engines and generators, lower energy costs, better indoor and outdoor air. Equity issue: switching costs are a barrier in low-income and rural areas, and household air pollution is unevenly distributed within and across countries.

Details

On land, people and offsets, the risk falls on those with least standing to refuse.

Considerations thirteen to eighteen. See Chikofsky et al. (2024) for the complete write-ups.

1  ·  Population
Benefit: voluntary, accessible family planning, reproductive services and education reduce global consumption and improve quality of life for women. Equity issue: such policies must be voluntary and accessible; coercive or poorly implemented ones infringe human rights and fall hardest on marginalised communities.
2  ·  Economic growth
Benefit: shifting from material consumption toward alternative measures of well-being, such as gross national happiness, aligns economic policy with environmental and social goals. Equity issue: recent decades concentrated the gains among the wealthiest, so policies must be tailored to local and regional circumstance.
3  ·  Deforestation
Benefit: preventing it reduces soil erosion, protects biodiversity, safeguards ecosystems and food sources, and supports small-scale gathering and sustainable forestry livelihoods. Equity issue: policies must engage local and indigenous stakeholders, or preservation restricts access to resources those communities have managed sustainably for generations.
4  ·  Methane and other emissions
Benefit: protects water quality and marine habitats, and plant-based diets are healthier for people and ecosystems alike. Equity issue: such policies can conflict with cultural values attached to foods, reduce food security, and threaten local economies built on industrial agriculture.
5  ·  Afforestation
Benefit: jobs in planting and care, restoration of deforested areas, returning plant and animal habitat, carbon sequestration and local economic development. Equity issue: large-scale projects can compromise historic land access for local and indigenous communities, and monocultures harm biodiversity and the livelihoods depending on it.
6  ·  Technological carbon removal
Benefit: nature-based approaches such as agricultural soil sequestration can improve smallholder and farmer profits while capturing carbon. Equity issue: most removal technologies are early-stage and unproven at scale, and their unknown risks would fall on the communities where they are deployed.

Implications

A professor who teaches sustainability and drives to campus has lost the argument already.

The responsibility runs in both directions, and the article is unsparing about both.

What undermines the teaching
✗Professors content with tenure and outdated syllabi, who have long stopped learning
✗Teaching sustainability from a textbook written decades ago
✗Emphasising emissions reduction while driving a fossil-fuelled car onto campus
✗Students who carry a reusable bottle, post about climate change, and stop there
✗Treating sustainability as a checklist of good behaviours rather than a way of life
What the article asks instead
✓Educators as lifelong learners — in engineering schools, first-semester material is often outdated by the fifth
✓Faculty kept awake by the success of their students, not by tenure and accolades
✓Sustainability professors who cycle twenty to thirty miles to campus, embodying what they teach
✓Students who ask uncomfortable questions and challenge both outdated norms and personal habits
✓Sustainability as the currency of every decision, from transport to energy consumption

Conclusion

The allure of a single lever is strong and the arithmetic does not support it.

Where a classroom failure and an equity cheat sheet meet.

education should not be a static transfer of information but a dynamic, collaborative journey of discovery and action

— Relearning Sustainability, 2 September 2024
The correction is structural, not motivational
Meaningful climate action requires a multifaceted approach. Embracing multisolving principles while addressing equity concerns makes solutions that are effective at reducing temperature and also fair and beneficial across communities — a holistic approach the article calls essential for just outcomes.
Neither side gets to sit this out
Both educators and students must recognise the transient nature of knowledge and treat the pursuit of learning — and of living sustainably — as a shared responsibility, with the values taught consistently demonstrated.
And the students in that room are the point
Despite the gaps, the article insists on what it saw: people at the beginning of a journey that could make them the leaders we need, given the right guidance and a commitment to lifelong learning.
