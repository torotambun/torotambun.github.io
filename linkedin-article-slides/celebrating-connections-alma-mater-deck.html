Celebrating Connections: How Our Bonds with Caring People Keep Us Tied to Our Alma Mater

What binds a person to an institution is not its prestige but an accumulated series of exchanges with particular people. An application redirected by weekend phone calls, a culture inherited from a predecessor, and a run of pandemic webinars are the exchanges; the tie that survives them is to two names, not to a brand.

Dr. Toronata Tambun

Opening

I applied to one programme and was redirected to another by phone, on weekends, after hours.

Five on-campus executive courses between 2016 and 2018, then an application to the Advanced Management Program in 2018 that did not go where it was aimed.

The article opens on the ordinary reason anyone applies: "I was initially drawn by the reputation of the institution and the promise of growth through its rigorous programs." The goal was AMP, "a path I believed aligned with my professional aspirations."

What happened instead is described without complaint: "in a turn of events that I would later recognize as pivotal, the admissions committee saw fit to redirect me to the General Management Program (GMP)." The detail that carries the piece is not the decision but its delivery — "This decision, conveyed through a series of personal phone calls on weekends, after regular working hours, marked the beginning of an experience that would shape not just my career, but my understanding of leadership and community."

Weekends and after hours are the whole observation. A redirection is an administrative outcome; a redirection delivered in person, repeatedly, on someone's own time, is an exchange.

The Turn

I came to recognise the redirection as the pivot, not the rejection.

The article is careful about what it can and cannot attribute, and the care is worth preserving.

The person on the calls was Ani Kharajian, who had recently taken over the organisation of GMP. Vicki Good had already moved to managing AMP. On what part Vickie played, the article stops short of a claim: she "might have played a role behind the scenes in the decision to shift my application from AMP to GMP."

That hedge is left standing here. What the article does assert is different and does not depend on it: "Though Vickie was not on the other end of the phone, her influence was felt through the culture she had cultivated within the GMP team, which Ani inherited." The claim is about a culture that outlasted its author's posting, not about who signed off on a file.

The hedge is the record

The article says she might have played a role, and the deck says no more than that. Whether Vickie Good influenced the decision is not established by the piece, and the argument does not need it to be — the inherited culture is the evidence, and that is observed rather than inferred.

Field Notes

Ani led the calls; Vickie's culture led Ani.

Two people, one still in post and one already moved on, and the article credits both for the same thing.

01
On the phone
Ani Kharajian
She had recently taken over GMP when the calls were made. The article names what she practised: "Ani's approach to leadership was a powerful demonstration of relational leadership, a style that values relationships and emotional connections within leadership." And it names the combination that made it land: "She led with empathy, understanding the challenges that come with entering a program like GMP." Then — "This empathy was paired with a decisiveness that made me feel valued, not just as a participant in the program, but as a person." She has since moved to the Owner/President Management programme and, the article records, remains a beloved figure in the GMP community.
02
Already elsewhere
Vickie Good
She had transitioned to managing AMP before the redirection, yet the article treats her as still constitutive of GMP: her "previous deep involvement with GMP left a lasting imprint, making her an enduring part of the GMP family, even as she moved on to AMP." The culture Ani inherited is described as reflecting "both her own leadership style and the lasting impact of Vickie's earlier work with the team." What is being credited is a handover that held — the rarer thing, and the harder one to see from inside a programme.

The Test

Then the pandemic came, and the institution kept teaching when there was no programme left to run.

The 2019 cohort finished; the exchanges did not stop, which is what turns the account from gratitude into evidence.

The support "extended beyond the program itself, becoming especially crucial during the global upheaval of the COVID-19 pandemic." Ani "took proactive steps to ensure that we, the alumni, were not left to face these challenges alone." Through a series of webinars, HBS provided ongoing learning that was "not just about professional development, but about building adaptive resilience in the face of unprecedented uncertainty."

The article's assessment of them is unqualified: "These webinars were a lifeline, reinforcing the idea of lifelong learning and reminding us that education is not confined to the walls of a classroom or the pages of a textbook." For the author personally they "were more than just an extension of my academic journey; they were a source of comfort and strength, a reminder that I was part of a community that cared deeply about my well-being."

This is the part of the record that does the analytical work. An institution serving a paying cohort is doing its job; an institution serving alumni during a crisis, with nothing to sell, is demonstrating what it is.

Framework

Social exchange theory names it: trust accumulates through exchanges, not through prestige.

One theoretical frame, applied once, to separate what an institution advertises from what actually attaches a person to it.

The article states the temptation before the correction: "It is easy to be enamored by the prestige of the institution—the accolades, the network, the opportunities it provides." And then the correction — "But as I have come to understand, the true value of HBS lies not just in its global reputation, but in the relationships that are cultivated within it."

The frame is named directly. This perspective, the article says, aligns with "social exchange theory, which posits that relationships are built on a series of exchanges that create trust and mutual commitment over time." The words doing the work are “over time”: the weekend calls, the inherited culture and the pandemic webinars are not three anecdotes but one accumulating series.

What the theory buys the piece is a reason the conclusion is not sentimental. If trust is built by exchange, then an institution's reputation is not what holds an alumnus — it is a claim about exchanges the alumnus has not yet had.

What I Carry

What ties me to HBS is two names, not a brand.

The piece ends where it started, on the people who made the calls.

The relationships I formed, the lessons I learned, and the care I received from Ani and Vickie are what make HBS a place I continue to return to—not just in memory, but in practice

— Celebrating Connections, 16 August 2024
Return in practice, not in memory
The distinction the closing sentence turns on is between nostalgia and continuing conduct — he carries forward "the values and insights that were imparted to me during that transformative time." An alma mater one returns to in practice is one whose way of treating people has been adopted rather than admired.
The programme was the smaller half
"As I look back on my journey through GMP, I realize that it was not just an academic endeavor, but a deeply personal one. It was a journey that challenged me, supported me, and ultimately reshaped my understanding of what it means to lead and to learn."
What it is offered as
The article closes by naming its own genre — "the convergence of personal experience and broader theoretical concepts, a hallmark of autoethnographic writing" — and its subject as "a story of leadership, resilience, and the enduring power of human connection".

Sources

The article carries no reference list. Relational leadership, psychological safety and social exchange theory are named without attribution to any author or work, and none is supplied here. Its material is the author's own record: five on-campus executive courses from 2016 to 2018, an application to AMP in 2018 redirected to GMP, the 2019 programme, and the pandemic webinar series that followed. The two people at its centre are named in the source and named here, both in credit. Whether Vickie Good influenced the redirection is stated in the article as a possibility, not a fact, and is left as one. The source spells her name Vicki at first mention and Vickie thereafter; the deck follows the form the article uses throughout.
