(2 of 2) "If in the unrighteous Mammon you are not Amina (faithful), who will Yatmin (entrust) to you the Amen (true riches)?"

Luke places a teaching on money right after the shrewd steward's parable so no one mistakes admiration for the steward's cleverness as license for dishonesty — and its Aramaic wordplay makes the point inescapable: faithfulness with worldly wealth is the test that decides whether anyone can be trusted with what is truly theirs.

Opening

A society that tolerates corruption is confessing where its real loyalty lies.

Before returning to Bailey's exposition, the piece opens on a live, local register: institutional corruption as advanced disease.

Situation

Many claim to stand for justice and integrity, but when corruption prevails, their true allegiance is revealed — not to principle, but to wealth and power.

Complication

"A cancer so advanced it resembles a Stage 4 or Stage 5. No medicine. No radiology. No chemical treatments." Corruption becomes, in the piece's own words, culture: reflexes, systems, mental models.

Question

If corruption is this entrenched, does the ancient teaching Luke places right after the shrewd steward's parable still have anything to say to it?

Answer

Yes — because that teaching's whole point is that faithfulness with money is the proving ground for trustworthiness, and a society only reforms once it stops tolerating the small betrayals it currently shrugs off.

Framework

Luke places this teaching right after the parable so no one mistakes the steward's cleverness for the point.

Bailey's explanation for why these two passages sit side by side in Luke 16.

Luke anticipated that non-Middle Eastern, Greek-speaking readers might misread the parable of the unjust steward as an endorsement of dishonesty. Placing an unambiguous teaching on honesty with money immediately after it closes that door.

The parable is about relying on the master's unqualified mercy for salvation; the adjoining passage is about integrity and faithfulness in worldly stewardship. Together they hold divine grace and human responsibility in the same frame.

Neither passage cancels the other. The parable does not license dishonesty just because it ends in mercy, and the teaching on money does not turn salvation into a wage earned by good bookkeeping.

Details

The Aramaic root word repeats three times, and the repetition is the argument.

Bailey's close reading of the verse's own wordplay.

TRANSLATED A
Amina — faithful
The root conveying faithfulness, assurance and truth, applied to how a person handles "unrighteous Mammon" — worldly wealth that was never really theirs to begin with.
TRANSLATED B
Yatmin — entrust
The same root, now asking who will *entrust* a person with something greater, once their handling of the lesser has been observed.
TRANSLATED C
Amen — true riches
The reward at stake: not more money, but the deeper spiritual knowledge and eternal blessing that only a demonstrably faithful steward is given.
TRANSLATED D
The test, stated once, plainly
"If you are not faithful in unrighteous Mammon, who will entrust to you the true riches?" Material wealth is a test of character before it is anything else.

Evidence

Two verses close the case: no one is really an owner, and no one serves two masters.

Bailey draws the passage's stewardship logic out to its conclusion.

01
Stewardship, not ownership
"That which is yours, truly yours, is the truth of God"
Material possessions are entrusted, not owned. Misusing what was only ever on loan is what undermines the claim to being trusted with what is genuinely one's own — the knowledge and truth of God.
02
An undivided loyalty
None can serve two masters (Luke 16:13)
The teaching does not permit a divided allegiance between Mammon and God as a stable compromise; it treats the choice as exclusive, the same way the opening's diagnosis of corruption treats a compromised allegiance as already a kind of confession.

Conclusion

Faithfulness in little things is the foundation for trust in the eternal ones.

Bailey's summary, closing both the parable and its companion teaching together.

the parable of the unjust steward teaches reliance on the master's unqualified mercy as the only hope of salvation, while the adjacent text emphasizes the necessity of faithfulness in managing material resources

— (2 of 2) If in the Unrighteous Mammon You Are Not Amina
Two lessons, one message
Luke's careful placement of these two passages invites reflection on both the mercy of God and the responsibility of living faithfully within what God provides.
A local application, not left abstract
The opening's diagnosis of Indonesian corruption is not a detour from the theology — it is the piece's own answer to what happens when a society treats unrighteous Mammon as something other than a test.
The historical horizon
Reversing corruption takes two to three generations—50 to 75 years—because it is not just a policy issue. It is a system-1 mental model, a nation's muscle memory, deeply embedded over time.
