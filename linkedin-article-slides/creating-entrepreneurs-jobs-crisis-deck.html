LinkedIn Article  ·  Policy · Jobs Crisis and the Making of Entrepreneurs  ·  16 July 2020

Creating Entrepreneurs won't just happen by simply providing hand-to-mouth seed funds intended for unemployed precarious people.

The article argues that a jobs crisis calls for social protection and job-retention schemes, and that cash aimed at precarious households cannot be expected to create entrepreneurs. It says entrepreneurs need particular personality traits and an anti-fragile temperament, that these are imprinted in the brain between ages five and twenty-four, and that creating entrepreneurs therefore takes early hands-on exposure and an ecosystem that exists before them. It closes on a meditation by Kagawa that the author says reflects his spiritual view of the unemployed.

Introduction

A jobs crisis calls for social protection, and cash for precarious households cannot be expected to create entrepreneurs.

Situation

The article opens with Covid-19 as a crisis that has moved from health to jobs and is likely to become a social crisis, which it calls no secret. Citing Well-being Indonesia, it says the impacts may increase the vulnerability of the most disadvantaged, among them people in poor health, in poverty or financial insecurity, in overcrowded or unaffordable housing, the socially isolated, low-wage workers and people in non-standard jobs.

Complication

The author has found a country running a conditional cash transfer programme for households earning under 300 US dollars a month for five people, which places recipients in the second income decile, one above the poorest of the poor and one below peddlers. The programme is good, the article says, but the wish that recipients use the fund to take up a life as entrepreneurs is a bit naive. Nothing in the OECD or World Bank recommendations suggests targeted social protection as a means of creating entrepreneurs.

Question

What should policy do for the unemployed in a jobs crisis, and what does it take to create entrepreneurs?

Answer

The article says social protection and job-retention schemes serve the unemployed and none is framed as a way to create entrepreneurs, while entrepreneurs come from particular traits, early exposure and an ecosystem.

Evidence note

The article does not name the country. It has no reference list beyond one book, cites the vulnerability passage only as Well-being Indonesia, and gives no citation for the OECD and World Bank statements.

Evidence

Documented responses to the jobs crisis are job retention, cash and extended benefits, and none is framed as a way to create entrepreneurs.

The article sets out examples of policy for the people affected and notes that none of them is disguised as an incentive to create entrepreneurs. It prints most of them as passages without a title or date.

CITED A
Who is hit hardest
Young people face a tough job market that could compromise their futures. Women and low-paid workers likely face greater unemployment. Part-time, temporary and self-employed workers, who make up 40 to 50 percent of the workforce in hard-hit sectors, bear the brunt.
CITED B
Hours and incomes
Total hours worked fell ten times faster in the first three months of this crisis than in the first three months of the 2008 financial crisis, in OECD countries with data. In lockdown, top earners were on average 50 percent more likely to work from home than low earners, and low-income workers were twice as likely to stop working completely.
CITED C
A precedent for cash
In the Democratic Republic of Congo, alongside the public health response in Ebola-affected zones, 50 million US dollars was quickly mobilised for a cash-for-work programme to raise community resilience, support the local economy and strengthen social cohesion.
CITED D
Job retention
Schemes let employers cut hours while workers receive support for the hours not worked: Germany's scheme, printed Kurzarbelt, and France's Activité partielle. Others also top up earnings, such as the Netherlands' NOW and Australia's Job Keeper Payment. About 60 million people across the OECD are included in company claims for them.

Gap

Not everyone can turn a crisis into opportunity, because entrepreneurs need particular traits and an anti-fragile temperament.

The article accepts that a crisis brings opportunity, and adds that not all people have what it takes to find it in the real sense of becoming an entrepreneur.

01
Minimum traits
Four personality traits
Entrepreneurs have minimum traits: high openness, high conscientiousness, low agreeableness and very low neuroticism.
02
Temperament
Anti-fragile
The article adds the anti-fragile, a term it credits to Nassim Nicolas Thaleb, as printed, and to Bill Aulet for propagating it. It says the term encompasses resilience and resistance.
03
The mix
Pirate and navy seal
Entrepreneurs must be creative enough like a pirate but super disciplined like a navy seal.

Framework

Passion is imprinted by neuron pruning and myelination between ages five and twenty-four, so entrepreneurs must be created early.

The article says passion is not given or handed down from heaven but physically imprinted in the brain through a process it calls firing neurons pruning. The stages are the article's own account and carry no source.

Age 5
The peak of connections
Firing neurons reach a peak of trillions of connections and stop multiplying.
Ages 5 to 17
Pruning by exposure
Instead of growing, the neurons are pruned according to the exposures a child obtains.
Ages 17 to 24
Myelination
The myelination process continues, and the mental model formed from 5 to 24 is carried subconsciously afterwards.
After 24
Limited neuroplasticity
Neuroplasticity remains possible, but only in the part of the brain made of the firing neurons that remain after 24. Under pressure, which is normal for entrepreneurs, a person lapses to the imprinted mental model, whatever the training or capital.
Evidence note

The sentence on brains not exposed to entrepreneurship is printed with a double negative. This deck reads it as meaning it is very unlikely that such a person becomes an entrepreneur.

Implications

Support workers with job schemes, and create entrepreneurs through early hands-on exposure and an ecosystem that exists before them.

The article borrows the saying that it takes a village to raise a child, and says it takes an ecosystem to create entrepreneurs.

1
Help precarious workers with job schemes, without expecting entrepreneurs
The article's examples of job retention, cash and extended benefits are for jobs and well-being, and it says none is disguised as an incentive to create entrepreneurs.
2
Expose children to entrepreneurship early, through hands-on schooling
The creation of entrepreneurs starts at a very early age and takes years of effort to plant the mental model. Early schooling can provide the exposures through rigorous training and a process-based curriculum, with far more hands-on experience and far less one-way lecturing.
3
Build the innovation and entrepreneurial ecosystem first
The ecosystem must pre-exist, to bear the future entrepreneurs and to propel them afterwards. The article adds that this kind of person does not need the targeted social fund scheme.
