(2 of 5): The Typology of The Beatitudes

Matthew stages Jesus delivering the Beatitudes from a mountain because he is deliberately painting Jesus as a new Moses giving a new law — and each Beatitude, read against its Torah counterpart, turns out to expand an existing value rather than replace it, from outward observance toward the heart.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

Why does Matthew put Jesus on a mountain to deliver this teaching?

The late Rev. Dr. John Shelby Spong argued the New Testament was written for audiences steeped in Hebrew Scripture — and the Beatitudes' own staging is the clearest evidence of it.

Situation

Matthew 5 opens with Jesus going up a mountain to teach, echoing the most consequential mountain scene in the Hebrew Bible: Moses receiving the Law at Sinai.

Complication

If that echo is deliberate, the Beatitudes are not simply wise sayings; they are staged as a new covenant proclamation — which raises the question of what, specifically, they are updating.

Question

Do the Beatitudes replace the Torah's values, or do they extend them?

Answer

Each Beatitude has a real Torah counterpart, and the pattern across all eight is the same: the value survives, but its center of gravity shifts from outward behavior to inner disposition.

Framework

Matthew stages Jesus as a new Moses, giving a new law.

Spong's own framing of Matthew's editorial project.

Certainly, if Jesus was to be the new Moses, he must give a new law. So Matthew, in order to complete his new Moses portrait, gathered a great collection of the teachings of Jesus.

— John Shelby Spong, This Hebrew Lord (1974)
A parallel covenant
"The beatitudes were set as universal principles of the new covenant to parallel the Ten Commandments—universal principles of the old covenant."
From external behavior to inner disposition
Rather than commandments focused on outward acts, Jesus speaks to the heart and character — meekness, mercy, purity, peacemaking — marking a shift toward the spiritual aspects of God's kingdom.
Not a replacement, an expansion
The Beatitudes do not mirror specific Torah commandments one-for-one; they expand the underlying values that already run through it.

Details

Each of the eight Beatitudes has a real Torah counterpart.

A representative sample of the piece's eight verse-by-verse pairings.

PAIRED A
Poor in spirit ↔ Deuteronomy 8:3
Deuteronomy 8:3: "man does not live on bread alone but on every word that comes from the mouth of the Lord" — humility and dependence on God, not material sufficiency.
PAIRED B
Those who mourn ↔ Exodus 3:7
God's own words to Moses in Exodus 3:7 — "I have indeed seen the misery of my people in Egypt" — ground the promise of comfort in God's established character, not a new claim.
PAIRED C
The meek ↔ Numbers 12:3
Moses himself is described as "very humble, more so than anyone else on the face of the earth" — the Beatitude's promise of inheriting the earth echoes the land-inheritance covenant made to Israel.
PAIRED D
The merciful ↔ Leviticus 19:18 and Exodus 34:6
Leviticus 19:18's "Love your neighbor as yourself" sits beside God's own self-description in Exodus 34:6 — "the compassionate and gracious God, slow to anger, abounding in love and faithfulness" — mercy modeled on divine character, not invented.

Evidence

The literary technique this uses has a name — several names, depending on the tradition.

The appendix distinguishes typology from its cousins.

Typology, in biblical studies, is the technique where earlier figures or events (types) are read as foreshadowing later ones (antitypes) — Jesus as the new Moses, the new Adam, the Passover Lamb. Intertextuality is the broader literary-theory version of the same move: later texts deliberately referencing earlier ones to build meaning.

Jewish interpretive tradition has its own related forms: Midrash expands and reinterprets biblical text for new generations; Pesher reads ancient texts as direct prophecy about the interpreter's own community, as at Qumran; Rewritten Bible retells biblical stories with expansions to fit new theological needs, as in the Book of Jubilees.

Naming the technique matters because it changes what kind of claim the parallel is making — not that the Beatitudes quote the Torah, but that they stand in a deliberate line of interpretive descent from it.

Conclusion

Not a replacement of the Torah's values — a continuation and expansion of them.

The piece's closing statement on what the parallel ultimately shows.

This connection highlights how the Beatitudes act as a continuation and expansion of the foundational principles laid out in the Torah, positioning Jesus as a teacher who fulfills and deepens the moral and spiritual essence of the law.

— (2 of 5): The Typology of The Beatitudes
Humility, justice, mercy, purity, peace, devotion
These six values run through both the Torah and the Beatitudes; what changes across them is where the emphasis falls — from outward observance to heart-centered faith.
A staged proclamation, not a casual sermon
Matthew's mountain setting is doing deliberate theological work, not just narrative color.
