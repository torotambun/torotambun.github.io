(3 of 4) Balancing Mental Health Support and Anti-Fragility: Lessons from Biblical Figures

Six biblical figures faced crises with no modern support system available to them, and five of six reached a form of transcendent resolution — the sixth, Jeremiah, did not, a deliberate reminder that not all struggle resolves cleanly.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Support for genuine suffering must remain a priority — and so must the strength that emerges from confronting hardship directly.

The conversation around mental health has rightly gained prominence, but any shift in societal focus needs balance.

Support for trauma, clinical depression, and other serious conditions must remain a priority, and those who face these struggles deserve empathy and professional care. Yet it's essential to balance this support with encouragement toward growth and resilience. Overemphasizing mental health risks creating an environment where challenges are seen solely as crises, rather than as opportunities for growth.

Biblical figures like Jesus, Moses, and Job teach us that adversity can shape resilience. Had the world shielded them from their struggles, their legacies might not have endured.

Evidence

Six figures, six crises, no external support available to any of them.

Each faced a moment that, today, might be read as a mental health emergency requiring intervention.

1  ·  Jesus — facing the weight alone
"My soul is overwhelmed with sorrow to the point of death" (Matthew 26:38), sweating "like drops of blood" (Luke 22:44) in Gethsemane. Roman authorities viewed him as a threat, religious leaders sought to undermine him — he had no external support or comfort. He found strength through prayer and surrender: "Yet not as I will, but as you will" (Matthew 26:39).
2  ·  Moses — strength through isolation
After fleeing Egypt, he spent 40 years in the desert as a shepherd, with no sympathetic ears and no comforting words. This solitude became a period of deep reflection that equipped him to accept the divine calling at the burning bush.
3  ·  Elijah — despair in the wilderness
After his victory over the prophets of Baal, threatened by Queen Jezebel, he fled utterly alone: "I have had enough, Lord. Take my life" (1 Kings 19:4). He found strength not from external comfort but from a still, small voice.
4  ·  David — growth through struggle
Betrayed, pursued by Saul, confronted by his own son's rebellion, David had no option to retreat from leadership. "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1) — he found solace in pouring his anguish into the Psalms.
5  ·  Job — endurance without support
Having lost his children, wealth and health, sitting in ashes with his friends offering only judgment, Job lamented: "Why did I not perish at birth?" (Job 3:11). He wrestled with his anguish essentially alone.
6  ·  Jeremiah — loneliness and an uncertain end
The "weeping prophet" was persecuted, thrown into a cistern, and mocked for a message no one wanted to hear: "Cursed be the day I was born!" (Jeremiah 20:14). He turned inward for strength — but his story, unlike the other five, ends without a clear resolution.

Details

A hypothetical dialogue stages the internal argument each of them had to win against doubt.

The Ego repeatedly offers Jesus an easier way out; each time, his answer is a form of surrender to purpose.

"Betrayal, loneliness—they are nothing new to me. I have faced rejection before, and yet, I have always found the strength to continue. This time will be no different." This staged exchange between Jesus and an "Ego" voice of doubt and temptation makes visible a mental health dilemma common to all six figures: the temptation to step back when support feels absent and effort seems unappreciated, met each time by a choice to find strength in purpose rather than in the support of others.

Implications

Five reach a form of transcendence; one does not — and that asymmetry is the point.

Reached transcendent resolution
✗Elijah — taken up in a whirlwind by chariots of fire (2 Kings 2:11)
✗Moses — dies on Mount Nebo having seen the Promised Land (Deuteronomy 34:5-6)
✗Jesus — resurrection and ascension (Acts 1:9)
✗Job — fortunes restored twofold, dies "old and full of days" (Job 42:10-17)
✗David — throne passed to Solomon, a legacy through the Psalms (1 Kings 2:1-10)
No clear resolution recorded
✓Jeremiah — taken against his will to Egypt (Jeremiah 43:6-7), with no clear record of his death
✓A life of enduring struggle rather than triumph, valuable without an outward-success ending

Conclusion

The goal is not to romanticize hardship but to build a society that values both compassion and inner strength.

Resilience is often carved through adversity, not around it.

The objective is not to downplay the significance of mental health support or to romanticize the experience of enduring hardship. Rather, the focus is on building a society that recognizes the dual value of compassion and inner strength — a culture where the deepest struggles are met with care, but where perseverance is celebrated and resilience is cultivated.
