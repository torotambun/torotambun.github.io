(5 of 5) Reference Lakon: Mandaraka Without Wind - The Reign of Prabu Jarasandha

The series closes as parable rather than argument. A king who waited twenty years inherits a throne already hollow, and loses it without a blow being struck — because what he spent those years doing was watching, not learning. The thing that cannot be returned to here is not childhood but discernment, and the lakon's claim is that its loss is never announced.

Opening

The screen is set up to cast truth rather than shadows, and the fall it shows makes no sound.

"Wayang is not just shadow play. It is a keeper of memory, a lamp that casts light through parable and echoes."

"It tells us that power, if not bound by discernment, will turn on itself."

"In the stories of old and in the stories we live now, the fall never comes with thunder. It comes when no one listens, no one believes, and no one follows."

The dalang opens in the same register: "Wayang is not merely a story—it is a mirror. Tonight, the screen shall not only cast shadows, but truth." And he names the three figures before the gamelan begins — "Of a king who waited too long. Of a prince who moved without stepping. Of a throne that crumbled without a sound."

How the transfer happened

"The throne of Mandaraka had been passed carefully, deliberately—like a ceremonial torch. No conquest, no insurrection. Only a transition. Not quite victory. Not quite surrender. Merely the illusion of continuity."

The King

Twenty years of waiting bought a throne and no capacity to sit on it.

"Prabu Jarasandha had ascended the throne not through valor or spiritual vision, but through patience."

OBSERVED A
What the waiting was spent on
"Twenty years of waiting, of watching, of longing—only to finally sit upon a throne that was already hollowed from within." "He had spent his years waiting, not seeking knowledge".
OBSERVED B
How he had risen in the first place
"Even in his younger days, Prabu Jarasandha had risen not through kawicaksanan, but through proximity—lifted by closeness rather than trials of virtue." "he was known for placing the wrong man in the wrong task at the wrong moment. That flaw, once whispered behind folded fans, returned in full now that he wore the crown."
OBSERVED C
Who he filled the palace with
"He ruled not from wisdom, but from command." He filled his keraton "not with pinisepuh, sages, or patih of good judgment, but with bala prajurit—loyal warriors turned into caretakers of affairs they did not understand." "Around him now were not loyal abdi dalem, but penjilat and penyongkong, who echoed only what pleased him."
OBSERVED D
What listeners began to hear
"And though his voice still rumbled through the pendhapa, those who listened heard something broken: phrases disjointed, meanings lost. He spoke with the thunder of old, but not its clarity." "the king's mind, once stubborn but coherent, had begun to drift like smoke after fire." "He rewarded those who bowed low and turned away from those who spoke plainly."

The Erosion

The barns emptied by habit, and the king answered by speaking.

"Across Mandaraka, the lumbung-lumbung kerajaan that once overflowed with rice now stood quiet, their gates open but their barns bare."

"The harvests were not poor, but the hands meant to guard the grain had turned inward—stealing by habit, not hunger." "Pajeg was demanded not once, but twice, and what reached the royal coffers was but the echo of what had been taken."

Even the Patih "had become a shadow. His name was spoken with a smile that hid bitterness, for it was said that under his watch, the wolves wore royal cloth."

"And still, Prabu Jarasandha did not act. He spoke. Often." "But those closest could see: his words circled, never landed. He spoke of restoring order, yet no one was dismissed. He called for honesty, yet never asked where the grain had gone. The theft continued—not in secret, but in habit."

Beyond the borders the same story arrived by trade. One messenger reported: "The Prabu speaks as if Mandaraka is rich, but even the water jars in the guest hall were dry."

The Turns

The prince rose by standing still while the king eroded in motion.

Raden Jayamurti is no more qualified than Jarasandha, and that turns out not to matter.

What Jayamurti did not have
✗"Jayamurti never sought knowledge."
✗Sacred chronicles and old teachings "often returned unread, still sealed, or with only the first page disturbed"
✗"He listened politely to the empus who recited history, but his eyes wandered, and his mind drifted elsewhere."
✗"What he knew, he knew through whispers, through those who sat close to him and thought for him."
✗"He was no warrior of legend, no sage of council. But that did not matter."
What rose anyway
✓"His strength was not in judgment, but in stillness."
✓When protests were crushed "it was Jayamurti’s envoys who arrived with beras and soft words"
✓"He did not rise by force, but by remaining still—steady, calm, untouched by the noise that wore down his king."
✓Behind him stood "his rama, the former Prabu, and his father’s old council" — those "who once guided a kingdom and now whispered into the ears of one who said little and promised even less"
✓"Jarasandha, too full of his own echo, did not see how Jayamurti had begun to rise—not by stepping forward, but simply by standing still while the old king eroded."

The Scene

The break arrives as silence, and one bow ends a reign.

"The murmur grew not from hatred—but from fatigue. Not rebellion—but the weight of being ruled by a voice that no longer touched the ground."

Perang Kembang
The whispers find words
"In the barak, warriors muttered, “The old prabu would have acted.”" "In noble halls, bangsawan exchanged glances: “Perhaps the time has come.”" And still "Jarasandha believed he still held the reins."
The test
A probe at the border, unanswered
"A skirmish broke at the eastern border. Not a full invasion, just a test. A probe. The kind of thing a firm ruler would answer swiftly." "But Jarasandha hesitated."
The pledge
One warrior bows to the wrong man
"The crack did not come with a blow. It came as silence." A single warrior "known by rank but not by voice" turned "not to Jarasandha, but to Jayamurti" and lowered his head. "The silence that followed was heavier than war."
Sanggar Pamujan
The reckoning, and the clown's question
"No one answered." "Jayamurti did not gloat. He did not flinch. He simply bowed." Then Semar, "the palace clown and truth-teller", let slip a dry laugh: "Ah, Prabu Jarasandha… the warrior who thought himself unmoved. Has he ever heard how his namesake fell—raging, but already forgotten?"
The exchange
Six words settle it
"Did you ever mean to betray me?" "Jayamurti tilted his head." "I never had to."

What It Revealed

The throne was not taken; it stopped waiting.

"Would the old king step down? Maybe: not because he was forced, but because he had already fallen."

Not by blow, but by erosion. The throne no longer waited for him. It had already chosen its next occupant.

— (5 of 5) Mandaraka Without Wind
What the ending refuses to settle
The final scene is written as three questions, not three outcomes. "Would then Jayamurti rise? It was not his footsteps that mattered. It was the quiet movement behind him." And whether the father's hands "still rest quietly on the back of the throne" — "The boy may have ascended, but the realm might still be guided by a voice much older, much calmer, and far more deliberate."
The dalang's closing
"The puppets return to their box. But the questions remain in the air. A king who ruled without seeing, a prince who rose without reaching". "In Mandaraka, the voices faded—but echoes do not die.The rice barn empty, the courtyards silent—yet the throne… still there".
Where the series ends
"Perhaps in another kingdom, another night, another name…The same shadow waits." The instalment makes no claim beyond that, and names no kingdom but Mandaraka.

The form
[1]The article is written as a wayang lakon in nine scenes with a prologue and epilogue, following the conventional sequence: Talu, Kadaton, Paseban Jawi, Perang Ampyak, Sabrangan, Gara-Gara, Perang Kembang, Sanggar Pamujan and Sampak or Kekayon.
[2]The gamelan pieces named in the overture are Gending Cucurbawuk, Gending Rebab, Ladrang Srikaton, Srepegan and Sampak.
[3]Semar, the palace clown and truth-teller, appears in the reckoning scene. He is a punakawan of Javanese wayang, not of the Sanskrit epics.
[4]Semar's line about the king's namesake refers to Jarasandha of the Mahabharata. The article makes the allusion and does not explain it, and no account of that death has been supplied here.

Notes on sources
[10]Mandaraka is a kingdom of the Javanese wayang repertoire, and Prabu Jarasandha and Raden Jayamurti are named as its king and prince here. Whether this lakon is drawn from an existing repertoire or written for the series is not stated in the article.
[11]The article opens by saying the parable speaks to the stories of old and the stories we live now, and closes on another kingdom, another night, another name. It identifies no person, party, office or country beyond Mandaraka, and this deck identifies none either. Any present-day reading is the reader's, and the text does not license one.
[12]The article carries a creation timestamp of 21 March 2025 and a publication timestamp of 26 March — drafted five days before the series it closes was published, and four before the first instalment appeared.
[13]Javanese and Indonesian terms are left untranslated in the article and are reproduced here as it has them: kawicaksanan, keraton, pinisepuh, patih, bala prajurit, abdi dalem, penjilat, penyongkong, pendhapa, lumbung, pajeg, dusun, padagang, pujangga, serat, empu, rakyat, beras, bangsawan, barak, pasar, tumenggung, adipati, tabib, resi, rama, kawi.
