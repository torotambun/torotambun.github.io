(5 of 7) Teaching Note: Calculations Behind VC Earnings - Revealing the Pandora Box

However eFishery's fraud resolves, Northstar had already banked an estimated $2.8 million to $5.6 million in guaranteed management fees on its stake — income that arrives regardless of whether the startup succeeds, fails, or is caught faking its revenue. Run the same fund math across five exit scenarios and the pattern holds at every valuation: a venture fund's own economics are decoupled from the portfolio company's fate, which is exactly why the fraud that devastated eFishery never put Northstar's own returns at real risk.

Introduction

A venture fund earns money on a fixed schedule, whether or not the startup ever succeeds.

The class question that opens the note: "If you were managing a $100M venture fund, how would you decide how much to invest in each startup?"

Situation

A VC fund deducts management fees and expenses before a dollar reaches any startup, then splits what remains between initial checks and reserves for follow-on rounds.

Complication

Northstar Group invested in eFishery across three rounds — Series B (2020), Series C (2022), Series D (2023) — and by the time eFishery's revenue-inflation fraud surfaced, Northstar's ownership had been diluted to an estimated 3–5%.

Question

Does a fraud that destroys a startup's value also destroy its lead investor's return?

Answer

No. "They already made $5.6M in management fees, meaning they still walk away with a profit" — even in the worst modeled scenario, a fire-sale exit below the original investment.

Framework

Four deductions turn a headline fund size into a much smaller number that actually reaches founders.

The mechanics, applied to Northstar's own $140 million fund (Northstar Ventures I, closed December 2023).

1  ·  Management fees come off the top, every year, regardless of performance
2% of $140M annually, over a 10-year fund life, is $28M in total fees — separate from and unaffected by any single portfolio company's outcome.
2  ·  Fund expenses take a further bite
Legal, admin and audit costs are estimated at roughly $14M over the fund's life, bringing total deductions to $42M before any capital reaches a startup.
3  ·  What's left is split between initial checks and follow-on reserves
Investable capital of $98M is typically divided roughly evenly — about $49M for initial investments, $49M reserved for follow-on rounds in companies that keep raising.
4  ·  The average check size follows from the portfolio size, not the other way around
Spread across an assumed 15 startups, the average initial check works out to roughly $3.27M per company — a number the fund manager sets by deciding how many bets to place, not by any single company's merits.

Evidence

Northstar's fees on the eFishery position alone were an estimated $2.8 to $5.6 million, before any exit.

Applying the fee framework specifically to Northstar's stake in eFishery.

ESTIMATE A
The guaranteed income
"Estimated Management Fees Earned from eFishery Investment Alone: $2.8M - $5.6M over 10 years." These fees are collected on committed capital, independent of eFishery's fraud or its ultimate exit value.
ESTIMATE B
The contingent income
Carried interest — Northstar's 20% share of investment profit — depends entirely on exit value, and is what the fraud actually put at risk. On an assumed $30M total investment, the note calculates profit and carried interest separately at each exit scenario.
What is estimate, not disclosed fact

"All investment amounts by Northstar Group are estimates based on typical VC funding structures and publicly available information" Exact figures for Northstar's Series B, C and D checks were not publicly disclosed; the note assumes $10M per round for illustration.

Evidence

Five exit scenarios, and the fee floor never disappears.

What happens to Northstar's position under different outcomes for eFishery, at an assumed 5% stake.

1  ·  Worst case — $500M fire sale
Northstar's stake is worth $25M against a $30M investment — a $5M loss on equity. "BUT: They already made $5.6M in management fees, meaning they still walk away with a profit!" Total earnings: $5.6M, guaranteed, no carried interest.
2  ·  Current valuation — $1.26B, staying private
Stake worth $63M; a $33M profit if sold privately; carried interest of $6.6M. Total earnings: roughly $12.2M.
3  ·  Moderate IPO — $2B on the Indonesia Stock Exchange
Stake worth $100M; a $70M profit; carried interest of $14M. Total earnings: $19.6M.
4  ·  High IPO — $5B NASDAQ listing
Stake worth $250M; a $220M profit; carried interest of $44M. Total earnings: $49.6M.
5  ·  Best case — $10B global acquisition or IPO
Stake worth $500M; a $470M profit; carried interest of $94M. Total earnings: $99.6M — described in the note as "a career-defining deal for Northstar."

Conclusion

VCs rarely lose money—they collect fees regardless.

The note's own summary of what the five scenarios prove.

That's why VCs rarely "lose" money—they collect fees regardless.

— Section 9, Aftermath — The Big Lessons About VC Exits
The incentive the fee structure protects
"Management fees make VCs profitable even in failure—this aligns incentives between fund managers and investors" in one direction, but decouples the fund manager's own income from the portfolio company's conduct in the other.
What founders should take from it
"Founders should understand dilution-early investors lose ownership as startups scale." and the same math that protects a VC's downside also shapes how much pressure a founder feels to keep the valuation story intact.
Where this sits in the fraud-bias series
