Easter through the lens of disruptive innovation

Read through Christensen in his fuller form rather than the slogan, Good Friday and Easter satisfy all four indicators of an empowering innovation: complexity became simpler, the unaffordable became free at the point of reception, the capital cost was relocated to the source, and a new field of work was created. Which makes the test civic, not private — whether the person holding power opens the way or becomes another barrier.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

The unusual reading works only if Christensen is taken in his fuller form.

"The idea of reading Good Friday and Easter through Clayton Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation may sound unusual. It is unusual."

Situation

Christensen's theory is one of the most widely deployed frameworks in management, and one of the most widely flattened. Many people hear only that disruption means something dramatic, or something that makes an expensive product cheaper and a complicated product simpler.

Complication

That is part of the story, but not the whole of it. The narrow textbook version cannot carry a theological reading, because it describes price and simplicity without describing who absorbs the cost of opening access.

Question

What does the framework look like when the later economic-engine distinction — empowering, sustaining and efficiency innovations — is put back into it?

Answer

A four-part logic that does carry the reading: simplification, affordability, capital absorption, and the creation of new value and work. Each of the four then has something specific to test against Good Friday and Easter.

The Framework

Disruption is not a cheaper product but a structural shift in access.

In Christensen's own teaching tradition, disruption explains what happens when an older architecture serves people through costly layers, overshoots what many can use, and leaves large groups of nonconsumers outside.

1  ·  Simplification
What had required specialized knowledge, institutional layers or procedural complexity becomes more direct and easier to use. "This matters because disruption is not merely about adding performance. It is about changing the path of entry."
2  ·  Affordability
Disruption expands because people previously excluded can now participate. The key is not only lower price in a narrow sense, but a new level of accessibility for people who lacked the money, time, skill or institutional standing to take part. That is why “affordable” should be read broadly as accessible at the point of use.
3  ·  Capital absorption
"New access does not emerge by magic." Empowering innovations require investment; they use capital to build the structures that let nonconsumers participate. The economic engine diagrams contrast "empowering innovations, which use capital and create jobs, with efficiency innovations, which free capital and eliminate jobs."
4  ·  New value and work
Genuine disruption does not stop at convenience. It creates new organized activity — bringing new users into participation, generating roles, institutions and habits, and often setting off wider social consequence. This is why the later literature connects market-creating innovation to prosperity creation rather than mere cost reduction.
The framework stated simply

"A true disruption is not just a better version of the old thing." It is a restructuring of access in which the complex becomes simpler, the inaccessible becomes affordable, serious capital is committed to make that opening possible, and the result creates new value, work and public consequence.

Criterion One and Two

The curtain tore, and layered access became direct.

Before the crucifixion, access to God was mediated through an older religious architecture: temple, priesthood, sacrifice, ritual order and sacred boundary.

That structure was not absurd. It reflected the seriousness of holiness and the seriousness of sin. Yet it was unmistakably layered. Access was real, but it came through mediation.

"In the Gospel account, the curtain of the temple is torn at the death of Christ. That detail is not ornamental. It signifies a structural shift in access." "What had been layered becomes direct." In Christensen's terms, the old complexity gives way to a simpler architecture of access.

The second criterion follows, and it needs stating with precision. Salvation is not bought, not earned, and not accumulated through ritual fluency, repeated sacrifice or moral bookkeeping. It is given by grace, which means the beneficiary does not pay the price of access. "The receiver receives."

What affordability does not mean here

"It does not mean the cross became cheap." It means that what no sinner could afford is now made available at the point of reception. The precision matters, because the flattened version of the framework would read affordability as the cost falling — and it did not fall.

Criterion Three

Salvation is free at the point of reception and infinitely costly at the source.

This is where the analogy becomes sharper. If new access is opened, someone must bear the cost.

The crucifixion is as expensive as it can be, so that access can be as free as it needs to be.

— Easter through the lens of disruptive innovation
The cost was relocated, not reduced
"The claim is that the cost was relocated. The receiver does not pay, but the source bears it in full." In theological terms, that capital is borne by God Himself.
Why this is the criterion the slogan loses
A framework that describes only simplicity and price cannot say who paid for the opening. Christensen's capital-absorption criterion asks exactly that question — and it is the one that makes the theological reading a structural argument rather than a metaphor.

Criterion Four

It did not stop at access; it generated a new field of work and institution.

A real disruption does not stop at access. It generates consequences. So the question must be asked: what did the crucifixion and resurrection create?

The answer cannot be limited to inward comfort or private belief. The Gospel generated a vast new field of mission, ministry, witness, teaching, service, stewardship and institution-building. It created roles, obligations and organized forms of labor.

Across centuries, Christianity helped produce churches, schools, hospitals, charities, traditions of literacy, manuscript culture, educational institutions, moral reasoning and habits of organized care.

"Christianity did not remain a private spiritual mood. It generated civilizational consequence." Stated across all four criteria: "Christ made access simpler. Grace made salvation free at the point of reception. God supplied the ultimate capital. The Gospel generated a new world of work."

What the article admits before claiming this

"This history is mixed, and that must be admitted. It contains coercion, hypocrisy, corruption, and violence as well as mercy, learning, and service." The fourth criterion is claimed only after that admission, not instead of it.

The Moral Turn

If the curtain was torn, no institution may rebuild the barrier.

Only then does the moral question arrive — and it does not stay theological.

"If the curtain has been torn, then religion must not rebuild barriers." If access has been opened, then institutions must not close themselves around the already secure. If Christ identifies Himself with the hungry, the stranger, the sick and the imprisoned, then the meaning of Easter cannot remain private. It becomes public, because access always has public consequence.

That is why the issue is not only theological. It is civic. "Any government that serves elites while neglecting the precarious stands against the moral direction of the cross." Any institution that protects privilege while excusing exclusion does the same.

Any official who separates authority from compassion repeats the logic of barrier rather than the logic of opened access.

Conclusion

The final question is not whether the story is believed but whether it entered conduct.

The article ends by turning the framework on the reader rather than on an institution.

When power is placed in my hands, do I open the way for others, or do I become another barrier?

— Easter through the lens of disruptive innovation
Belief is not the test the piece applies
"The final question is whether its force has entered conduct." The four criteria were never only a way of describing what happened; they are a way of testing what the reader does with power that is already in their hands.
Why it ends without recommendations
The article states a moral direction rather than a programme, and closes on a question addressed to the author as much as to anyone else. No policy actions are proposed there, and none are supplied here.
