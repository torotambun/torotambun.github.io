Prediction Machines Revisited: The Microeconomics of Cheap Prediction

Cheaper machine prediction does not merely make a task cheaper. It changes the cost-minimizing mix of prediction, judgment and human forecasting, so organizations must redesign how decisions are produced.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

AI matters economically because it lowers the price of prediction, which is one input into a decision.

"Prediction Machines argues that AI matters economically because it reduces the cost of prediction."

Situation

"When prediction becomes cheaper, organizations must reconsider how decisions are made."

Complication

Agrawal, Gans and Goldfarb separate a decision into three parts. "Prediction estimates what may happen. Judgment attaches value to possible outcomes and determines acceptable trade-offs." Action then follows the decision.

Question

What does standard microeconomics predict when the price of one input into a decision falls?

Answer

The whole input mix is re-optimized, so cheap prediction is an economic reason to reconsider the surrounding inputs and not a tool added to an unchanged organization.

What the formulas are

"The formulas below are my own formalization of the economic logic. They are not equations presented in Prediction Machines."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

Standard production theory says that when one input becomes cheaper, the cost-minimizing combination of inputs changes.

The argument uses ordinary production theory and the economics of complements and substitutes.

"If the price of one input falls, the cost-minimizing combination of inputs can change." The note then states the point that carries the rest of the argument: "The important point is that firms do not choose each input independently. They choose a combination of inputs."

Applied to AI, the input whose price is falling is prediction. "Prediction is not simply a new tool added to an unchanged organization."

The chain runs from cheaper prediction to more machine prediction becoming viable, to complementary inputs gaining value, to substitute activities facing downward pressure. The note calls this "standard production logic, not an AI-specific rule".

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Prediction and judgment can be complements while machine prediction and human prediction can be substitutes.

"Decision quality depends partly on prediction and partly on judgment." The two relationships that follow are different in kind.

Where machine prediction complements human judgment
•"Prediction alone does not produce a decision."
•"When prediction and judgment are complementary, better prediction increases the marginal contribution of judgment, while stronger judgment increases the value obtained from prediction."
•"Human judgment and machine prediction may complement each other inside the decision loop."
•The hedge is stated: "This does not mean prediction and judgment are always complements. Their relationship depends on the structure of the decision."
Where machine prediction substitutes for human prediction
•"Routine human forecasting may perform part of the same economic function as machine prediction."
•"Human prediction and machine prediction may compete for the same function."
•If machine prediction becomes cheaper and sufficiently accurate, "some routine human prediction tasks can face substitution pressure"
•The cost of a decision then depends on how much machine prediction, judgment and human forecasting are used, and what each costs
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

A fall in the price of machine prediction changes the cheapest way to produce a decision.

When the price of an important input falls, the organization has reason to re-optimize the production process. Four consequences follow.

1  ·  More of the cheaper input
"More of the cheaper input may be used." Prediction is used more widely because it is now economically viable.
2  ·  Complements gain value
"Complementary inputs may become more valuable." Judgment is the main candidate where it is technologically complementary to prediction.
3  ·  Substitutes lose value
"Substitute activities may lose economic value." Routine human forecasting is the exposed input.
4  ·  Workflows are redesigned
"Workflows may need redesign because the relative economics of the inputs have changed." Forecast-driven processes such as inventory, underwriting, triage, routing and maintenance are named as examples.
The mechanism in one sentence

"A fall in the price of prediction does not merely make an existing task cheaper. It can change the economically preferred way of producing the decision."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Reframing a task as prediction shows where AI helps, but no activity is only prediction.

"Prediction supplies information. Judgment determines what to do with it."

The part that can be reframed as prediction
•Driving as predicting what other road users will do next
•Diagnosis as the probability of a condition given observed evidence
•Fraud detection as predicting whether a pattern belongs to an abnormal class
•Manufacturing as prediction of equipment failure, process deviation or defect probability
The part that still requires judgment
•"Diagnosis still requires judgment about treatment."
•"Insurance still requires decisions about acceptable exposure."
•"Manufacturing still requires decisions about maintenance, shutdowns, quality thresholds, and safety."
•"Without careful reframing, organizations may automate the wrong part of the decision."
The more demanding question

"Which part of this decision is prediction, which part requires judgment, what action follows, and what happens to the economics when prediction becomes cheaper?"

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Implications

When prediction is abundant, the constraint moves to thresholds, exceptions and governance, and errors scale.

Organizational consequences are the second half of the economics.

The constraint moves to "setting thresholds, defining acceptable risk, deciding exceptions, establishing escalation rules" and deciding what counts as good enough.

Errors scale differently. "A mistaken human prediction may affect one decision. A mistaken prediction embedded in an automated workflow can be repeated thousands of times." So "Governance therefore becomes part of the production system rather than an afterthought."

Adoption is also a system question. "AI adoption cannot always be analysed one task at a time because organizations consist of interdependent tasks." Later work on insurance makes the related point that better prediction can alter mechanisms built for environments where prediction was costly or weak (Agrawal et al., 2024a, 2024b).

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Implications

Human work whose judgment complements prediction gains value, while routine prediction-like work faces substitution pressure.

"This is where my own reading of the economics extends beyond simply summarizing the book."

Humans "with depth in judgment, domain expertise, critical reasoning, and contextual interpretation can become more important where those capabilities are complementary to prediction."

The opposite follows from the same economics. "Human roles dominated by routine prediction-like work face greater substitution pressure because machine prediction attacks the cost of precisely that input." The cited authors describe the labour-market effect as ambiguous and not as simple wholesale replacement (Agrawal et al., 2019c).

For education and professional development the test moves. "The durable question is not whether humans can perform tasks that AI cannot perform today. That boundary will continue to move."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

The better question is which human capabilities stay productive once prediction becomes cheap.

"Once that price changes, the optimal input mix can change with it."

AI can be understood not merely as another tool to adopt, but as a reduction in the price of an important input into decision-making.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, Prediction Machines Revisited
