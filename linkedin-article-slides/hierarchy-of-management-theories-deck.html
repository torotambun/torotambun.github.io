Understanding the Hierarchy of Management Theories: From Grand Theories to Low-Range Theories

The article builds a structure the field does not supply. Management, unlike sociology, has no agreed hierarchy of theories, and the grand, middle-range and low-range scheme it uses is borrowed from Merton and from disciplines such as nursing rather than settled within management itself. That borrowing is stated openly rather than concealed, which is what makes the piece usable. Five grand theories are proposed as the top tier, each with a named originator and a dated main work, and the defining property of the tier is a weakness rather than a strength: grand theories are too abstract to be easily tested or directly applied. Middle-range theories are where empirical work actually happens, and each grand theory is given its own derivatives beneath it. Evolutionary theory then fails to fit any of the five and is admitted as a sixth on its own terms. A disclaimer added eighteen days after publication concedes that the whole categorisation is subjective and that every scholar ends up drawing a different map.

Introduction

The hierarchy is borrowed rather than native to management, and the article says so before using it.

The scheme comes from elsewhere and the article traces it. In sociology "the term “middle-range theory” was popularized by Robert K. Merton in the mid-20th century to describe theories that are more focused than grand theories but broader in scope than highly specialized, situational theories."

Management adopted it without formalising it. "In management studies, the hierarchy is not typically attributed to a single theorist but is more of an analytical tool used by scholars to categorize and discuss theories based on their practical or empirical utility, conceptual breadth, and level of abstraction."

Which makes the structure a convention rather than a finding, and the article states that plainly: the idea of a hierarchy "is more of a scholarly convention than a formalized concept proposed by a single individual."

Its provenance is named too. The categorisation "borrows from the language used in other disciplines, like sociology and nursing, to describe the breadth and depth of theories." A management reader is therefore told where the frame came from before being asked to accept it.

A disclaimer added eighteen days after publication

Dated 23 September 2023 and placed at the head of the article: "The categorization of management theories can vary depending on the perspective taken. It took me days to finally settle with this, not until I manage to write and conclude and draw my own roadmap. Every scholar tends to have his/her own roadmap that is very much subjective to his realm of research."

Framework

What makes a theory grand is a weakness, not a strength.

The article defines the top tier by what it cannot do, which is the opposite of how such tiers are usually presented.

Grand theories "aspire to offer universal principles that can explain a wide range of phenomena across different types of organizations, industries, and even time periods." The cost of that reach is immediate: "Because of their broad scope, grand theories are often more conceptual and abstract, providing a high-level view that may not be easily testable or directly applicable in specific situations."

Their function is therefore orientation rather than instruction. They "serve as big-picture frameworks that guide more focused research, which may, in turn, develop into middle-range or low-range theories that address specific questions or challenges within the grand theory's broader context."

And the article does not overstate the category's rigour: "The term “grand theory” is not rigidly defined, and its application can vary based on the scope and the impact of the theory in question."

The practical consequence for a manager

"While grand theories offer valuable insights for academic discourse and can influence managerial thinking at a conceptual level, their broad nature often makes them less actionable for solving specific, day-to-day managerial challenges, which are better addressed by middle-range or low-range theories."

Details

Five grand theories, each with an originator and a dated work, so the tier can be checked.

This is the article's own summary rather than a standard list, and every entry carries a proposer, a main work and a worked example of application.

1  ·  Scientific Management — Frederick W. Taylor
Main work: The Principles of Scientific Management (1911). It "advocates for the systematic training of workers in “one best method” for performing their tasks, rather than allowing them personal discretion", emphasising time studies, work studies and job specialisation. Applied by Ford on the Model T assembly line.
2  ·  Human Relations Movement — Elton Mayo
Main work: the Hawthorne Studies (1924 to 1932). The theory "argues that workers are not solely motivated by money but could be better motivated by having their social and psychological needs met." The modern example given is employee-friendly workplaces at companies such as Google.
3  ·  Systems Theory — Ludwig von Bertalanffy
Main work: General System Theory: Foundations, Development, Applications (1968). It "considers organizations as systems of interrelated and interdependent parts", offering insight into organisational complexity, and is applied where departments in large corporations or healthcare organisations must work cohesively.
4  ·  Contingency Theory — Fred Fiedler and Henry Mintzberg
Main works: Fiedler's A Theory of Leadership Effectiveness (1967) and Mintzberg's The Structuring of Organizations (1979). Optimal performance comes from "fitting characteristics of the organization, such as its structure and leadership style, to the demands of its environment."
5  ·  Organizational Culture and Climate — Edgar Schein
Main work: Organizational Culture and Leadership (1985). It "explores how the shared values, beliefs, and practices within an organization shape employee behavior and job performance", with Southwest Airlines given as the applied case.

Evidence

Middle-range theories are where research can actually be done, which is why each grand theory gets its own derivatives.

The second tier is defined by testability rather than by scope, and that is the whole point of having it.

These are frameworks "that focus on specific domains or aspects of organizational behavior, leadership, and strategy, aiming to offer actionable insights that are generalizable across a range of situations", operating at an intermediate level of abstraction.

The contrast with the tier above is stated directly: "Unlike grand theories, which aim to offer overarching explanations for many dimensions of organizational life but can be hard to test or apply directly, middle-range theories are empirically grounded. They can be tested and refined through research, and they provide actionable guidelines for practitioners."

The resource-based view is the worked example. It "doesn't aim to explain every aspect of organizational behavior or success, but it does provide a focused lens that is widely applicable across different kinds of organizations and industries." Transaction cost economics, agency theory and the leadership theories are placed in the same tier.

The derivatives, sorted under their parents

Under Scientific Management: Resource Advantage Theory (Hunt and Morgan, 1995) and Time and Motion Studies (Taylor and Gilbreth). Under the Human Relations Movement: Job Enrichment (Herzberg, 1968), Equity or Organizational Justice Theory (Adams, 1965), Psychological Contract Theory (Rousseau, 1989), Social Exchange Theory (Homans and Blau, 1950), Stakeholder Theory (Freeman, 1984), the leadership theories, and Job Characteristics Theory (Hackman and Oldham, 1980).

Gap

Evolutionary theory fits none of the five, so it is admitted as a sixth on its own terms.

The article's most interesting move is to break its own taxonomy rather than force a theory into it, and the reasoning is set out rather than asserted.

1  ·  Why it does not fit
"Evolutionary theory doesn't neatly fall under the traditional grand theories of management like Human Relations, Scientific Management, Systems Theory, or Contingency Theory." It aligns instead with population ecology, which "emphasizes the importance of external factors over managerial strategy in determining organizational success", and with institutional theory.
2  ·  Its three mechanisms
Variation, where organisations differ in processes, strategies and structures "either by design or by accident". Selection, where "The environment (market) determines which of these variations are most suitable." Retention, where "Successful and effective strategies, processes, and structures get institutionalized and retained over time."
3  ·  What it explains that the others do not
Organisational life cycles of birth, growth, maturity, decline and renewal. And the impermanence of advantage: "organizations develop competencies and strategies over time, which give them a competitive advantage. However, as environments change, previously advantageous traits might become obsolete."
4  ·  Its own derivatives
Dynamic capabilities, the resource-based view, disruptive innovation after Clayton Christensen, organisational learning, institutional theory, and path dependency, which "Considers how the set of decisions an organization faces currently is limited by the decisions made in the past, even though past circumstances may no longer be relevant."
A perspective the article declines to promote

The Market-Based View is discussed and then deliberately not admitted as a middle-range theory: of it the article says "it's generally not formalized as a standalone middle-range theory in the same way RBV is." It is treated as a perspective focused on external market conditions instead. Porter's Five Forces and PESTEL are named as aligning with it.

Conclusion

The taxonomy ends where its author admits it was always going to end: in a personal map.

The article closes on the Theory of Planned Behavior, given with its critiques attached, and on a sourcing note that few articles in this archive carry.

Every scholar tends to have his/her own roadmap that is very much subjective to his realm of research.

— Understanding the Hierarchy of Management Theories, 5 September 2023
The Theory of Planned Behavior
Proposed by Icek Ajzen in 1985 as an extension of the Theory of Reasoned Action developed with Martin Fishbein, structured around attitude toward the behaviour, subjective norm, and perceived behavioural control.
Its critiques, given equal space
Overemphasis on rational decision-making, "overlooking the impact of emotions, habits, and unconscious processes on behavior". Limited predictive power for spontaneous behaviour. Perceived behavioural control being hard to distinguish from self-efficacy. And limited cultural sensitivity, which "may not fully account for cultural differences in social norms and attitudes".
The alternative taxonomy it flags
The article notes at the outset that a different organising principle yields a different list: sorted by historical development, the categories become classical theories covering scientific, administrative and bureaucratic management, and neoclassical theories covering the human relations movement, behavioural science, Theory X and Theory Y, and human resources theory.
