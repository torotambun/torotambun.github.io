The Hydrocarbon Extractions in Indonesia are Still in Demand

The article uses six observations from the 2019 BP statistical review of Indonesia as a starting hypothesis. Oil and gas fell in power generation, hydro fell 21%, coal consumption rose 38%, non-hydro renewables stayed small, and oil supply declined 3.5% in 2017 to 2018 against an average annual decline of 1.5%. It asks whether the national oil company's learning curve or a generation gap explains the steeper decline, proposes recruitment, training and coaching as the first remedy if so, and argues that hydrocarbon extraction deserves renewed attention because non-hydro renewables and geothermal probably will not be ready to replace hydrocarbon within 7 to 10 years.

Introduction

Indonesia's 2019 BP figures are a starting hypothesis, and the hypothesis is that hydrocarbon extraction is still in demand.

Situation

The article applies number crunching to the BP statistical review published in 2019, as an example from Indonesian energy. It calls number crunching a small part of analysis and always a good start to establish a hypothesis.

Complication

In the figures it reads, oil and gas fall in power generation, hydro falls by 21%, coal consumption rises by 38% and non-hydro renewables stay small. Oil supply declines by 3.5% in 2017 to 2018, against an average annual decline of 1.5%.

Question

What do those figures imply for a country whose goals include securing energy for its people?

Answer

That the country must pay more attention to revisiting hydrocarbon extraction, because its yields are more certain than those of non-hydro renewables, and that it should not abandon hydrocarbon for battery technology that is not yet here.

Evidence

Oil and gas fell in power generation, hydro fell 21%, coal consumption rose 38%, and non-hydro renewables stayed near 5.5% of generation.

Five of the article's six numbered observations concern the energy mix. The sixth, on oil supply, follows on the next slide.

1  ·  Oil
Oil stays at about 45% of energy consumption, but its use in electricity generation fell from 14% to 7.6%, which the article prints as a 46% reduction.
2  ·  Gas
Gas fell from 32% to 22% for power generation, in line with a fall in its consumption demand from 26% to 18%, which the article prints as 31%.
3  ·  Coal
Coal compensated for both, with a 38% increase in consumption, mainly for electricity generation, where the increase was 42%.
4  ·  Hydro
Renewable hydro decreased by 21%. The article asks why and does not answer.
5  ·  Non-hydro renewables
The article says no renewable source other than biofuel and geothermal is used in Indonesia. They are 1.8% of total consumption, a 29% increase from 1.4% in 2008, and their share of power generation is printed as 5.6% to 5.5%, the same since 2008.
Evidence note

No comparison period is printed for the oil, gas, coal or hydro changes, and none is assumed here. The 21% hydro fall is not qualified as consumption or generation.

Gap

Oil supply declined 3.5% in 2017 to 2018 against an average annual decline of 1.5%, and the article asks why.

This sixth observation is the one the article says needs to be addressed.

The long-run average
•An average annual decline of 1.5%
•Derived while the international oil companies (IOCs) still managed most of the major fields in the country
•Derived before the handover of those fields to the national oil company (NOC)
2017 to 2018
✗A steeper decline rate of 3.5%
✗The article asks why the decline is this steep
✗It offers no measured cause, only the questions on the next slide

Details

If the cause is a human-capital gap, the article's first remedy is a changed recruitment process, then training and coaching.

The article puts its causes as questions and its remedy as a conditional. The deck keeps them that way.

1  ·  The learning curve
Is the steep decline due to the NOC's slower learning curve in handling the assets as professionally as the IOCs used to operate their assets in a host country?
2  ·  The generation gap
Is it due to the generation gap of 8 to 15 years that occurs throughout the oil and gas industry and was inherited from the IOCs?
3  ·  The counter-question
If so, how is it explained that the IOCs also suffered from the human-capital gap yet always found ways to overcome it and stayed productive and efficient for many years?
4  ·  The conditional remedy
If the answer is yes, the article says perhaps the first remedy is to change the recruitment process dramatically, for a horizon of 3 to 4 years, and to fill the competence gap in both soft skills and hard skills through training and coaching.
Evidence note

The article gives no data on the generation gap, on the NOC or on the IOCs' productivity, and it does not answer its own questions.

Implications

Non-hydro renewables and geothermal probably will not be ready to replace hydrocarbon within 7 to 10 years, provided the economics of climate change are common ground.

The article expects an objection from climate change and answers it with a challenge.

The objection the article expects
•Climate change and CO2 emission are raised against addressing the decline in oil supply
•The article says those who raise them must think what alternative solution they propose
The article's reply
✓It sets a condition: some common ground on the economics of climate change and CO2 emission
✓On that condition, non-hydro renewables and geothermal probably will not, in the next 7 to 10 years, have anything substantial ready to replace hydrocarbon as a source of energy
Evidence note

The 7 to 10 year outlook is stated as a probability, with no source or calculation. The article does not define the common ground on climate economics.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

A country that aims to secure energy for its people must revisit extraction, and should not abandon hydrocarbon for a battery technology that is not yet here.

The article closes on extraction yields and on a video's advice about batteries.

After all, the yields are more certain than the renewable non-hydro anyway.

— The Hydrocarbon Extractions in Indonesia are Still in Demand, 18 June 2019
The stated goal
The article names securing energy for its people as one of the country's goals and reasons from that goal to the case for revisiting extraction.
The battery point
The last line, offered as suggested in a video from 19:55 onwards, says the country should not naively abandon hydrocarbon to chase a battery technology that is not so soon to come. The video was not opened.
What supports it
Six observations from one BP report and a 7 to 10 year expectation with no source. The article offers no reserve, cost or volume data.
