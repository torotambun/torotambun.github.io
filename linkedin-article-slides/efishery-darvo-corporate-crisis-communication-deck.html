Teaching Note: The eFishery Case and the Application of DARVO in Corporate Crisis Communication

DARVO — a framework built to describe how individuals accused of interpersonal abuse respond to confrontation — reappears, largely unconsciously, in corporate crisis communication. Applied hypothetically to the eFishery-Northstar fraud scandal, the same three-move sequence (deny, attack, reverse victim and offender) shows up on both sides of the dispute: the accused founders redirect scrutiny toward their own values, and the investors who backed them redirect it toward the founders alone. DARVO does not resolve a crisis; it reshapes who carries the burden of accountability, which is exactly why a reader needs the framework to see it happening.

Introduction

A framework built for abuse survivors reappears, unconsciously, in corporate scandal.

The DARVO framework — Deny, Attack, Reverse Victim and Offender — was introduced by psychologist Jennifer Freyd in 1997 to describe how perpetrators of wrongdoing respond when confronted with accusations (Freyd, 1997).

Situation

Originally studied in cases of abuse and interpersonal misconduct, the framework has since been observed in political, corporate and financial fraud cases — Theranos, WeWork, FTX — where public figures or organizations manipulate narratives to evade accountability (Harsey & Freyd, 2017).

Complication

These tactics may not always be deliberately orchestrated but can emerge naturally as individuals and organizations seek to protect their reputations and financial interests.

Question

Can the same lens explain how a company and its investors both respond to a live fraud scandal — and does naming the pattern require proving intent?

Answer

No — and the note is explicit about the limit. "While no definitive conclusions can be made about the intent behind eFishery's and Northstar's responses, analyzing their public statements through the DARVO framework allows us to" identify patterns rather than assign intent.

Framework

Three moves — deny, attack, reverse victim and offender — recur across Theranos, WeWork and FTX.

The pattern precedes eFishery by years.

In corporate settings, DARVO manifests as: Deny — the accused party rejects the allegations outright or reframes them to diminish their significance; Attack — the accused party discredits accusers, whistleblowers or critics rather than engaging with the claims; Reverse Victim and Offender — the accused party presents itself as the true victim while portraying the actual complainant as the aggressor.

During the FTX collapse, Sam Bankman-Fried initially denied misappropriating customer funds, attacked regulators and media critics, and later framed himself as a victim of external pressures (Hern, 2022). Elizabeth Holmes of Theranos publicly framed regulatory investigations as suppressing innovation: "This is what happens when you work to change things: first they think you're crazy, then they fight you, and then all of a sudden you change the world" (CNBC, 2015).

Context

eFishery inflated revenue by roughly $600 million, and the fallout was immediate.

Founded in 2013 by CEO Gibran Huzaifah, eFishery built automated smart-feeding solutions for aquaculture and reached a valuation above $1.4 billion, backed by SoftBank, Temasek and Northstar Group (Guild, 2025).

In December 2024, allegations surfaced that eFishery had inflated its revenue by approximately $600 million over several years (South China Morning Post, 2025). A forensic audit found leadership had manipulated financial statements, inflating revenue nearly fivefold for the first nine months of 2024 (DealStreetAsia, 2025).

CEO Gibran Huzaifah and CPO Chrisna Aditya were suspended pending an internal investigation; FTI Consulting was appointed interim management to run an independent forensic review; Northstar filed a formal police report against eFishery's executives; and the company laid off over 300 employees (Dinilhaq, 2024; Reuters, 2025; Tech in Asia, 2025; The Jakarta Post, 2024).

Evidence

eFishery's own response tracks all three DARVO moves, one statement at a time.

Hypothetical application of the framework to the company's public statements.

01
Deny
Values instead of allegations
"We are fully aware of the gravity of the market speculation, and we take this matter with the utmost seriousness. We remain dedicated to upholding the highest standards of corporate governance and ethics" (Outlook Business, 2025) — a statement that affirms values without engaging the specific fraud claims.
02
Attack
Isolating the issue to individuals
The suspension of Huzaifah and Aditya "appears to be an attempt to isolate the issue to specific individuals, potentially diverting attention from systemic governance problems" (Jakarta Globe, 2024) — the same pattern WeWork used when it changed leadership rather than address structural issues.
03
Reverse victim and offender
The nation as the injured party
"The recent revelations of alleged misconduct (including fraud) within the Group have been deeply disheartening to us all and may jeopardize confidence in the Indonesian investment climate..." (DealStreetAsia, 2025) — reframing the harm as national rather than internal.

Evidence

Northstar's response runs the same three moves, aimed at its own exposure instead.

Investors are not traditionally cast as perpetrators, but the note applies the same lens to their public conduct.

01
Deny
We trusted the audited numbers
Investors stated they were unaware of financial discrepancies and had trusted eFishery's audited reports — "effectively shifting the focus away from potential investor due diligence failures" (The Diplomat, 2025), a pattern also seen when Sequoia and SoftBank distanced themselves from FTX.
02
Attack
A police report against the executives
Northstar reported eFishery's executives to law enforcement, "ensuring that legal scrutiny shifted toward company leadership, not investors" (Tech in Asia, 2025) — echoing how Theranos investors shifted focus onto Elizabeth Holmes alone.
03
Reverse victim and offender
Investors as the deceived party
Public statements emphasized that investors lost substantial funds, "reinforcing the narrative that they were misled rather than complicit" — the same framing FTX-adjacent venture firms used when they claimed to have been blindsided.

Conclusion

DARVO does not resolve a crisis — it reshapes who has to answer for it.

The note's closing argument, and the reading discipline it asks of the audience.

DARVO does not resolve crises—it RESHAPES them, shifting the burden of accountability and influencing how stakeholders interpret events.

— Closing conclusion of the teaching note
What critical reading looks for
"When denial replaces transparency, when accusers are attacked instead of being addressed, and when those responsible reposition themselves as victims, it signals a deliberate effort to CONTROL THE DISCOURSE RATHER THAN CONFRONT THE TRUTH."
Not exclusive to individuals
"This case underscores that DARVO is not exclusive to individuals accused of misconduct but extends to corporations and institutional investors seeking to manage public perception."
What the note does not claim
The analysis is explicitly hypothetical and speculative, offered as a conceptual tool rather than a finding of fact about eFishery, Northstar, or any named individual.
