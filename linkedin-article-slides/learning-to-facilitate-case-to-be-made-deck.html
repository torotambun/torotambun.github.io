Learning to facilitate a case study class from a case study "The Case to Be Made"

The case handed to a facilitator in training was itself about whether the case method is worth teaching. Its objections are cultural and structural rather than pedagogical, and the two answers that address them — piloting and hybrids — concede rather than rebut. The preparation record shows a facilitator planning not only what to ask but who should answer, and it stops before the class it was written for.

Opening

I learned to facilitate a case class by preparing one, and the case I was given was about whether the case method works.

The article is a preparation document, written the same afternoon as the class and published before it, in four labelled parts.

The four parts are the author's own: a two-to-three minute summary — headed "My 2-3 minutes summary of the case on the key convicts" and perspectives — a bullet-pointed mind map of the case's branches, the points he would raise if he were in the room as a participant, and finally the section headed "What I did prepared prior to the class today".

The recursion is in the material rather than in any claim the article makes about it. The exercise is to practise facilitating a case discussion, and the case chosen for the practice is a faculty meeting arguing about whether case discussions should be expanded. Every objection raised inside the case is an objection to the method being used to teach it.

What kind of document this is

The article records preparation, not outcome. It is written from before the class, and the deck follows it in describing only what was planned.

The Case

Dean Grant's faculty split along disciplines: strategy and marketing for, finance and accounting against.

"The case revolves around Dean Morgan Grant of Coburn School of Business considering the introduction of more case method teaching into the school's MBA and undergraduate curriculum."

The split is not random and the article's mind map makes that visible. On one side, "Strategy and Marketing professors advocate for the case method, emphasizing its ability to develop critical thinking, decision-making, and linking theory to practice. They highlight increased student engagement and active participation as key benefits."

On the other, "Finance and Accounting professors argue that the case method is less effective for technical, content-heavy courses. Other faculty express concerns about its fit with Coburn's student culture, fairness in participation, and the significant time investment required from faculty."

Where that leaves the dean is stated as the case's ending, not as a recommendation: "Dean Grant is left to weigh the potential benefits of expanding case method teaching against the cultural and logistical challenges presented by the faculty, sparking a broader discussion on how to evolve Coburn's teaching approach while maintaining academic rigor and inclusivity."

Details

The objections are cultural and structural — fairness, language, faculty time, classroom design — not claims that the method fails.

Ten faculty members speak in the case. Almost none of them argues that students learn less; they argue that the method costs something the school is not set up to pay.

1  ·  It does not fit quantitative or content-heavy courses
The finance professor's position is that the method is "Ineffective for quantitative disciplines" and the accounting professor's that it is "Inefficient for content-heavy courses." This is the closest the case comes to a pedagogical objection, and it is bounded by discipline rather than general.
2  ·  It does not fit undergraduates
One organisational-behaviour professor holds the method "Unsuitable for undergraduate students." The concern is readiness, not the method — the same content, a different audience.
3  ·  It is unfair, and the unfairness is measurable in the grade
A second organisational-behaviour professor raises "Fairness concerns: dominance of assertive students, subjectivity in grading." The article's own concern map pairs them: fairness is alpha students dominating, participation is subjectivity in grading. The unfairness is not only in who speaks but in what the speaking is worth.
4  ·  It costs faculty the time they owe to research
A strategy professor objects that it is "Time-consuming for faculty, impacts research productivity." The article's concern map adds a second cost — a potential decline in teaching ratings. Neither is about whether students learn.
5  ·  It does not fit the culture, or the rooms
An operations professor names a "Mismatch with collaborative, non-competitive student culture"; an international-business professor raises "Classroom design issues for case discussions." The article records that "Coburn's student culture is more collaborative, less competitive" and that the method is "perceived as at odds with this culture."
6  ·  It is not rigorous, and it is not experiential either
A managerial-economics professor raises "Concerns about academic rigor"; an entrepreneurship professor holds that the "Case method not truly experiential learning." These two objections pull in opposite directions — too loose for one, not real enough for the other — and the case leaves both standing.

The Position

Piloting and hybrid models are the two answers that concede the objections rather than argue with them.

Asked what he would say if he were in the room with Dean Morgan's team, the article lists six points. Two of them are the only proposals in the piece that would actually move.

The first is incremental: strategies "might include piloting case-based courses in specific programs, offering faculty development workshops on case teaching, providing additional support to students unfamiliar with the method, and using hybrid teaching models that blend lectures and cases." None of it requires winning the argument about whether the method is superior.

The second is architectural — "Consideration of hybrid approaches that blend case teaching with lectures to accommodate different learning needs and preferences." A hybrid grants the finance and accounting objection in full and keeps the method where it works.

The inclusivity concern is treated the same way: "Addressing concerns that the case method might favor more assertive students and potentially disadvantage those who are quieter or have language barriers." The remedies listed are "structured participation guidelines, alternative assessment methods, and additional support for non-native English speakers." And the article names the downside of moving badly — "The risk of alienating faculty and students if the transition to case teaching is not managed carefully."

Field Notes

For each question I prepared, I also chose who should answer it.

The preparation section carries five questions, and every one of them is followed by an expected answer and a "Preferred Student Profile". The casting is planned as deliberately as the questioning.

1  ·  Opening — the benefits, from the proponents
The question asks for "the core benefits of the case method in business education, particularly in an MBA program, as outlined by the proponents in the case?" Preferred profile: "An experienced MBA student or someone with a background in strategy or marketing" — one who "would likely have strong opinions on the benefits of applying theory to practice."
2  ·  The objections, and where they come from
The question asks how the arguments against "reflect the specific culture and structure of Coburn School of Business?" Preferred profile: "A student with a background in finance, accounting, or another technical field could provide insight into the challenges of applying the case method in these disciplines." The objection is put to someone who holds it.
3  ·  Fairness and participation
The question asks how "issues of fairness and student participation play into the debate" and what solutions exist. Preferred profile: "A student who has experience with case discussions, possibly in an OB or psychology context, where understanding group dynamics and inclusivity is crucial."
4  ·  How to integrate without alienating anyone
The question asks what strategies could "gradually integrate more case teaching into Coburn's curriculum without alienating faculty or students?" Preferred profile: "A student with an interest in educational strategy or curriculum design, possibly someone with experience in educational administration".
5  ·  Rigor, and whether it can be reconciled
The question asks how academic rigor intersects with the method "according to the faculty, and is there a way to reconcile these concerns?" Preferred profile: "A student with a strong analytical background, possibly from an economics or operations research background, who can critically assess the balance between academic rigor and practical application."
Why the profiles matter

A question list is a plan for the content. A question list with a preferred respondent attached to each item is a plan for the room — who speaks, in what order, and from which position. The article states the purpose in its own terms: "These questions are designed to engage students from various backgrounds and fields of expertise, ensuring a well-rounded discussion that explores multiple facets of the case method in business education."

Closing

The preparation ends at the questions; the article never reports how the class went.

What the record contains and what it does not is worth stating plainly, because the piece is easily read as an account of a class it does not describe.

These questions are designed to engage students from various backgrounds and fields of expertise, ensuring a well-rounded discussion that explores multiple facets of the case method in business education.

— Learning to facilitate a case study class from a case study "The Case to Be Made", 15 August 2024
Published before the class it prepares for
The source is created and published on the afternoon of 15 August 2024, and its fourth section is headed "What I did prepared prior to the class today". No outcome, no student response and no revision after the fact appears anywhere in it.
The case's question is left open here too
The case ends with Dean Grant weighing benefits against costs, and the article ends the same way. It sets out both sides, adds two workable paths, and does not say what Coburn should do.
What a companion piece would add
The delivered analysis of a case series — a directive style, its collapse, and the shift in the base of authority that repaired it — appears in a separate article published the same evening, just over two hours later. The two pieces are not labelled as a series and neither cites the other.

Sources

The article carries no reference list and cites no works. "The Case to Be Made" is named as its source case and is not otherwise identified. Coburn School of Business, Dean Morgan Grant and the ten faculty members quoted by name are characters within that case, not real people, and are treated as such here. Three of the article's illustrations are the author's own hand-drawn mind maps — the first three branches, the second three, and what he would say if he were on Dean Morgan's team — and they are images that do not survive text extraction; the deck is built from the prose.
