What it Feels to be a Citizen ? (context: in a Society Marked by Inequality and Corruption)

A closing philosophy class takes Rousseau's lament that specialists abound while citizens have disappeared, and the article asks whether that diagnosis survives transplantation. Its claim is that it does not, or not intact: Rousseau describes civic virtue eroding in a society otherwise functioning, while the societies the author has in mind are failing earlier than that, at the level of equal access to rights. The class's answer, drawn from citizenship regime studies, is that citizenship in such places runs on personal networks and clientelistic exchange rather than on rules, so rights operate as privileges granted by brokers. The article's own conclusion locates the lever in early education.

Opening

Rousseau counted physicists, poets and painters in plenty and no citizen among them.

The closing class of a political philosophy course opened on Rousseau, given in Indonesian and then in English, and the article reproduces both.

We have physicists, geometricians, chemists, astronomers, poets, musicians, and painters in plenty; but we have no longer a citizen among us

— Rousseau, quoted by Prof. Karlina Supeli in the closing class, reproduced in the article
The passage continues
"or if there be found a few scattered over our abandoned countryside, they are left to perish there unnoticed and neglected. Such is the condition to which we are reduced, and such are our feelings towards those who give us our daily bread, and our children milk"
What Rousseau was arguing
Written in response to a prompt from the Academy of Dijon, the discourse examines whether the arts and sciences improve a society's morals, and argues they may corrupt rather than purify human character.
The mechanism he feared
That specialised professions and individualistic goals erode communal values and civic engagement, so that the citizen, meaning someone actively engaged in the political life of a community, diminishes in importance as expertise grows.
What citizenship meant to him
Not residence and not membership. The article's rendering is active participation, civic engagement and a sense of collective responsibility, with his critique read as a call for balance between individual accomplishment and civic duty.

The Question

That lament diagnoses a decline in civic virtue, and it presumes a society not already broken by inequality.

The article accepts Rousseau's account on its own ground before questioning its reach. The quotation reflects a broader philosophical position about human nature and about balancing personal freedom against the needs of the community, and on that ground the article does not dispute it.

The move it makes is to ask about transfer. The perspective takes a different dimension, it argues, when applied to societies far removed from Rousseau's ideal, particularly those deeply divided by wealth and power and where corruption is rampant.

The author states his own standing before pressing the point, and the hedge is his own: he attributes the question to a lack of exercise in the philosophy discipline rather than presenting it as an expert correction. What follows is framed as an intrigued question about application, not as a refutation.

The author's own qualification

"Due to my lack of exercise in philosophy discipline, I was intrigued how this theory to be applied in countries where a vast chasm exists between the rich and the poor…" The article carries the limitation itself rather than leaving it to a reader.

The Gap

In a country where oligarchs overshadow professionals, the problem is not lost virtue but systemic injustice.

The article's central distinction is one of ordering. Rousseau's specialists crowd out the citizen; here, the article argues, something crowds out both. Where the influence of oligarchs and what it calls hungry wolves, a metaphor it glosses as those who exploit or oppress others, overshadows the contributions of professionals, the situation is structurally different from the one Rousseau described.

The consequence the article draws is that the diagnosis has to be re-ordered rather than re-applied. Such societies are not primarily experiencing a decline in civic virtue; they are grappling with systemic inequality and social injustice, and the civic decline is downstream of that.

The article describes the resulting arrangement as a concentration of power and wealth in the hands of a few, producing the marginalisation and disenfranchisement of the majority, which it sets in stark contrast to Rousseau's picture of engaged citizens balancing rights against responsibilities.

The re-ordering, stated

"These societies are not just facing a decline in civic virtues and citizenship but are MAINLY grappling with deep-rooted systemic inequalities and pervasive social injustices." The emphasis is the article's own.

Diagnosis

Four conditions compound: economic stratification, oligarchic power, absent voice and intellectual stagnation.

The article sets out what such a society actually consists of, and the four reinforce one another rather than sitting side by side.

1  ·  Economic inequality and social stratification
A wide gap between wealthy and poor signifies severe economic disparity, which limits access to education, healthcare and other essential services for the underprivileged and perpetuates a cycle of poverty.
2  ·  Oligarchy and power dynamics
The dominance of oligarchs indicates a structure in which a few individuals or groups hold disproportionate control over resources, decision-making and policy direction, producing corruption, nepotism, and policies favouring the elite at the general population's expense.
3  ·  Lack of representation and voice
Where governing bodies do not represent their interests, the average citizen may feel disenfranchised and powerless, which the article says leads to apathy, disengagement from civic duties, and in some cases social unrest.
4  ·  Cultural and intellectual stagnation
The absence of a thriving community of intellectuals, artists, scientists and scholars stalls cultural and intellectual development, whether through lack of funding, censorship, or an environment that does not value creative and scientific pursuit.
Which framework the article thinks fits

It proposes that Rousseau's model, balancing individual rights against communal responsibility, may not be entirely applicable here, and names social justice and critical theory as offering more relevant frameworks for equitable distribution, critique of power structures and inclusive decision-making.

Evidence

Van Klinken finds that citizenship in country X runs on clientelistic exchange rather than on rules and laws.

The class's answer came through citizenship regime studies, which read citizenship from concrete daily interactions between citizens and the state rather than from formal theory. The article writes the country as X throughout, and the deck follows it.

1  ·  The theories do not transfer
"The formal citizenship that emerges in the citizenship theories discussed above has not yet been applicable to…" post-colonial countries of this kind, because the theories are drawn from reflections and empirical experience of Western societies.
2  ·  First conclusion: an inherited clientelism
"contemporary citizenship in country X inherits a clientelistic bureaucracy from the pre-colonial era, which was then perpetuated during the colonial period when the imperialist chose to govern their colony through local nobility." Daily interactions with the state are formed by personal relationships and exchange norms rather than by rules and laws.
3  ·  Second conclusion: democratisation has not strengthened institutions
The post-reformation process has not yet succeeded in strengthening state institutions. High costs of local and general elections produce clandestine exchanges between politicians, bureaucrats and business actors, to those business actors' benefit.
4  ·  Third conclusion: informality blocks legal recourse
A very large informal economic sector makes it difficult for workers to seek protection through legal channels, so they rely on political intermediaries and ethnic networks to limit transaction costs and protect livelihoods, and access to resources depends on clientelistic exchange. The article's own aside notes the irony in a land where millions of micro enterprises are celebrated.

Details

Rights expanded on paper while the ability to realise them depends on the quality of a personal network.

The class's summary of the citizenship studies turns on a discrepancy rather than an absence. Citizens' rights were formally expanded, as documented in law, while the actual ability to realise them in some areas is not improving or is declining.

The explanation offered is the informal character of the state. Political interference in bureaucracy and policy weakens the rule of law, and what then determines whether a formal right can be exercised is something outside the law entirely.

Identity compounds it. Traditional community concepts shape understandings of rights, and identity groups act as intermediaries in dealings with the state, so membership partly determines access to resources. The result is a layered citizenship in which community membership mediates the relationship with the state, and strong religious identities further shape how citizenship is interpreted and practised.

Participation shows the same doubleness. The post-reform public space is active with labour unions, human rights activists, farmer, fisher and women's organisations, which indicates critical citizens building a republican tradition; at the same time citizens still depend on personal connections with power holders.

What determines a right

"Citizens' ability to realize formal rights, and thus the quality of their citizenship, depends on the quality of their personal networks."

What that turns a right into

"Connections are more important than formal rights, leading to the treatment of citizen rights as privileges granted by authorities and intermediaries/brokers, rather than as impersonal rights inherently held by all citizens."

Conclusion

Rousseau's participatory ideal applies only after the inequality is addressed, and the lever is early education.

The article's conclusion is conditional rather than dismissive, and its final move locates the intervention decades before any act of citizenship.

creating a society with noble shared values, system 1 thinking, automatic, reflect, muscle memory

— What it Feels to be a Citizen ?, 18 December 2023
The condition
The article's own sentence is that only once such societies are transformed may the noble insight of Rousseau, which it tags as participatory democracy, become applicable. The ideal is not rejected; its preconditions are named.
What has to come first
Equitable distribution of resources, fair representation of all societal groups in governance, and an environment that supports intellectual and cultural growth, with policies reducing inequality, combating corruption and promoting social justice.
Where the class landed
The author records that the professor shared his belief that educating children is the main backbone of a citizen-focused society, in different terminology, and that her emphasis on early education was the highlight of the sixteen-week course.
The phrase he supplies for it
The quotation above is the author's own vocabulary for what he understood her to mean, offered explicitly as his translation of her point rather than as her words.
