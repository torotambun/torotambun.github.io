(1 of 3) Religion Is Not to Blame. Moral Void Behind Ritual Obedience, Perhaps?

Indonesia's corruption is not evidence that religion failed. It is evidence that religiosity was never sufficient — religion evolved to ease fear rather than to build conscience, and conscience is the product of moral development that most people, and most societies, never complete.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

One of the world's most religious nations keeps failing the ethical test religion is assumed to guarantee.

The piece opens from M. Zaid Wahyudi's article on the same paradox.

Situation

"why does one of the most religious nations in the world continue to struggle with deeply rooted corruption?"

Complication

"The assumption that religion naturally produces moral behavior is not only common—it is culturally reinforced. Yet the facts point elsewhere."

Question

"This contradiction cannot be resolved simply by blaming weak enforcement or cultural habits." So what is religion actually for?

Answer

"It requires examining what religion is, what it was originally for, and why it still holds such power—even when separated from ethical consequences."

Gap

Religion emerged to ease fear, not to build conscience.

W. W. Howells, a biological anthropologist, is the starting point — The Heathens: Primitive Man and His Religions, 1962.

Howells "argued that religion did not emerge to make people good, but to help them survive." He framed it "not as a moral command from heaven, but as a cultural adaptation forged in fear and uncertainty." Early humans built it to ease anxiety, give meaning to suffering, provide a sense of control through ritual, and hold the group together through taboos, myths and symbols.

"In Howells’ framing, religion’s primary function was comfort, not conscience. It was never meant to be a perfect guide to ethics—it was a survival tool."

That function has not lapsed. "What was true for “primitive man” still operates subtly today making religion more about belonging than transformation." Prayers, fasting and sacred symbols continue to deliver emotional reassurance, social belonging and perceived protection — and none of those is conscience.

"One can fear God yet cheat taxpayers. One can perform rituals yet abuse office. One can wear piety as a badge, yet act with impunity." The disconnect is not religion failing at its job. It is a misreading of what the job was.

"The solution is not to abandon religion, but to reclaim it as a means—not an end."

Framework

Most public religiosity stalls in the four stages where morality is still external.

Lawrence Kohlberg's stages of moral development explain why devotion and ethics can move independently.

1  ·  Stage 0 — Pre-moral
"the level of very young children or individuals who act solely based on what brings pleasure or avoids pain, without understanding rules or others' needs"
2  ·  Stage 1 — Obedience and punishment
"Many people pray or follow religious rules out of fear of hell or social shame—not inner conviction."
3  ·  Stage 2 — Instrumental relativism
"Morality is transactional. Acts like charity or public piety may be done to “gain merit,” not to pursue justice."
4  ·  Stage 3 — Good boy / good girl
"Religious identity becomes more about being socially accepted than ethically grounded."
5  ·  Stage 4 — Law and order
"Religion may serve to uphold institutions— even corrupt ones—under the name of tradition and stability."
6  ·  Stage 5 — Social contract
"At this stage, a religious person may critique a corrupt institution, even if it appears religious or popular."
7  ·  Stage 6 — Universal ethical principles
"Moral reasoning here is autonomous. Religion, if present, serves as inspiration—not control."
Where it stops

"Very few reach stages 5 and 6. They require critical reflection, empathy, and moral courage —qualities not always nurtured by systems that emphasize conformity and obedience."

Details

Knowing the right answer is only one of four components, and it is not the one that fails.

James Rest, building on Kohlberg, separates moral behaviour into four parts. Religion reaches the first two.

01
Component 1
Moral sensitivity
"Recognizing that a situation has ethical dimensions." Religious formation supplies this readily.
02
Component 2
Moral judgment
"Knowing what the right action is." Also well supplied — and insufficient on its own.
03
Component 3
Moral motivation
"Prioritizing moral values over personal gain." This is where the chain breaks.
04
Component 4
Moral character
"Having the inner strength and resilience to act." Without it, judgment stays inert.
The gap named

"A politician may know that bribery is wrong (judgment), and even feel disturbed by it (sensitivity), yet lack the courage or motivation to reject it." "This gap between knowing and doing is where moral failure thrives."

Evidence

Where conformity and security outrank justice, a society can be devout, collectivist and quietly unethical at once.

Shalom Schwartz on the ranking of basic human values, and Ruth Benedict with Koentjaraningrat on shame.

What the values ranking rewards
✗Conformity, security and tradition dominate in highly religious but hierarchical societies
✗Protective values maintain harmony and suppress dissent and critical thought
✗Moral autonomy shrinks when religion fuses with national identity or political power
✗"The cost of standing up becomes higher than the comfort of blending in."
✗Corruption flourishes in churches, mosques and temples "because they succeed too well at creating belonging"
What a shame culture does to conscience
✗Benedict separates shame cultures, shaped by fear of public judgment, from guilt cultures guided by inner conscience
✗Koentjaraningrat observed Indonesia leaning to the former
✗"what is hidden is tolerated; what is exposed is punished"
✗Religion practised for recognition leaves "the moral core remains hollow"
✗Conscience "usually develops through moral maturity, not ritual compliance"

Implications

The first pillar of Pancasila means nothing severed from the four that follow it.

"Its first pillar—Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa—only finds meaning when it walks alongside the second (just and civilized humanity), the third (unity), the fourth (deliberative democracy), and the fifth (social justice)."

"A belief in God that doesn’t lead to compassion, humility, and fairness contradicts the very foundation of this nation’s philosophy." Severed from them, "It turns sacred belief into performance, and religious rituals into camouflage."

"Religion, when cut off from humanity, becomes spectacle. When aligned with conscience and justice—it becomes strength."

For the young, the stake is not abstract. "A future where institutions serve people, where leadership is earned, not bought, and where Pancasila is not merely recited, but embodied." "The task is not to reject religion, but to refuse to hide behind it" — and that refusal is the whole demand.

What the piece does not claim

"This is not a call to abandon religion. It is a call to stop outsourcing conscience." Howells and Kohlberg are read as clarity about religion's function, not as cynicism about it: "It is scaffolding—not the structure."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Corruption survives because it pays now, and the system is too saturated to punish without implicating itself.

Adam Smith supplies the discount rate; Tutu, Tillich and King supply the verdict on silence.

In a house built on silence, even the walls are guilty.

— (1 of 3) Religion Is Not to Blame
Why the bribe wins today
Smith, in The Theory of Moral Sentiments: "The pleasure which we are to enjoy ten years hence, interests us so little in comparison with that which we may enjoy today." Toro's list of what it already costs is present tense — "It’s the broken clinic that couldn’t save a mother. The collapsed road that killed a child. The job lost because someone paid to skip the line."
Why enforcement stalls
He entertains "A death penalty that truly terrifies, or a policy of lifelong poverty for the corrupt" and then withdraws it: "the system is so saturated, so interwoven with complicity, that no one clean enough remains to hold the knife."
Where the sin actually lands
Tutu: "If you are neutral in situations of injustice, you have chosen the side of the oppressor." Tillich warned that "the separation of religion and morality leads to the corruption of both." King: "The church must be reminded that it is not the master or the servant of the state, but rather the conscience of the state." Toro's own closing is narrower — "silence in the name of religion—when injustice is in plain sight—is no different from blessing corruption itself. And that may be the greatest sin of all."
