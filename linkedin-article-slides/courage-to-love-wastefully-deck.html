The Courage to Love Wastefully: A Good Friday Reflection

On Good Friday 2025 the author published a rendering of John Shelby Spong's crucifixion sermon with a single sentence of framing and no commentary at all. Two days later, the Easter essay makes Spong the sixth of seven resurrection frameworks and quotes the same phrase about loving wastefully. The Good Friday post is not a stray repost. It is the source text laid down in public before the argument that would be built on it.

Opening

A sermon was published almost unaltered on Good Friday, and the framing is one sentence long.

The piece runs to 474 words. Roughly thirty of them are the author's.

Situation

The whole of the framing reads: "The following is a poetic rendering of a sermon by Reverend Dr. John Spong, shared in reflection on Good Friday".

Complication

Nothing follows the sermon. No commentary, no application, no closing paragraph in the author's own voice — an unusual silence in an archive whose other pieces argue at length.

Question

Why post a sermon on Good Friday and say nothing about it?

Answer

Two days later, on Easter Sunday, the author published a seven-framework essay in which Spong is framework VI, and quoted the same phrase about wastefulness. The silence here is the setup, not an omission.

The Reading

Spong locates glory in the moment of total self-giving.

The sermon's central move is Johannine, and it inverts what glorification usually means.

In the sermon's reading of John, the moment of Jesus' glorification is the moment he gives himself away entirely while still loving the people taking his life from him. Spong calls it "a new kind of humanity" and "a new vision" — glory located not in vindication or rescue, but in the refusal to withdraw love under maximum provocation.

The sermon then runs the same structure five times, each time naming an injury and the person who inflicted it. "His was a humanity that could be denied, and he loved the denier." Betrayed, and he loved the betrayer. Forsaken, tortured, killed — and in each case the love runs toward the one responsible. The repetition is the argument: this is not a single act of forgiveness but a disposition that does not break.

This is why the sermon can end on the hymn line about glorying in the cross without any triumphalism. What is being gloried in is not a victory over enemies but a capacity that held while everything else was taken.

What It Refuses

The sermon defines Christianity by what it refuses.

Spong opens by rejecting religiosity outright — "God does not call you to be religious" — and the definition that follows is built entirely as a series of negations.

What it is not about
✗Guilt
✗Religion
✗Surviving
✗Small-mindedness, and being "dedicated to tearing someone else down"
What it is about
✓"being made whole"
✓"becoming free enough to give your life away"
✓"becoming deeply and fully human"
✓Whole and real people, who accept themselves and others as they are
Why the negations matter to the author

The Easter essay names what Spong gave him: language "not to reject resurrection, but to recover it from coercion." Every item in the left column is a form of coercion — guilt, religious performance, mere survival. The sermon is the demonstration of the recovery.

What Carried Forward

The line the Easter essay would carry forward is the one about wastefulness.

One phrase from this sermon reappears two days later, and it is the phrase the article was retitled around.

The sermon's closing movement asks for a life lived fully enough to give life away to others, and for learning "to love so wastefully that I can share my love with others" — then extends it outward: the courage to be everything one can be, so as to enable others to be everything they can be.

The word doing the work is wastefully. Love measured for return is not the thing being described. What Spong is asking for is expenditure without accounting, which is exactly the disposition the sermon has just traced through denial, betrayal and execution.

In the Easter essay the author sets apart a four-line Spong quotation — to live fully, to love wastefully, to be who we were created to be, and that this is resurrection — drawn from Spong's 2016 Chautauqua address rather than from this sermon. The same phrase, from a different source, two days later. It is the hinge between the Friday post and the Sunday argument.

The published slug preserves what the piece originally was: a poetic rendering of John Spong's Good Friday sermon. The title under which it now stands takes its name from the phrase, which is the clearest signal of which line the author was keeping.

What the Pairing Shows

The Good Friday post is the source text; the Easter essay is the argument built on it.

Read alone, this is a reposted sermon. Read as a pair with what followed on 20 April, it is something more deliberate.

To learn to love so wastefully that I can share my love with others.

— John Shelby Spong, in the sermon rendered here
Why the silence was the right form
Commentary on Good Friday would have pre-empted the Easter argument. Publishing the sermon whole and unannotated puts the primary text in front of readers before the framework that would read it — the ordinary discipline of showing the source before the analysis, executed across two days rather than two sections.
Where Spong sits in the larger record
The Easter essay's biographical note records the encounter: Spong met in Perth in 2008 through talks and writings, at the point where the question of what space is left for those who collapse had broken open the author's inherited certainty. Framework VI is not a survey entry. It is one of the named ruptures.
