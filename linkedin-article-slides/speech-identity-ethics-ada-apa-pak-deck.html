Speech, Identity, and Ethics: Why 'Ada Apa, Pak?' Feels Off in Indonesian Contexts

Lowering power distance and flattening politeness are not the same operation, and the second is being mistaken for progress on the first. Japan, Scandinavia and the United States each keep structured speech while innovating, which means formality is not the obstacle it is assumed to be. In a particularist society the way a sentence is received is a professional variable, and a generation that treats words as neutral is giving up a tool it will need.

Introduction

Two words returned to a missed call mark a shift their own parents would not have made.

Situation

"There was a time when speaking to a teacher, a parent, or an elder came with an unspoken expectation of deference—an instinctive softness in tone, a choice of words that acknowledged the presence of hierarchy not as an oppressive force, but as a structure that governed relationships with a sense of grace" (Parsons, 1951). "That is no longer guaranteed."

Complication

Students now return a missed call with "Ada apa, Pak?" "Their parents, in their youth, would never have spoken that way." "Their parents—now professionals, entrepreneurs, or business owners—would not dream of addressing their own clients or superiors with such casual directness"

Question

"How did this shift happen? Why do today’s younger generations feel less inclined to express deference in speech? And what does that say"

Answer

"This is not about etiquette in academia. It is about a cultural drift in communication norms".

The Dispute

The same change reads as liberation or as erosion depending on what you think speech is for.

The article sets the two readings against each other before choosing between them.

Read as progress
•"Some argue that this is a sign of progress, that rigid hierarchies are breaking down, and that language is merely catching up with the times" (Matsumoto, 2018; Taras et al., 2012)
•The question put to the author directly: "Aren’t we trying to lower power distance?"
•Formality is treated as a residue of deference owed to rank
•Casual speech is taken as the observable sign of a flatter organisation
•On this reading, the shift needs no defence
Read as erosion
•"Others worry that this is not about openness, but about erosion—the loss of a social instinct that once acknowledged the dignity of others" (Ricklefs et al., 2010)
•The author's answer: "Lowering power distance does not mean flattening respect."
•"Openness is not about how casually we speak—it is about how we choose to value others"
•"This is not an argument for rigid formalities."
•"It is an exploration of why language still matters, and why flattening hierarchy in decision-making does not mean flattening politeness in speech"

The Framework

Words have no fixed value, so the same sentence is priced differently by each listener.

"If language were merely a tool for exchanging information, then the way we phrase things would not matter. But language is more than that."

"Words shape relationships. Words dictate perceptions." Following Derrida, words "do not hold fixed meanings; rather, they shift based on context, interpretation, and the power dynamics between speaker and listener." "This is why the same phrase—spoken in different situations—can be read as polite, dismissive, hostile, or indifferent."

A student asking the question "may believe they are simply asking a neutral question, but the subtext of their words carries more than they intend." "It is not just a matter of semantics—it is a matter of how their language is received, how it positions them within the social hierarchy, and whether it creates a perception of professionalism or carelessness" (Parsons, 1951).

Parsons supplies the axis. Societies "operate on a spectrum between universalism and particularism—some cultures treat all interactions the same, while others adjust speech based on who is being spoken to and what the context demands."

"Indonesia is a particularist society—relationships and status shape communication". "In collectivist societies like Indonesia, where relationships matter as much as competence, this distinction is crucial". And the trajectory runs the wrong way for anyone assuming it fades: "The world may be flattening, but the higher one rises in any organization, the more they must craft their words carefully".

Evidence

Three societies keep structured speech and innovate anyway, which removes the trade-off entirely.

"Japan challenges the assumption that progress and hierarchy are mutually exclusive".

1  ·  Japan — honorifics and robotics together
"As one of the world’s most technologically advanced and innovative economies, Japan maintains one of the strictest linguistic hierarchies". Keigo "is not seen as a relic of the past but as a mechanism that fosters harmony and reduces unnecessary friction in interactions". "Despite maintaining rigid social hierarchies in speech, Japan’s innovation ecosystem thrives."
2  ·  Denmark and Sweden — flat, but not casual
"At first glance, these societies might seem to have eliminated formality in speech. However, this perception is misleading." "Employees may address their superiors by first name, but they still adhere to structured professional language. Direct speech is expected, but it is carefully moderated—rudeness or abruptness is not tolerated". "The absence of explicit hierarchical language does not equate to a lack of structure in communication".
3  ·  The United States — informal surface, formal architecture
"While American workplaces often encourage informal interactions, this does not mean that hierarchy is ignored". "Corporate emails still follow formal structures". "What distinguishes the American approach is not the absence of hierarchy in speech but rather its adaptability." "The rules governing speech may not always be explicit, but they are understood and upheld across professional settings."
4  ·  What the three cases jointly establish
"innovation is not about destroying structure—it is about understanding which structures serve a purpose and which do not". "Language, far from being an outdated formality, is one of the most powerful structures of all".

Implications

Speaking strategically is a professional skill, and the generation dropping it will need it first.

"The assumption that directness fuels innovation ignores a fundamental reality" — "Words are not neutral".

CONSEQUENCE A
The same sentence, differently received
The student's question "may believe they are simply asking an innocent question. But in another interpretation, those words might come across as dismissive, impatient, or even challenging authority". "This is not just about hierarchy. It is about how language shapes perception".
CONSEQUENCE B
Ideas do not survive their own phrasing
"Innovation does not exist in a vacuum. It must be communicated, understood, and accepted before it can be implemented". "A brilliant idea, if expressed carelessly, can be misinterpreted, rejected, or dismissed". "A negotiation, if phrased poorly, can result in an unintended breakdown of trust".
CONSEQUENCE C
What practitioners actually do
"A diplomat does not simply say what they think. They calculate how their words will be received". "A leader does not simply express an idea. They structure it in a way that ensures buy-in". "A negotiator does not just state facts. They control the framing, tone, and delivery to influence the outcome".
CONSEQUENCE D
The risk being run
"Younger generations risk misunderstanding the role of respect in speech. If they assume that words are only words, they will fail to see how language is constantly being interpreted by others, often in ways they do not anticipate".

Conclusion

Flattening a hierarchy does not remove the power inside it.

"Some argue that as organizations become less hierarchical, speech should become more casual".

Language is not merely a tool for communication; it is a mechanism for power, persuasion, and perception

— Speech, Identity, and Ethics
The claim the title rests on
The phrase is not wrong in itself; it is wrongly placed. "successful individuals do not only think critically—they also speak strategically." What the article defends is the choice of register, not the existence of ranks.
Where perception decides outcomes
"And perception, whether in business, diplomacy, or leadership, is everything". That is the ground on which the article rests its case for teaching speech rather than leaving it to drift.
The ethical footnote
The article closes with a note that in societies where faith informs behaviour, speech becomes an ethical responsibility rather than mere self-expression — citing Kamali on Islamic teachings on speech as guiding conscience, and Nasr and colleagues on the Qur'an encouraging speech that fosters understanding and harmony.
