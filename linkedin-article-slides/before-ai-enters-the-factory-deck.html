Before AI Enters the Factory: Human judgment, culture, and the ontological work of HR

AI in the factory is not a tooling question. Because it makes prediction cheap while raising the value of judgment, the binding constraint becomes whether an institution permits people to question, verify, decide and remain answerable. HR's mandate is therefore not mass tool adoption but the architecture of judgment.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

The paper begins one step before the use case, with what a human being at work actually is.

KEYNOTE PAPER, first draft, written for 400 HR leaders representing 400 factories.

Situation

"Artificial intelligence is usually introduced to organizations through demonstrations, use cases, productivity promises, and lists of skills."

Complication

"This paper begins earlier. It asks what a human being is at work, what human resources is for, and why a factory exists." Two of those are ontological questions and two are teleological.

Question

The four questions are set against the predictable ones. Not what AI is, which tools to use, which jobs disappear or how fast people can be trained, but: “What is a human being at work?” “What is HR for?” “What is a factory for?” And “What kind of person and society should emerge from the factory gate at the end of every shift?”

Answer

They determine "whether AI becomes an instrument of human flourishing or merely a faster way to reproduce an institution’s existing habits". The question is "not whether AI will enter the factory". It already has.

Ontology

What a person is taken to be decides what the machine will be asked to do.

"The distinction matters." The same technical capability "can serve care or control".

If a person is a unit of labour cost
✗"If a person is primarily a unit of labor cost, HR will approach AI as a tool for removing cost."
✗AI can "make surveillance cheaper, conceal responsibility, and accelerate a decision nobody is willing to own"
✗Production is treated as the whole answer
✗"The tool is new; the factory remains old"
If a person is a responsible agent
✓HR asks "what should the machine do so the human can become more, rather than merely cost less?"
✓AI can "help an operator see a defect earlier and prevent harm"
✓"A factory also distributes opportunity, forms habits, confers dignity, organizes cooperation, and teaches people what kinds of conduct are rewarded."
✓"Every attendance rule, safety ritual, promotion decision, production meeting, quality escalation, and response to a worker’s voice tells employees what the institution believes a person is worth."
Neither half is dispensable

"A factory must produce. It must meet quality, cost, delivery, safety, and commercial obligations. There is nothing humane about an enterprise that cannot survive. But production is not the whole answer."

What Is New

AI is old; what is new is that a machine can act on the words an institution uses to evaluate, discipline and remember.

"Artificial intelligence did not begin with ChatGPT." Turing asked in 1950 whether machines could think, the Dartmouth proposal named the field in 1955, and the transformer arrived in 2017.

"What is new is the proliferation of generative AI: its accessibility, linguistic fluency, low marginal cost, and ability to enter almost every symbolic activity of an organization."

The comparison is precise about what each technology acted upon. "A steam engine acted on mechanical power. A spreadsheet acted on calculation. A large language model can act on the words through which an institution explains, persuades, evaluates, instructs, recruits, disciplines, and remembers."

Which is why scale is the risk rather than malice. "A weak idea once travelled at the speed of the person carrying it. A weak idea expressed fluently by a machine can now be repeated across thousands of reports, job descriptions, performance reviews, training modules, policies, and management decisions."

The sentence the paper turns on

"The model does not need to be malicious. Scale turns an unexamined assumption into infrastructure."

Precedent

Motors entered factories decades before factories were redesigned around them, and the redesign is where the value was.

"The most useful historical analogy is factory electrification, provided we tell it accurately."

1880s–90s
The machinery changes before the factory does
Manufacturers "initially placed them inside production systems designed for steam power". "Replacing the steam engine with an electric motor did not automatically change the logic of the factory."
Later
The layout follows the work, not the shaft
"The larger gains came when firms reorganized the factory around distributed electric power." "Layouts could follow the sequence of work." Supervision, material flow, maintenance and building design changed with the source of power.
to 1930
Three to four decades, stated carefully
"So I would not tell this audience that every factory took exactly 35 years to adopt electricity. The historical record is more uneven than that." The defensible claim is that broad diffusion and the complementary redesign took roughly three to four decades.
Now
The electric-motor mistake, repeated
Firms "buy licenses, train people to prompt, and place a conversational model inside approval chains, performance systems, meeting habits, and data structures built for another age".
Where the analogy stops

"Yet AI is not simply electricity repeated." Electricity "altered where machines could stand". This one "alters who appears to know, who is allowed to decide, and where accountability sits", which makes the organizational redesign "inseparable from a human and moral redesign".

Culture

Artifacts are easy to copy; what resists copying is what keeps them working on an ordinary day when no official is visiting.

Culture can be read at three levels: "visible artifacts; espoused values; and basic underlying assumptions" (Schein &amp; Schein, 2017).

"AI first appears as an artifact—a platform, dashboard, copilot, camera, model, or robot. Management then surrounds it with espoused values: innovation, efficiency, agility, safety, empowerment." The effect, though, "is governed by deeper assumptions".

Those assumptions are stated as questions a factory can actually answer about itself. "Can a junior employee challenge a senior manager? Does reporting an error protect the system or endanger a career? Is documentation a discipline or a burden? Is productivity measured as human value created or headcount removed? Who is permitted to say that the model is wrong?"

At SMK Mitra Industri MM2100 the artifacts were visible: "clean toilets, disciplined movement, workshops that looked like production floors, a teaching factory, and students who could state where they intended to go". But "artifacts are easy to copy: paint the pedestrian lane green, buy the equipment, install the screen, create the program, arrange the visit". What is difficult to copy is what keeps them working unobserved. "The ontology is different," he wrote after the visit.

The same holds for AI. "An AI center, a policy, and a training certificate are artifacts. They become culture only through repeated conduct: whether evidence is demanded, whether dissent is safe, whether shortcuts are rewarded, whether workers are treated as sources of knowledge, and whether leaders remain answerable for the consequences of automated decisions."

A correction the paper makes to its own source

"Ruth Benedict did not offer a canonical list of “seven patterns of culture” that includes technology." Her useful insight was that a culture "selects from a wide range of human possibilities, emphasizes some, neglects others".

Mechanism

Cheap prediction makes judgment expensive, because machines handle patterns and humans handle consequences.

"One useful economic lens treats AI as a fall in the cost of prediction" (Agrawal, Gans, &amp; Goldfarb, 2018). "This does not exhaust what AI is, especially in its generative forms, but it clarifies what changes inside a decision."

1  ·  Prediction
"Prediction estimates what may happen: which machine may fail, which product may be defective, which applicant may succeed, which employee may leave, or which schedule may reduce delay." This is the part that just got cheap.
2  ·  Judgment
"Judgment determines what matters, what risk is acceptable, what trade-off is legitimate, what evidence is sufficient, and what should be done when values conflict." Its value rises as prediction falls.
3  ·  Action
"Action changes the world and creates consequences for which somebody must remain responsible."
4  ·  Why the training module does not solve it
"Critical thinking is often treated as another module to add to an AI workshop. It is not. It rests on domain knowledge, memory, reading, structured reasoning, exposure to disagreement, moral courage, and the institutional permission to ask an inconvenient question."
The asymmetry that drives the paper

"Machines handle patterns. Humans handle consequences." And: "AI has made language cheap. It has not made judgment cheap."

Why fluency conceals the gap

"Prompting can be learned quickly; corpus knowledge accumulates slowly. A fluent output can conceal weak evidence because people without depth may not know what is missing."

The Objection

Training someone to operate a machine is not the same as forming someone who can contest one.

"This also answers a reasonable objection. Factories have long brought technologies into Indonesia and trained workers to operate them. Why should AI be different?" The reply concedes first: "The difference is not absolute."

Earlier industrial technology
•Manufacturing "has always required tacit knowledge, diagnostic skill, maintenance judgment, and moral responsibility for safety"
•"Many industrial technologies can be bounded by stable procedures and measurable tolerances"
•An error tends to announce itself
•Operating competence is largely sufficient
Generative systems
✗"Generative systems are probabilistic, fluent, and able to cross functional boundaries"
✗"Their errors can look persuasive"
✗Embedded in "hiring, scheduling, evaluation, discipline, safety, or quality processes, one mistaken rule can be repeated at scale"
✗The change "is in the center of gravity"
What contesting a machine requires

"The second task requires knowledge and also character: the willingness to stop the line, challenge the output, ask for evidence, and accept responsibility for the final call."

Implications

HR is not a supporting function in the AI transition but one of its central system designers.

"The task is not to “create” critical thinkers from nothing in a two-day program." Formation begins much earlier, and "The workplace also receives people whose habits have long histories.". What HR can do is "select, protect, exercise, and reward judgment", and "stop punishing the very behavior the company later claims it wants".

1
Define the human before defining the use case
"Safety accountability, disciplinary decisions, promotion, termination, grievance handling, medical accommodation, and judgments affecting dignity should never disappear behind the phrase “the system decided.” AI may inform these decisions. A named human must own them."
2
Redesign decisions, not merely tasks
"The unit of redesign should be the decision loop, not the software license." Map each workflow into prediction, judgment and action, and name who has authority to intervene.
3
Build evidence habits into ordinary work
Require recommendations "to state the claim, source, assumptions, uncertainty, counterargument, and consequence if wrong". "It is to prevent fluency from masquerading as knowledge."
4
Make disagreement safe and useful
"A factory cannot ask for critical thinking while rewarding only obedience." "A junior technician who can stop a senior manager from making a wrong AI-assisted decision is an organizational asset, not a cultural disturbance."
5
Measure whether AI improves the whole system
“Do not count prompts, licenses, training attendance, or generated documents as transformation.” Measure instead “fewer defects and near misses, less unplanned downtime, faster recovery, better worker voice, fairer decisions, stronger learning, lower rework, higher customer value, and clearer accountability”.

Productivity without dignity is not a complete productivity measure.

— The fifth responsibility: measure whether AI improves the whole system

Details

A disciplined 90-day cycle turns the philosophical opening into something a plant manager can start on Monday.

"The philosophical opening must lead to action. Each factory can begin with a disciplined 90-day cycle."

1  ·  Choose three consequential decisions
"Select decisions where prediction matters and consequences are real" — predictive maintenance, quality escalation, shift scheduling, safety intervention, recruitment or retention.
2  ·  Draw the current decision loop
"Identify the data used, the person who predicts, the person who judges, the person who acts, and the person answerable when the result is wrong."
3  ·  Mark the red lines
"State where AI is advisory only, what evidence is mandatory, what triggers escalation, and who can stop the process."
4  ·  Run a paired pilot, then audit the cultural response
"Compare the old workflow with a redesigned human-plus-AI workflow. Do not simply add AI to the old process." Then watch what happens when someone questions the model: "Are they heard, delayed, embarrassed, or punished? The answer reveals more about readiness than the model’s benchmark score."
Train through cases, not slogans

"Use actual factory ambiguities: a false maintenance alert, a high-performing employee flagged as a flight risk, a candidate downgraded by an opaque screen, a production target that conflicts with safety, or a confident summary based on incomplete data."

What 400 factories could become

"Four hundred factories do not need to become 400 copies of the same AI program. They can become 400 laboratories of responsible organizational learning."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

The factory becomes intelligent only when it redesigns work around a more demanding distribution of accountability.

"The electrified factory did not become modern because it replaced steam with motors. It became modern when people reimagined the factory around a new distribution of power."

If we do not know what a human being is at work, we cannot know what must not be automated. If HR does not know why it exists, it will count adoption while judgment erodes. If the factory does not know what it is for, AI may make it extraordinarily efficient at doing the wrong thing.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, Before AI Enters the Factory
Technology does not protect dignity. People do.
The paper's shortest sentence, and the one carrying the ontological argument into practice.
