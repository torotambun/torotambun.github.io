Plato’s Myth of Hades and Its Influence on the Concept of the Sheol: Personal Reflection

Sheol was a neutral pit for everyone for roughly a thousand years, and then it acquired a court. Between the two sit an exile, a Persian empire and Alexander — and while the piece traces every point of contact, it never claims Plato caused the change. What it does claim is that myths of this kind are built to resolve a contradiction people cannot otherwise carry.

Introduction

A week-two class on myth versus logos left me with a question I could not put down.

A semester-long philosophy course for executives on cultural philosophy, at the Extension Course STF Driyarkara, 2 September 2024.

The class was Myth vs. Logos, and it started me wondering about the influence of myths on religious belief. The inquiry took one form and did not leave it: "How did Plato’s myth of Hades influence the concept of the afterlife in the Hebrew Bible, which originally had a very different view—Sheol—for thousands of years?"

What follows is an exploration of the historical and cultural exchanges between Greek philosophy and Israelite thought, particularly in the post-exilic period — the transformation of Sheol in the Hebrew Bible, and what part Plato's ideas about Hades might have played in it.

The Original

Sheol was neutral for a thousand years: a pit where everyone went, righteous or not.

Not a place of punishment or reward, but a destination.

Sheol in the Hebrew Bible is a shadowy, silent place where the dead reside regardless of their moral standing in life — a pit or grave deep beneath the earth, where the dead exist in a state of inactivity or sleep. Unlike the later Hades, it serves as a neutral destination for all the dead, both righteous and wicked (Coogan, 2009).

Psalm 88 describes it as darkness and despair: "For my soul is full of troubles, and my life draws near to Sheol. I am counted among those who go down to the pit; I am a man who has no strength, like one set loose among the dead" (NRSV). Ecclesiastes puts the emptiness plainly: "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with all your might, for there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going" (9:10, ESV).

The concept did change. During and after the Babylonian Exile, influences from surrounding cultures — Zoroastrianism among them — introduced ideas of resurrection and moral judgment that were not originally part of Sheol, shifting a neutral afterlife toward one carrying moral retribution and divine justice (Wright, 2002).

The Comparison

Plato's Hades judges, purifies and sends the soul back for another life.

Three differences that are not differences of degree.

01
Neutrality vs. judgment
A court, not a pit
Sheol takes all the dead regardless of morality. Plato's Hades, in the Phaedo and the Republic, is a realm where souls are judged and experience consequences based on their earthly lives — the Myth of Er illustrates reward and punishment according to deeds (Plato, 2007).
02
The soul
Immortal, and distinct from the body
In the Hebrew Bible the soul is less defined, and Sheol is a shadowy existence rather than a conscious afterlife. For Plato the soul is the true essence of a person, temporarily housed in the body, immortal, and seeking to return to the realm of forms (Smith, 2002).
03
Finality
A cycle rather than an end
Sheol is a final resting place with little emphasis on what follows. Plato's Hades is part of a cyclical process in which souls are judged, experience an afterlife according to their deeds, and may be reincarnated (Plato, 2007).

The Contact

Between the two sit an exile, a Persian empire and Alexander.

The three periods in which Israelite thought met other people's afterlives.

586–538 BCE
The Babylonian Exile
Hebrew belief began absorbing Mesopotamian influence. The Babylonians had a well-established underworld similar to Sheol but without the moral dimension found in Greek philosophy. Exposure to Zoroastrianism introduced moral judgment after death, and Daniel — written during or shortly after this period — reflects an emerging belief in resurrection and divine judgment.
Post-538 BCE
Persian influence after the return
The exiles returned and continued to be shaped by Persian religious ideas. Zoroastrianism's dualistic view of good and evil, and its moral afterlife, made resurrection and judgment more pronounced in Jewish eschatology — a final reckoning in which the righteous are rewarded and the wicked punished (Wright, 2002).
Post-332 BCE
The Hellenistic period
Alexander's conquests brought Greek culture into close contact with Hebrew belief. Plato's ideas about the immortality of the soul and moral judgment began to influence Israelite eschatology, and Jewish-Hellenistic philosophers such as Philo of Alexandria show Platonic ideas adapted to a Jewish framework (Smith, 2002).

The Neighbours

Every neighbouring culture had an underworld, and only some of them had a court.

Hebrew belief did not develop in isolation — so what surrounded it, and what preceded it?

1  ·  Sumerian, Akkadian, Babylonian — circa 3000–500 BCE
An underworld known as Kur or Irkalla, where the dead reside, was established by the early third millennium BCE. Dark and dreary, much like Sheol, and it persisted through Akkadian and Babylonian cultures (Bottéro, 2004).
2  ·  Egypt — circa 3000 BCE–400 CE
The Duat and the Field of Reeds as a paradisiacal afterlife were developed by the Old Kingdom. Judgment after death by Osiris became prominent in the Middle Kingdom (Taylor, 2001) — a court, early.
3  ·  Canaan — circa 2000–1200 BCE
A view close to the Mesopotamian one: the dead descended into a dark underworld ruled by the god Mot, with no moral judgment involved (Parker, 1997).
4  ·  Zoroastrianism — circa 1500 BCE, formalised by 600 BCE
A dualistic afterlife, with the Chinvat Bridge judging souls and sending them to the House of Song or the House of Lies. It became influential around the time of the Babylonian Exile (Boyce, 2001).
5  ·  Hebrew belief — Sheol circa 1200–586 BCE, Gehenom after
Sheol emerged in the early Israelite period as a shadowy place for all the dead, and remained largely unchanged until Gehenom developed as a place of divine punishment during and after the Exile — a way to imagine justice for the wicked that Sheol had never offered (Coogan, 2009; Wright, 2002).
6  ·  India and China — Vedic religion and the Shang
Early Vedic texts name Yama, god of the dead; by the Upanishads, reincarnation and karma introduce Svarga and Naraka as temporary states before rebirth (Gavin, 1995). In Shang China the dead became venerated ancestors, with detailed heavens and hells arriving later through Buddhist influence (Thompson, 2002). Hebrew and Chinese belief share a focus on the present life.

The Limit

The contact is documented; the causation is not, and the piece never claims it.

Where the reflection stops, deliberately.

The article's language on causation stays conditional throughout: the Israelites may have seen their concept of Sheol evolve through acculturation; Zoroastrian exposure likely introduced moral judgment; Plato's ideas began to influence eschatology. The contact is documented; the causation is not asserted.

What is asserted is a motive worth examining. Reflecting on the exiles and their evolving belief, the piece asks "whether the acculturation with surrounding cultures influenced their concept of divine justice. Did they seek solace by envisioning punishment for their oppressors, much like the development of Gehenom?" That is a question about need, not about transmission.

The ancient Jewish framework had located justice inside this life: God would reward the righteous and punish the wicked within their own days, as the Deuteronomic tradition holds. Confucianism places justice in virtuous living and proper governance, Daoism in alignment with the natural order. Where belief in an afterlife is less emphasised, legal and social systems take on the work — and restorative approaches focus on healing rather than retribution (Camus, 1955; Epictetus, 2008; Thompson, 2002).

Conclusion

Myths are made to resolve contradictions people cannot otherwise carry.

Which is the answer the piece arrives at, in place of the causal one it declined to claim.

People love myth more than logos.

— Dr. Romo A. Setyo Wibowo, quoted in the article
Levi-Strauss supplies the mechanism
"myths are created to resolve contradictions in human experience." Plato's Hades and the evolving Sheol both serve as tools addressing deep-seated concerns about justice, morality and the human condition (Levi-Strauss, 1963).
Plato knew what he was doing
The article records Wibowo's closing statement in full: myth is created by the ruler with good intentions, as Plato did. Myths, for Plato, are not fanciful stories but necessary tools for conveying truths that reason alone does not easily deliver.
Which is why they have not gone away
In the absence of empirical evidence, myths fill the gap with narratives that resonate with experience, helping people make sense of suffering and injustice. Not everyone is guided by reason or evidence alone, and belief in cosmic justice — Sheol, Hades, or another form — works as a psychological and cultural mechanism for maintaining social order and personal integrity.
