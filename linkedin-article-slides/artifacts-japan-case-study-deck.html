LinkedIn Article  ·  Innovation Ecosystems · Japan and the Basic Values Behind Its Artifacts  ·  21 February 2023

ARTIFACTS AMONG HIGHER INNOVATION ECOSYSTEM COUNTRIES: Japan Case Study

The article argues that what visitors copy from Japan's innovation centres are artifacts, while the cause of Japan's success is a long history of investing in human capital, which shows up as basic values. It borrows Hofstede's national scores as a helicopter view of those values, traces six observed artifacts (queuing, cleanliness, respect for pedestrians, spotless public toilets, public gardens and blending construction fences) to basic values, and offers a seventh, government respect for citizens, through a host family's recovery from cancer. It closes by asking for an innovation index weighted by culture and for at least a generation-long reset in basic values, starting with kindergarten students.

Introduction

Self-claimed innovation labs copy the artifacts of Japan's Leave a Nest and miss the human-capital values beneath them.

Situation

Ryuta Takeda, Ph.D., CEO of Leave a Nest Co., Ltd., described in the article as a very famous techno-park or innovation centre, hosted the author on a public holiday and let him learn the history and portfolio of his movement.

Complication

Many self-claimed innovative labs in the author's country have tried to learn from it, and mostly copy artifacts: similar warehouse-like co-working spaces, similar business models and similar events. The author says "They do not seem to learn the essence of why", and that they fail to replicate the underlying assumptions and basic values behind the success.

Question

If the artifacts that others copy do not explain the success of Leave a Nest, what does?

Answer

The article points to the long history of Japan in investing in human capital, even before the Second World War, and puts it in one line: "The Human Capital Precedes the Capital".

Evidence note

The article names no lab and gives no data on the ones it criticises. The comparison rests on the author's own observation.

Gap

Japan's postwar growth points to human capital, the factor it shares with Germany and Italy, and the author proposes a generation reset.

The author put a question to ChatGPT: what caused the 7.7 percent rise in Japan's GDP after the Second World War, during the Marshall Plan. The answer corrected the premise, since Japan did not take part in the Marshall Plan and received aid through the Dodge Plan of 1949. It then listed five factors: reconstruction, export-led growth, investment in human capital, economic liberalisation and political stability. The article reproduces the answer at length. It is ChatGPT's text and not the author's.

Further reading, the author says, marks the third factor as the underlying common one. Germany and Italy, the objects of the Marshall Plan, had hundreds of years of developing human capital before the war: working ethics, a productive mindset, a long-term horizon mentality and respect for both mind and hands. He gives it as "The Human Capital Precedes the Capital".

For a country wishing to reach the same level, he tends to think at least "a generation reset" is needed: start pruning the firing neurons of current kindergarten students to believe in those values, plus diversity, anti-corruption, anti-quick-rich schemes and, above all, love of humans and nature.

Citing Schein (2010), he adds that the making of an innovative ecosystem relies on the basic values of its region, and that the success of a corporation's innovation depends on its own basic values, neither its artifacts nor its espoused values. In his words: "With basic values like the engine, the artefacts will be reinforced positively when taking place." For a nation, he says, building this may take two generations, and jumping ahead is at one's own peril.

Framework

Japan scores high on masculinity, uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation and low on indulgence.

The author borrows Hofstede's national index for a helicopter view of Japanese basic values. He describes basic values as derived from national, tribe, corporate, work-group, social-group and family culture, the ingredients of individual performance at work and outside it.

Scores high
•Masculinity, 95: one of the most masculine societies in the world, yet, combined with mild collectivism, without the assertive and competitive individual behaviour usually associated with it
•Uncertainty avoidance, 92: attributed to the constant threat of natural disasters, so that anything done in Japan is prescribed for maximum predictability
•Long-term orientation, 88: life seen as a moment in the long history of humanity, with people guided by virtues and practical good examples
Intermediate or low
•Power distance, 54: an intermediate score, with awareness of hierarchical position in any social setting, though less hierarchical than most other Asian cultures
•Individualism, 46: collectivist traits such as group harmony above individual opinion and an intense sense of shame for losing face, though less collectivist than her Asian neighbours
•Indulgence, 42: a culture of restraint, with less emphasis on leisure time and control over the gratification of desires
What the visits since 1988 show

The author reports that masculinity, uncertainty avoidance and long-term orientation remain high and indulgence remains low across his visits, while digital waves and the pandemic have moved power distance and individualism a touch.

Field Notes

Six observed artifacts, from queuing to fences, are each traced to a basic value.

The author first came to Tokyo in 1988 as an exchange student and has returned for work over the years. Each artifact below is his observation, followed by the basic value he reads behind it.

1  ·  Queuing and respect for others' space
In 1988 the author saw a mass performance of discipline and love of nature: queuing, priority for the needy, respect for teachers and for the space of others, and time kept like an atomic clock. On later visits people still queue and still refrain from talking loudly on phones in buses and trains. He reads these as indicators of a restrained, low-indulgence culture.
2  ·  Cleanliness, seen in segregated garbage collection points
On daily walks he found collection centres every few blocks, with organic, burnable and non-burnable waste separated. He presents cleaning as an ascetic practice that cultivates the mind, and not being wasteful as a spirit of gratitude towards objects (Matsumoto, 2018). He links discipline to innovation: most innovative countries are clean, and Japan ranks 13 of 132.
3  ·  Respect for pedestrians
In Tokyo he found sidewalks free of motorcycles, parked cars and scooters, and treated as a sacred place for practising mindfulness. From more than 50 countries visited, he says that those that respect pedestrians have citizens who are well taken care of. He read a set of nine pedestrian rules on his 12 km walk and would add a tenth, from Thich Nhat Hanh, on walking with attention to every step.
4  ·  Spotless public toilets
The toilets are clean enough that users relax in them and keep them tidy for the next person. For the basic value, he says he was not guessing wildly when he coined "sidewalks are a sacred space": the toilet is called a holy space in Zen tradition, and etiquette attributed to Master Dogen (AD 1200 to 1253) asks for no speaking and a clean area.
5  ·  Public gardens
Large, well-pruned and well-attended city gardens with a circumference of about 6 km are found in Japan, Singapore, Norway, Boston, Munich, London and Switzerland. The basic value is a sanctuary where one can contact nature, and not a mock-up garden where large sums are spent to no effect. He asks whether the reader's own city has such gardens, and credits Lee Kuan Yew's garden city movement in Singapore.
6  ·  Construction fencing that blends with its surroundings
Temporary installations look harmonious and deceptively natural. The basic value is Japanese aesthetics: fences, not walls, suggesting semi-seclusion and not total division, because the party given the luxury of enjoying the fence is the public and not the people inside it.
Evidence note

The links from artifact to basic value are the author's readings. The toilet, garden and fence passages are footnoted to a 1982 paper on Chinese walls and Japanese fences and marked as paraphrased, and the photographs of each artifact are images that the text extraction does not carry.

What It Revealed

The host parents' recovery from cancer is credited to Japan's health system and to five habits of mind.

The article files this account as Artifact 7, government respect to citizens. The author met his host parents in 1988 on a short exchange program offered by Mombusho. The father, an English teacher at Tachikawa Metropolitan High School, was diagnosed with lung cancer just before the pandemic. A medical device used in his operation left him unable to speak for a year, and during treatment he injured his back and ruptured his kneecap, which brought a year of bed rest. The mother, a piano teacher, was diagnosed with breast cancer while caring for him and lost her hair to radiotherapy.

1  ·  A growth mindset
They all have a growth mindset instead of a poor fixed mindset.
2  ·  Acceptance without self-pity
They humbly accept the sickness and the accident as part of life.
3  ·  Action on what is in their hands
They chose to act on what is in their hands instead of complaining about the lives beyond them.
4  ·  A choice to be positive
They choose always to be positive.
5  ·  Support from their nucleus people
They have support from the people closest to them.
What the author saw on his visit

Both were on oral and herbal medication toward what the author calls 100 percent recovery and had regained their weight. He accompanied them to their first dinner out in three years and their first drive on their own, to Ome City, where the father climbed stairs for the first time since diagnosis. Two weeks earlier the father had renewed his driving licence. Over the last four months he spent four hours a day on self-rehabilitation, ate only healthy food, drank no alcohol and slept at 22:00.

The credit to the health system

The author says the couple could enjoy the best medical care in the world through an extraordinarily dedicated support of health system provided by the Japanese government, and he puts the five habits above as their answers when he asked how they went through cancer without giving up.

Conclusion

The article asks for an innovation index weighted by culture and a generation-long reset in basic values.

The conclusion says that enormous effort at generating GDP, and the blind implementation in Indonesia of models heard from elsewhere, have proven to fail: a startup sustainability ratio below 1 percent, fewer than 7 percent of entrepreneurs innovation-driven, and an absorption rate of university graduates below 12 percent.

1
Weight the innovation index by culture
Design an innovation measurement index based on the basic values of a region's unique culture, and measure the weight of the culture factor with a system dynamics simulation model as a precursor of innovation-ecosystem success. The article says the writing is meant to raise a sense of urgency for this.
2
Reset basic values across a generation, starting in kindergarten
Prune the firing neurons of current kindergarten students towards the basic values the article traces in Japan, plus diversity, anti-corruption, anti-quick-rich schemes and love of humans and nature. The article asks for at least one generation, says a nation may need two, and warns against jumping ahead.
3
Build the entrepreneur-creation machine on listening
Basic values are one foundational institution of an innovation-entrepreneurial cluster ecosystem, which also needs a robust, systematic and dynamic machine for creating entrepreneurs. It requires in-depth primary market research, ethnography and listening to humans at every step, based on genuine interest in others and in making the world a better place to live.

Start with transforming people to have noble basic values instead of simply building artefacts.

— ARTIFACTS Among Higher Innovation Ecosystem Countries, 21 February 2023
