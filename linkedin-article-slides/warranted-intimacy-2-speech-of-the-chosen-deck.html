(2 of 3) Warranted Intimacy: The Speech of the Chosen

A raw, unplanned prayer at a pulpit made sense only after I returned to Elie Wiesel — protest that rises up within faith, not outside it, is the oldest form the chosen have. I did not choose that prayer's shape; Wiesel's reckoning with Auschwitz and the Rebbe's answer to it gave me the vocabulary for what my own grief had already done.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

Wiesel's protest rises up within faith, and mine has always taken the same shape.

Elie Wiesel, a Nobel laureate, on faith, justice and divine silence.

"I have never renounced my faith in G_d. I have risen against His justice, protested His silence and sometimes His absence, but my anger rises up within faith and not outside it. I admit that this is hardly an original position. It is part of Jewish tradition (read: fear of God's people)."

"It has always been this way—the way I pray to my G_d. Not in quiet submission, but in raw defiance, in trembling dialogue, in the aching tension between belief and protest."

Especially after we lost our baby in the womb.

The Rupture

The prayer arrived without planning, and it startled the congregation.

3 November 2024. I stood before a congregation, the weight of the moment pressing against my ribs.

"I had just delivered a sermon, and without planning, without calculation, I prayed—not the gentle, measured kind, but the prayer of the forsaken, the betrayed, the desperate. It startled them."

"My voice, my stance, my very presence in that moment—what I now call my “parrhesia”, my “irony and antiphrasis,” my “pathos”—held them still."

It was not until later, returning to Wiesel's Night, Dawn and Day, that I saw myself in his words — as if I had unknowingly traced his steps, echoing his reckoning, his outrage, his ultimate surrender, not to silence, but to faith in spite of it.

Field Notes

At Auschwitz in 2017, I prayed for a reason I still cannot name.

The autoethnographic ground beneath the theology.

"I visited Auschwitz and Buchenwald in 2017. I have stood where they stood. I have placed my hands on the cold remnants of unspeakable horror. I have wept where millions were silenced. And yet, in that place of death, I prayed—not out of obligation, but out of something inexplicable, something beyond myself."

"The souls of the persecuted spoke to me. They welcomed me, thanked me somehow, and entrusted me with a mission—to spread 'Never Again.'" "I met an angel there. Not the kind sculpted in marble or adorned in light, but a presence beyond words, a moment outside of time."

"As an autoethnographer wannabe, I write this not as mere reflection but as truth, as lived experience. My subjectivity is my objectivity."

What is not offered here

"An encounter I cannot share with those who are too proud of their intellect to grasp it. But it happened." The article makes no claim beyond the author's own experience, and none is added here.

The Turns

Wiesel's own faith turned twice — once toward reason, once through a single dialogue with the Rebbe.

The biography the article draws on before returning to the author's own moment.

Sighet
A household split between reason and faith
"I spent most of my time talking to God more than to people. He was my partner, my friend, my teacher, my King, my sovereign, and I was so crazily religious that nothing else mattered." Wiesel's father, Shlomo, represented reason; his mother, Sarah, faith.
After the war
Recognition in another man's suffering
"I saw the Rebbe at work, and that was enough—I saw him and realized that all the suffering in the world, all the Jewish suffering in the world, all the pain in the world, were on his face. I came back as often as possible, as I could."
A turning point
"How can you not believe in G‑d after Auschwitz?"
Wiesel asked the Rebbe, "How can you believe in G‑d after Auschwitz?" The Rebbe, after a long silence, answered: "How can you not believe in G‑d after Auschwitz?" Wiesel later called it "a turning point in my writing, that simple dialogue."
April 1965
A letter that would not let the question rest
The Rebbe wrote: "Nevertheless, I believe (ani mammin)!" "On the contrary - even more strongly." True faith, the Rebbe suggested, includes protest and grief but leads to an even stronger belief.
1969
"There could be no greater refutation of Hitler"
The Rebbe encouraged Wiesel to settle down, marry and start a family. Wiesel fulfilled that blessing in 1969, and later called the Rebbe's bouquet at his wedding "The most beautiful bouquet of flowers I received in my life, probably, was the Rebbe's."

What I Carry

The question that remains is the one the Rebbe already answered.

For those who endure suffering, injustice and real darkness.

How can you not believe in G‑d after Auschwitz?

— The Lubavitcher Rebbe, Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson, quoted in the article
Abraham's precedent
"Shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25) — Abraham's own bold challenge before Sodom's destruction is read here as the same warranted intimacy the trilogy's first part catalogued.
What the Rebbe taught him to do
"with sensitivity and persistence, the Rebbe taught him how to believe again and how to begin again." — not by resolving the protest, but by placing it inside a faith large enough to hold it.
What this part hands to part three
