Culture and Its Stratification: Schein, Anthropologists, and the Mind's Underpinnings

The article answers the objection that Schein does not belong in anthropology by showing that anthropology keeps arriving at his structure independently. Seven figures are taken in turn, and in each the same three strata appear under different names: Geertz's thick description of the Balinese cockfight, Levi-Strauss's kinship systems and myths, Malinowski's Trobriand fieldwork and the functional role of ritual, Mead on Samoan adolescence, Benedict's patterns of culture, Koentjaraningrat on Javanese worldviews, and Turner on ritual and liminality. Underneath all of them the article places Kahneman, arguing that basic underlying assumptions are made of System 1 thinking, muscle memory, mental models and the neural pathways that hold them, which is what connects a theory of organisational culture to a theory of how brains form. From that structure it draws the warning the whole dissertation turns on: people fixate on artifacts, copy what they admire from another country, and cannot sustain it because the shared values beneath were never there.

Introduction

The hypothesis needs Schein, and the discipline will not grant him, so the parallels have to be shown.

The article opens on the same eight-month hypothesis that recurs across this period, and on the same obstacle: "There's a notable hesitancy within the anthropological academic circle to adopt this framework set by Schein, who, despite his profound influence, isn't traditionally recognized as an anthropologist."

The precedent offered is the discipline's own founder: "This reminds me that even Bronisław Malinowski, often heralded as an anthropological beacon, was fundamentally educated, and trained as a physicist."

What follows is not a defence by authority but by inspection. "After delving into the works of prominent anthropologists, I am startled in awe by the marked parallels in their stratifications."

The model itself is given first, in three layers. Artifacts are "the physical and observable manifestations of an organization's culture", hard to read because they "are the product of underlying beliefs and values but may not explicitly reveal what those beliefs and values are." Espoused values are "the stated values and norms preferred by an organization", the aspirational layer. And beneath both sit the basic underlying assumptions.

The deepest layer, and why it resists change

"These are the deepest level of an organizational culture, representing the unconscious, taken-for-granted beliefs and values that have evolved over time as a response to repeated experiences. These assumptions are deeply embedded, often unquestioned, and shape behavior in ways that members of the organization might not even be consciously aware of. It's this level that's hardest to change."

Gap

Fixating on artifacts is how a country copies what it admires and fails to keep it.

One paragraph in this article carries the argument that the rest of the corpus keeps returning to, and it is stated here more directly than anywhere else.

The diagnosis is about attention rather than effort: "When people are fixated only with the artifact, they are avoiding looking at the “cultural essence” of a company (read: nation)." The parenthetical is the article's own, and it is what moves Schein from management to national policy.

The mechanism is then spelled out plainly. Fixation means "people's hype to imitate what beautiful, works and adorable found in another county into their own country."

And the failure is structural rather than a matter of execution: "They may copy the artifacts but they won't sustain them due to unavailability of the shared values or basic underlying assumption."

Where the same argument surfaces elsewhere in the archive

The Vietnam article, published earlier the same day, reaches it from field observation and concludes that the objects of admiration are merely artifacts. The Echoes from the Past literature review reaches it from Mulder's account of an elite imitating Western manners. Three articles, three routes, one finding.

Framework

Kahneman supplies what the basic assumptions are actually made of.

This is the article's own contribution rather than a survey, and it is the move that connects a theory of culture to a theory of how brains form.

The claim is that Schein's bedrock has a cognitive substrate. Thinking, Fast and Slow is cited for the point that Kahneman "accentuates the reflexive, automatic processes underpinning our actions." Those processes are held to rest on habitual behaviour and on ingrained beliefs about how the world operates, and the article grounds both physically: "Neural pathways, shaped by experiences and societal conditioning, solidify these behaviors and beliefs."

The mapping is explicit: "System 1, characterized by automatic, fast, and intuitive thought, parallels the foundational beliefs beneath conscious awareness described by Schein and many anthropologists."

And the physical claim underneath it: "These values are deeply rooted in neural pathways, forming mental models guiding perceptions and reactions. Such values are so entrenched that they become second nature, part of muscle memory."

The synthesis the article states

"Whether termed as artifacts or thick descriptions, espoused values or cultural patterns, or basic underlying assumptions, System 1 Thinking, and mental-models - the quest is to understand the visible, the articulated, and the deeply ingrained elements of culture." Three vocabularies, one object.

Evidence

Seven anthropologists, and each one's work sorts into the same three strata.

The comparison is the article's substance. Each figure is given a work at each layer, which makes the parallel checkable rather than asserted.

1  ·  Clifford Geertz
At the surface, the Balinese cockfight read "not just as a mere spectacle but as a meaningful artifact encapsulating larger cultural narratives", in Deep Play (1973). At the espoused layer, culture as story: "man is an animal suspended in webs of significance he himself has spun", from The Interpretation of Cultures. At the base, deep understandings "often hidden beneath layers of rituals, stories, and symbols", in Negara (1980).
2  ·  Lévi-Strauss and Malinowski
Lévi-Strauss magnified kinship systems as artifacts, myths as espoused values "through which societies expressed, mediated, and even negotiated shared meanings", and beneath both "the unconscious infrastructure of thought" in The Savage Mind. Malinowski's Trobriand fieldwork supplied the artifacts, ritual function the espoused layer, and the view that "cultures essentially arise from universal human needs" the bedrock.
3  ·  Mead and Benedict
Mead's Samoan observations of adolescence served as "tangible artifacts", with child-rearing and gender roles as espoused values, and at the base a challenge to the Western reading of adolescence as storm and stress. Benedict read whole cultures as having personalities, Dionysian or Apollonian, and in The Chrysanthemum and the Sword traced Japanese behaviour to "deep-seated cultural values and beliefs."
4  ·  Koentjaraningrat and Turner
Koentjaraningrat is the Indonesian entry, with Introduction to the Study of Indonesian Culture (1975) at the artifact layer and Javanese Culture (1985) at the base. Turner supplies rituals and symbols as artifacts, the meanings assigned to them as espoused values, and liminality at the deepest stratum, "the transitional phase in rituals when individuals or groups experience a state of ambiguity and transformation."
What the seven establish together

"Combining the insights from these anthropological giants reveals a mosaic of perspectives that beautifully dovetail with Schein's stratified understanding of culture." The defence is that Schein did not import a structure into anthropology; he arrived independently at one anthropology already had.

Conclusion

The domains differ and the object does not, which is what licenses reading a nation like an organisation.

The article's closing position is modest in form and is what the rest of the dissertation is built on.

While the domains might vary, the essence of understanding culture, whether in a corporate setting or a traditional society, remains consistent.

— Culture and Its Stratification, 22 August 2023
What the parallel licenses
If the same three strata appear in corporate culture and in traditional societies, a framework built for organisations can be turned on a nation without category error. Every later article in the corpus that applies Schein to Indonesia rests on this step.
What it does not settle
The article shows structural similarity, not shared method. Geertz read meaning, Lévi-Strauss read universal structure, Malinowski read function, and these are different claims about what a culture is. The parallel is in the stratification, not in the theory.
The objection that arrives four days later
The Lévi-Strauss article of 26 August opens on this article's closing sentence and then records Professor Laksono's criticism, that structuralism is deterministic and cannot account for change. This piece sets up the position the next one has to defend.
Why Kahneman matters to what follows
Placing System 1 and neural pathways beneath Schein's bedrock is what makes the entrepreneurship argument neurological rather than only cultural, and it is the bridge to the Firing Neurons article of the following year on why formation has to begin in childhood.
