Building an A-Team: The Cultural Shift in Harvard's $53.1 Billion Endowment

I went to the event carrying Yale's case as the standard and found that Harvard is not running a bolder version of the same strategy. Its difference is a mandate — financial return first, impact never as a separate objective — and a culture built to execute it.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Opening

The world's largest university endowment arrived in walking shoes.

A Harvard Club of Singapore morning, in a private house forty guests deep, a hundred metres from an MRT station.

N.P. Narvekar, the CEO of the Harvard Management Company, had not come alone. He brought a select group of senior team members — one based in Singapore, the rest from Boston, routed through London on the way somewhere else.

Their appearance was low-key in a way that registered. Walking shoes and comfortable working shoes, nothing flashy — in stark contrast to the custom-made banker shoes I have watched New York and European bankers order in Paris, on the Champs-Élysées, with metal soles protecting the leather. No Patek Philippe, no Audemars Piguet, no Breguet, no Vacheron Constantin, no Jaeger-LeCoultre.

Narvekar led with a dark red Harvard folder and a yellow paper notepad, the tools of a hands-on leader in transit. Afterwards, he joked: "I only had wrinkles after I joined Harvard", a remark about what managing this fund costs the person doing it.

Why the detail is in the article

The absence of the usual signals is the article's first piece of evidence about culture. What follows — the mandate, the board, the generalist model — is the same observation stated institutionally.

Preparation

Yale is taught as the gold standard for four separate reasons, and I came looking for all four at Harvard.

The Yale Investment Fund case, studied in three private equity and venture capital courses — at Harvard Business School, at Haas at Berkeley, and at Saïd at Oxford.

1  ·  An innovative investment strategy
David Swensen's focus on less efficient, higher-yield asset classes — private equity, hedge funds, real assets — set against the traditional equity-and-bond-heavy approach.
2  ·  A long-term investment philosophy
Yale's emphasis on long-term returns over short-term gains, with exposure to illiquid assets and external managers, defying the convention of staying in domestic equities and bonds.
3  ·  Crisis management under a real loss
The 2008 financial crisis cost Yale's endowment 25 percent. Holding to the long-term strategy through that loss, and recovering, is what makes the case a lesson in risk management rather than allocation.
4  ·  The use of external managers
Yale's reliance on external managers raises the institutional-relationship questions that make the case teachable across finance and management alike.

The Question

Harvard manages climate tech and biotech through a return-only lens, and the team said so directly.

The question I asked at the event, and the answer that reframed the comparison.

With Harvard's investment in climate tech and biotech, areas traditionally associated with impact investing, what factors led to the decision to manage these sectors through a 'normal' investment office structure rather than setting up a dedicated impact fund?

— The question put to the Harvard Management Company team, 16 September 2024
The mandate is financial return
The primary mandate of the endowment is to generate solid financial returns. The team could not be held accountable for managing an impact fund in which social or environmental outcomes might take precedence over financial ones.
Promising sectors, ordinary lens
Climate tech and biotech are promising, and Harvard approaches them through a traditional, return-focused structure — seeking growth and profitability above all else, in sectors usually associated with broader societal impact.
Which is a statement about accountability
Unlike impact funds or family offices that weigh social goals alongside financial ones, the responsibility here runs first to the university's long-term financial stability. Societal benefit is acknowledged as possible, never as the objective being measured.

Governance

A thirteen-member board with no academician on it is that mandate made visible.

The board has grown from ten members to thirteen.

Three sit ex officio: Narvekar as CEO of the Harvard Management Company, Alan Garber as President of Harvard University, and Timothy R. Barakett as Treasurer of Harvard and Chair of the HMC board.

The rest are drawn from investment and finance: the chief financial officer of Blackstone, the co-CEO of Madison Dearborn Partners and former chair of the board, a co-founder of Canyon Partners, the chief investment officers of the Ford Foundation and of Northwestern University, a managing partner at Advent International, the chief investment officer of Jain Global, Harvard's own chief financial officer, and the former chairman of T. Rowe Price.

No academicians serve directly on the board. The composition is not incidental to the answer given about impact investing; it is the same answer expressed as governance — industry investment expertise brought in to hold a fund of $53.1 billion to growth and stability.

The Turn

Narvekar's change was cultural — a generalist model and an A-team — not a new asset class.

Where the approach was formed, and what it changed on arrival.

J.P. Morgan
Risk discipline learned first
A career begun in an environment known for a rigorous approach to financial markets, and for managing risk while pursuing growth. The balance of risk and return is the skill he took from it.
Columbia
The leadership style refined
At Columbia he developed a culture centred on team collaboration, strategic foresight and long-term risk management, and guided the university's endowment through the 2008 crisis by holding to long-term growth rather than reacting to short-term movements.
2016
A turnaround mandate at Harvard
He arrived at a fund struggling with inconsistent performance and restructured the Harvard Management Company around a generalist model, assembling a team of top-tier professionals, many of them from Columbia or from earlier roles.
After 2016
Generalists rather than asset-class specialists
Investment professionals manage diversified portfolios instead of being assigned to specific asset classes — a shift toward more nimble decision-making and better risk-adjusted returns, and a marked departure from the previous structure.

Details

The biotech fund began as a colleague's proposal in a New York meeting.

One example of what the culture is for.

Among the key hires Narvekar brought to Harvard was Erick Slocum, a former colleague from Columbia. On Narvekar's appointment, Slocum met him in New York and proposed creating a biotech fund at a moment when biotech was gaining traction as a high-growth sector.

Narvekar saw the promise in it, and within a short period a management team was assembled around the idea. The fund has since become a success story for the endowment — strong financial returns, and a position at the front of investment in cutting-edge technology sectors. It is the generalist, collaborative structure doing what it was built to do: act quickly on an idea that arrived from inside the team.

Implications

Harvard's conservatism is a mandate, not a temperament.

The comparison I arrived with, restated after the event.

Yale, the case I studied
•Aggressive allocation to alternatives — private equity, venture capital, hedge funds
•Higher long-term performance than Harvard, on the article's own account
•A 25 percent loss in 2008, held through without abandoning the strategy
•Heavy reliance on external managers as a deliberate structure
Harvard, as the team described it
•The largest endowment by size, and the more conservative in approach
•Financial return as the sole accountable objective, impact never separate
•A generalist model built for nimbleness rather than for specialisation
•Biotech and climate tech entered on financial terms, not impact terms
•Scale and operational flexibility held together rather than traded off

Conclusion

Scale, conservatism and operational flexibility are what make this endowment peculiar.

What the morning established that the Yale case could not.

This balance of conservatism and strategic innovation is what makes Harvard’s endowment peculiar and uniquely resilient.

— Building an A-Team: The Cultural Shift in Harvard's $53.1 Billion Endowment, 16 September 2024
Pragmatic flexibility, not a change of principle
Willingness to engage in a sector like biotech shows flexibility that captures growth without departing from prudent risk management. The core financial objectives are never the thing being flexed.
The mandate is what supports the mission
A financially robust fund is a reliable source of income for the university's mission. Keeping impact out of the objective function is, on this account, how the endowment funds work that has impact.
