LinkedIn Article  ·  Mentoring · Ten Arguments For, Ten Against, and a Self-Audit  ·  18 August 2023

The Dual-Edged Sword of Diverse Mentorship: Navigating the Pros and Cons for Startup Success

The article is built symmetrically and the symmetry is the argument. Ten reasons why many mentors help a startup, each with a named case, from Jobs and calligraphy to a finding of nineteen percent higher innovation revenue in diverse management teams. Then ten reasons the same arrangement harms one, again with named cases: Quirky slowed by its own feedback, Kodak dallying on digital strategy, BlackBerry citing conflicting recommendations for its inertia. What resolves the symmetry is not a rule but a published finding, that advisors who learn a seeker consulted others feel devalued, because advisors think their job is to narrow options while seekers are trying to widen them. From that mismatch the article derives its practical advice, and then it does something advice pieces rarely do: it turns the lens around and audits its own mentoring practice, listing what to stop doing after roughly 250 student groups in five years.

Introduction

Ten arguments for many mentors, each attached to a case rather than to a principle.

The article opens on the advantages and insists they are concrete: the recipe for startup success "is not solely rooted in their unique ideas but significantly amplified by diverse mentorship."

1  ·  Perspective, culture and creativity
Jobs drawing on calligraphy courses shows that "unexpected insights from diverse backgrounds can sculpt transformative innovations." Airbnb's discrimination complaints are used for cultural competency, where "A stronger initial emphasis on cultural competency might have preempted such issues." Slack's team of writers and chefs supplies the creativity case.
2  ·  Blind spots, and the numbers attached
"A 2019 BCG study illuminated that companies boasting diverse management teams reap 19% higher revenues courtesy of innovation." And on decisions: "A Cloverpop study spotlighted that inclusive teams outpace their counterparts, making superior decisions 87% of the time, achieving this feat two times faster with half the meetings."
3  ·  Networks, adaptability and expertise
Reid Hoffman is cited for how "eclectic connections can unveil unexpected avenues". Netflix's global expansion supplies adaptability. And behind figures like Musk the article places "a legion of diverse experts, spanning finance to design".
4  ·  Crisis, and inclusion as business case
Goldman Sachs in 2008 is credited with charting the crisis "more adeptly than many rivals, thanks to a medley of perspectives on risk and strategy." McKinsey supplies the closing claim, that leaders on gender, racial and ethnic diversity outperform peers financially, which makes inclusivity "not merely a moral imperative but a bona fide business stratagem."

Gap

Then the same arrangement is shown failing, with the same kind of evidence.

The reversal is what makes the piece useful rather than promotional, and the article gives the disadvantages the same ten-item treatment and the same named cases.

1  ·  Analysis paralysis, at the company used earlier as a success
Quirky's community-driven model "often resulted in an overwhelming influx of feedback for other products. The ceaseless stream of ideas and tweaks frequently elongated product development cycles." Rather than streamlining invention, "the avalanche of ideas sometimes had a counterproductive effect", contributing to bankruptcy in 2015.
2  ·  Conflicting directions, and diluted vision
A tech startup "torn between the advice of one mentor pushing for swift international expansion and another advocating for intensified penetration within existing domains." And early Twitter, where amid varied advice on its central purpose it shipped features that Jack Dorsey "later conceded were “clunky” and subsequently jettisoned."
3  ·  Delay, inconsistency and drained resources
Kodak, where "Amidst numerous voices, they dallied too long on their digital strategy, leading to their eventual downfall." Windows 8 merging computing forms after diverse advice. And the Startup Genome Report, which "notes that 70% of startups scale prematurely, often because they hasten to implement diverse growth advic".
4  ·  Reduced accountability
The sharpest of the ten, because it concerns what many advisors let a founder do. "When many voices are in the mix, it's tempting to blame failures on “bad advice.”" BlackBerry's co-CEOs are named as having faced the iPhone challenge inadequately and "later citing conflicting recommendations for their inertia."
The tactical answer the article gives

Match specific challenges to the relevant mentor's expertise. Replace ad-hoc meetings with structured sessions. Separate feedback into core and peripheral. Measure all advice against the company's own mission. Get the mentors talking to each other. And document feedback before acting on it, "mitigating impulsive reactions to every counsel."

Evidence

The published finding underneath it all is that advisors and seekers want different things from the exchange.

The article's one peer-reviewed source explains why multiplying mentors carries a cost that has nothing to do with the quality of the advice.

The first finding concerns the advisor rather than the advice: "Advisors, particularly those who consider themselves experts, feel penalized when their advice is disregarded. This can lead to strained interpersonal relationships between the advice seeker and the advisor."

The second turns the standard remedy into a hazard. Soliciting from multiple sources "can backfire. Advisors may feel devalued and distanced from seekers who they learn consulted others, primarily because they believe their advice might be overshadowed or disregarded."

And the third names the underlying mismatch: "Advisors typically believe that their role is to provide direction and narrow down options, while seekers often approach the advice exchange as a means to expand their options and gather more information."

The question the article proposes to fix it

Setting the purpose of the exchange before it begins, because "Clearly communicating the purpose of seeking advice (whether to gather diverse perspectives or to narrow down options) can help in aligning the advisor's expectations." The suggested opener is direct: "Are we here to explore options or narrow them down?"

Details

An ancient line about too many books turns out to describe the modern condition exactly.

The first appendix reads Ecclesiastes 12:12 as a startup problem, and the fit is closer than the conceit suggests.

On the volume of advice: "Of making many books there is no end..." is updated directly. "Today, replace 'books' with 'articles', 'webinars', 'podcasts', 'courses', and 'workshops'." The risk named is "getting trapped in a cycle of constantly seeking more advice without ever taking decisive action."

On the cost of absorbing it: "...much study is a weariness of the flesh." Applied to founders who already juggle multiple roles, continuous intake "can lead to decision fatigue and burnout."

And on what gets lost: "Sometimes, amidst the noise of external advice, founders forget to trust their own instincts and insights. They know their business best."

The behavioural biases the article catalogues

Twelve are named, and several explain why founders over-collect: information bias, "The tendency for people to seek and use more information than necessary to make decisions."; diversification bias; naive diversification, distributing attention evenly "regardless of each mentor's relevance or expertise"; the halo effect; and best practice bias, "The belief that the methods of high performers in one context are universally applicable."

Conclusion

The article ends by auditing the author rather than advising the reader.

The second appendix turns the subject around, and it is framed as a correction to himself rather than guidance for others.

It doesn't matter if I have mentored close to 250 group of students, in the past 5 years (not to mention hundred others in the previous 26 years). Never be complacent and act "know it all" as a sense maker.

— The Dual-Edged Sword of Diverse Mentorship, 18 August 2023
How the list is framed
"The list below is a reality check, a feedback moment for me to be a better human for others. I will use this in my next bootcamp for the mentors (and mentees) in my class." The practice described is deliberate: "I have learned to download my past patterns every time I plan to sit and to learn."
What to stop doing
Do not impose ideas on students. Do not give answers directly. Do not make decisions or do the work for them. Do not lecture or talk down. Do not criticise or judge them. Every item is a thing a confident expert does by default.
What to keep doing
Hold students accountable and encourage decision-making. Guide them to find solutions themselves. Offer additional perspective or connections. Let them try, and let them make mistakes. Prepare for sessions, be committed, and listen actively.
The five lines it says to remember if all else is forgotten
That it is the student's project and learning. That there are no model answers. That how far they go is up to them, since the more they put in the more they learn. And that the task is to empower students and build their confidence, or to balance it.

Sources

The article cites one peer-reviewed paper with a DOI, which is more than most pieces of this period, and names several studies without references: a 2019 BCG study, a Cloverpop study, a 2018 Harvard Business Review study on CEO isolation, McKinsey on diversity and financial performance, and the Startup Genome Report. None of those five can be resolved from the text. The summary matrix of advantages, disadvantages, biases and remedies is an image that does not survive extraction. One sentence is truncated mid-word in the source, ending at "diverse growth advic", and is reproduced here as published.
