D-7 Betrayal: There Is Nothing New Under the Sun

Repeated betrayal is explained by the betrayer's intrinsic traits rather than by the trust extended to him — so the question that actually needs answering is why the betrayed party kept returning, and cutting ties is the rational answer rather than a betrayal in kind.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Trust is what makes shared opportunity possible, and it is exactly what betrayal spends.

"Trust is a cornerstone of meaningful relationships. It enables collaboration, builds connections, and allows us to share opportunities with others."

Situation

You extend a hand, share your network, and offer someone access to opportunities they could never reach on their own — in the hope "that such trust will be valued and returned."

Complication

"Yet, once they gain the foothold they wanted, they repeatedly disregard your contribution, sidestep your relationship, and march forward as if you never mattered." And it repeats.

Question

"Why do they keep doing this? Why didn't I walk away sooner?"

Answer

The first question has an answer in the research and it is about them. The second has an answer in your own biases — and it is the one that decides what happens next.

Diagnosis

The research puts the cause in the betrayer's traits, not in the trust you extended.

"This scenario aligns with patterns of betrayal narratives identified in research."

Fanny Lalot (2023) categorised betrayals of this shape under "manipulating or taking advantage" and "cutting ties unexpectedly," highlighting how these actions stem from intrinsic traits of the betrayer.

"These categories emphasize selfishness and opportunism, directly undermining the trust and integrity foundational to meaningful relationships." The breach is achieved through those traits, "leaving the betrayed party hurt or confused."

The attribution matters more than it first appears. Lalot "emphasizes that causal attributions for these betrayals often point to intrinsic characteristics of the betrayer, such as selfishness or opportunism. These traits drive the betrayal, revealing that the problem lies with the betrayer, not with the one who placed their trust in them."

The damage is cumulative rather than episodic. "The sting of such actions is not just personal—it accumulates, deeply disorienting your trust in relationships." Each incident pushes toward confrontation or retaliation, and the pattern forces a reckoning "not just with the individual, but with your boundaries, values, and capacity for self-preservation."

What this does not license

Locating the cause in the betrayer settles the attribution question. It does not settle the decision question — which is why the article moves straight to why the betrayed party stayed.

The Real Question

So the harder question is not why they did it but why you went back.

"Yet, why do we often tolerate repeated betrayal? This question challenges us to confront the biases and personal values that can cloud our judgment."

1  ·  The Halo Effect
It "might make us focus on the betrayer's outward persona—a self-proclaimed “God-fearing” philanthropist or their wealth and success—rather than their actions." The persona is doing the work the conduct cannot.
2  ·  The Sunk Cost Fallacy
It "might compel us to salvage the relationship due to the emotional or professional investments already made." What has already been spent argues for spending more.
3  ·  Confirmation Bias
It "can lead us to emphasize their positive traits while rationalizing or downplaying their betrayals." The evidence is available; the weighting is not neutral.
4  ·  Forgiveness held as a virtue
"Forgiving others is often seen as a virtue, and a desire to avoid conflict may make it harder to sever ties." A value that is genuinely good becomes the mechanism of the harm.
5  ·  The projected image of integrity
"Moreover, the betrayer's ability to project an image of moral integrity can create an illusion of reciprocity, making their actions even harder to confront." You are not only forgiving him; you are being managed.
Why the article asks this at all

"Understanding these patterns not only clarifies our reasons for staying but also prepares us for the pivotal decision to prioritize self-preservation by severing ties." The purpose of the diagnosis is the decision it makes possible.

Framework

Trust survives everything except self-interest in the denominator.

The Trusted Advisor's Equation "provides a useful lens through which to understand trust and betrayal, especially in recognizing and addressing betrayal."

"The equation suggests that trustworthiness is determined by credibility, reliability, intimacy, and the absence of self-interest. When self-interest is too high, trust erodes."

Applied to the case at hand: "In the case of “manipulating or taking advantage,” the betrayer's overwhelming self-interest undermines their value as a trusted advisor, reducing the equation's outcome and leading to inevitable distrust."

The framework's usefulness here is diagnostic rather than consoling. It says the collapse was structural — three components can be entirely intact while the fourth destroys the result. "This framework underscores the importance of identifying and addressing behaviors that undermine trust to protect oneself from exploitation."

The Older Record

The Proverbs describe the same person, down to the charming speech.

"Proverbs from the Hebrew Bible echo the wisdom of discernment in relationships, building on Lalot's (2023) insights about intrinsic traits of betrayers." Proverbs 26:23–25 warns against deceptive appearances; the four traits below are the ones the article says apply.

TRAIT A
A lying tongue
Manipulating through dishonesty to gain trust. Second of the seven listed in Proverbs 6:16–19, which the article identifies as the "seven abominations" referenced in 26:25.
TRAIT B
A heart that devises wicked schemes
Planning to take advantage of someone's goodwill. Not opportunism discovered in the moment, but intent formed in advance.
TRAIT C
A false witness who pours out lies
Misrepresenting intentions or actions — which is what makes the pattern hard to name while it is happening.
TRAIT D
A person who stirs up conflict
Leaving relational damage or mistrust in their wake. The harm outlives the individual relationship.
The warning itself

"Like a coating of silver dross on earthenware are fervent lips with an evil heart. Enemies disguise themselves with their lips, but in their hearts they harbor deceit. Though their speech is charming, do not believe them, for seven abominations fill their hearts."

What the passage is for

"Betrayers often present themselves with charm and apparent goodwill but conceal harmful intentions beneath the surface. It advises caution, urging us to guard against manipulation and prioritize prudence in relationships."

The Response

Cutting ties in response to harm is self-preservation, and it is not the same act as cutting ties without warning.

"When faced with betrayal, the healthiest response is often to sever ties." Lalot's research "notes that behavioral responses to betrayal frequently include cutting ties to protect oneself from further exploitation."

Cutting ties as betrayal
✗Done without explanation or justification
✗Initiated by the party who caused no harm
✗Leaves the other person without a reason
✗One of Lalot's two named betrayal categories
✗Breaks a relationship that was still functioning
Cutting ties as self-preservation
✓"Severing connections in these cases isn't a failure; it's self-preservation"
✓A reaction to harm already repeatedly done
✓"On the contrary, it is a protective and rational action to maintain emotional stability"
✓"It sets boundaries and ensures that relationships are built on mutual trust and respect"
✓"By stepping away from toxic relationships, you reclaim control over your emotional and mental well-being"
The distinction stated plainly

"Cutting ties as a response does not make one a betrayer." The article grants that "cutting ties unexpectedly" can itself be a betrayal — and then says why this is not that: "severing connections as a reaction to harm is an act of self-respect."

Conclusion

Forgiveness frees the heart without requiring restoration.

"The Christmas season offers an opportunity to reflect on our relationships and the values we hold dear."

Faithful are the wounds of a friend, but deceitful are the kisses of an enemy

— Proverbs 27:6, quoted in the article
What the season is being asked to do
"This reflection calls us to discern between true friends and those who exploit trust." The task is discernment, not warmth — and the article puts it in the season rather than despite it.
The line that keeps both
"Forgiveness does not always mean restoration, but it does free the heart from resentment." Neither half is dropped: the grievance is released and the relationship is not resumed.
What walking away is not
"Walking away from harmful connections is not an act of hostility but a necessary step toward creating a life filled with trust, respect, and genuine care."
Where it leaves the reader
"Not all connections are meant to last—and recognizing when to let go is essential for restoring peace of mind." The article ends on a wish rather than an instruction: "This Christmas, may we find strength in setting boundaries and focus on cultivating relationships that uplift and inspire."
