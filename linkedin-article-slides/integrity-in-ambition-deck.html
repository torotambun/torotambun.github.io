Integrity in Ambition: Questioning the Ethics of Funding

Better a little with righteousness than great gain with injustice — applied to the scholarship. Scripture repeatedly refuses to sanctify tainted money by reallocating it to a good purpose, and a university that returned a grant found relief rather than outrage. The harder case is the national treasury, where clean and tainted revenue become indistinguishable: recipients are not necessarily culpable, but they are not automatically insulated either.

Dr. Toronata Tambun

Introduction

A scholarship can be life-changing and still raise a question about its source.

"Imagine a bright student receiving a coveted scholarship – a life-changing opportunity."

Situation

"Now imagine that the funds for this scholarship come from a culture where corruption and unethical conduct are pervasive, massive, embedded. Should the student feel grateful, concerned, or both?"

Complication

"In our world, such dilemmas are not hypothetical." Universities and cultural institutions increasingly "reconsider or even sever ties with donors when money is linked to wrongdoing". "If world-class institutions struggle with these questions, how much more should we, as individuals, reflect on the source of the funding behind our own education and opportunities?"

Question

"It is noble and necessary to ask: does the money that supports our ambitions align with our moral values?" Or might we be benefiting unwittingly from what the article calls ill-gotten gains?

Answer

"These questions are uncomfortable but crucial." The article's own stated aim is to "question without condemning" — to provoke moral clarity "without condemning students who benefit from public scholarships".

The Verse

Better a little with righteousness than great gain with injustice.

"One golden verse of Scripture frames our exploration." — Proverbs 16:8

"This concise proverb contrasts two scenarios – having only “a little” that is gained righteously, versus enjoying “great gain” that comes through injustice."

"The value system of Scripture is clear: integrity is worth more than income, and a clean conscience is better than a large bank account obtained by unethical means."

"It reminds us that from a biblical standpoint, the how behind our blessings matters as much as the what. Gaining an education or funding is a positive good, but how it is funded – whether with righteousness or injustice – makes a profound difference in God's eyes."

The Exposition

Scripture repeatedly refuses to sanctify money by reallocating it.

"The Bible from cover-to-cover calls God's people to approach resources with stewardship and integrity."

1  ·  Stewardship makes the source a matter of faithfulness
"The earth is the Lord's, and everything in it" (Psalm 24:1). "If all resources are God's, then how we obtain and use money or funding becomes a matter of faithfulness to God's trust." Jesus makes it a test: "So if you have not been trustworthy in handling worldly wealth, who will trust you with true riches?" (Luke 16:11).
2  ·  The Law barred tainted money from holy use
"You shall not bring the wages of a prostitute or the price of a dog into the house of the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 23:18). "The principle was that money tainted by gross unrighteousness was not fit for holy use."
3  ·  Even the chief priests knew it
"In a striking New Testament example, even the chief priests who orchestrated Jesus' betrayal refused to put Judas's returned silver into the temple treasury, calling it “blood money”" (Matthew 27:6). "Think about that – these were not paragons of virtue, yet they instinctively knew that tainted money could not be sanctified by simply reallocating it."
4  ·  The prophets made it a matter of judgment
They denounced those who "oppress the righteous and take bribes, and deprive the poor of justice" (Amos 5:12). Micah asks: "Can I tolerate wicked scales and a bag of dishonest weights?" (Micah 6:11). "The answer, of course, is no – God cannot bless corrupt gain."
5  ·  And wisdom made it practical
"Ill-gotten treasures profit nothing" (Proverbs 10:2). "Whoever is greedy for unjust gain brings trouble on his household, but he who hates bribes will live" (Proverbs 15:27). Paul adds the obligation to name it: "Have nothing to do with the fruitless deeds of darkness, but rather expose them" (Ephesians 5:11).

The Case

A university returned the money and its people felt relief, not outrage.

A prestigious university had accepted a generous grant from a wealthy foundation to support dozens of graduate students' research — full scholarships, cutting-edge lab resources, the prestige of a well-known benefactor.

"However, as time passed, revelations about the source of the foundation's wealth began to surface. It turned out that the fortune behind the scholarship fund was built on a product that had caused tremendous harm in society – a drug that had fueled a public health crisis." "The very money paying for young scholars to earn degrees was intertwined with a narrative of addiction, exploitation, and loss of life."

"The reaction was not immediate, but it grew steadily. Students whispered in halls about their discomfort; some felt a pang of guilt that their stipend was linked (however indirectly) to suffering." The institution eventually judged that it had to "step away from gifts that are not in the public interest, or in our institution's interest".

"The university chose the long-term honor of its name over the short-term benefit of money." "This decision was not without cost. Hundreds of thousands of dollars (if not more) in future funding were relinquished."

"Yet, something interesting happened: rather than outrage, many students and faculty felt relief and pride. The initial unease in the corridors was replaced by a sense of moral clarity." "In time, new donors (with less baggage attached) stepped in to help, and the gap began to close."

What one board member said

Paraphrasing Proverbs: "We realized we'd rather tighten our budget belt than know we're educating students with money that has blood on it." A smaller institution elsewhere had returned a £125,000 gift for the sake of its reputation — "setting an example that principle can trump even a large sum of money".

The Harder Case

Once money enters a national treasury it becomes indistinguishable.

The original move in the piece, and the one that reaches furthest.

"Even public institutions with the best intentions, like certain national scholarship bodies, may receive funding from a general treasury that is itself opaque. In many countries, tax revenues, SOE dividends, and royalties from natural resources are pooled without ethical filtration."

If upstream corruption taints some of those revenues — through inflated procurement, illegal commissions or misreported royalties — then "downstream institutions, though clean in their operations, may unknowingly distribute what Scripture would call “unrighteous mammon”".

The claim is carefully bounded. "This is not an accusation against the institution itself, but a sober observation about structural reality: once money enters a national treasury, it becomes indistinguishable. Therefore, recipients of such funds are not necessarily culpable—but they are not automatically insulated either."

"Integrity requires not only examining the purpose of a fund, but also its possible origins. For those who seek to minimize association with systemic injustice, externally sourced scholarships—host-country or university-funded—may offer clearer moral footing."

Conclusion

Grace for people, conviction about principles.

"Yet, we also notice the story wasn't about demonizing anyone – not the students who benefited, nor even the donors on a personal level. It was about systems and choices."

This teaches us an important balance: grace for people, conviction about principles.

— Integrity in Ambition: Questioning the Ethics of Funding
Why means matter
"It would have been far easier to say, “Money is money – as long as it's being used for a good cause, why ask questions?”" "The answer from our story and Scripture is that means matter to God. Using tainted means to achieve a good end is not true righteousness. The end does not justify the means if the means offend the values of justice and mercy."
What to do with the discovery
"In our own lives, when we discover that some opportunity or funding, we received has ethical clouds around it, we need not spiral into guilt or harsh judgment of ourselves or others. Instead, we can acknowledge the complexity but still seek a more excellent way forward." The leadership "shielded the students by making the hard call at the institutional level".
Institutions can repent too
"In a sense, the institution “repented” – it had taken money, later realized the moral implications, and then turned away from that course." "Here we see an academic institution enacting a kind of corporate repentance – changing its direction to realign with what is right. It's heartening because it shows it's never too late to do the right thing."
