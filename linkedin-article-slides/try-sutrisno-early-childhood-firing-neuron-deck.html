Vice President General (Ret) Try Sutrisno: Early Childhood Firing Neuron

A fifteen-minute courtesy call ran to an hour and forty minutes, and what came out of it was not a career summary but a claim about formation. The hardships sharpened him; what made him were the moments someone fed him, believed in him, or gave him a chance he had not asked for. Not every child is given those, and the article ends by saying so.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

A house he had jogged past for years became a meeting the moment he finally asked.

"It was never just another house." "Yet, despite jogging past it every week, I had never once considered stopping by." It stood in Menteng, "a home that had witnessed the rise and fall of post-New Order eras, a silent keeper of stories."

The occasion was a walk with an alumnus of SMA Taruna Nusantara — an institution Try Sutrisno helped found. "Bagas, like so many others, was a product of Try Sutrisno’s vision—an institution built to shape Indonesia’s future leaders."

"It wasn’t something I had planned. The thought had simply surfaced, unbidden, in the heat of the day. But the moment it did, it seemed absurd that I had never acted on it before."

"Curiosity gave way to action. I reached out to Pak Try’s security staff, expecting nothing more than a polite rejection." "Yet, less than 48 hours later, a response came."

"What was meant to be a brief 15-minute courtesy call became something entirely different." "One hour and forty minutes." "It wasn’t just a conversation—it was an immersion into history, a rare glimpse into the mind of someone who had stood at the crossroads of Indonesia’s most pivotal moments."

The Formation

Exclusion taught him what merit was before the war taught him anything else.

"Try Sutrisno's first experience with injustice came not from war, but from education."

OBSERVED A
The school he could not enter
He aspired to Hollandsch-Inlandsche School, "the elite Dutch-run institution that was considered the best path to a successful future." "However, he quickly realized that access was not based on intelligence, but on privilege. His family, despite their hard work and values, did not have the social status needed to enroll him."
OBSERVED B
The school he did enter
Taman Siswa, "a nationalist school that resisted colonial education." Founded by Ki Hajar Dewantara, it "emphasized character over privilege, teaching students to think critically and to serve their people rather than the colonial system."
OBSERVED C
What he took from the exclusion
"At Taman Siswa, Try learned that education was not just about knowledge—it was about identity, justice, and self-reliance." "The unfairness of being excluded from HIS left a deep mark on him, but instead of resentment, it gave him a lifelong belief: true merit comes from ability, not background."
OBSERVED D
Then 1945
"Indonesia declared its independence, and with it, chaos descended." "Surabaya turned into a battlefield, and Try’s family had no choice but to flee to Mojokerto." His father joined a battalion as medical staff; "His mother, struggling to provide, had to sell her belongings to sustain their livelihood." At ten "he carried water on his head to sell", "later upgrading to selling newspapers while caring for his mother."

The Missions

At thirteen the war refused him a rifle and gave him a cap with letters in it.

"By thirteen, war had pulled him in completely. He wanted to fight." "But war did not hand rifles to boys." "Instead, it handed him a mission."

The route
Over a hundred kilometres on foot, without a map
"He became a courier, an intelligence runner, entrusted with carrying classified messages across enemy territory. His missions spanned Kediri, Kertosono, Jombang, and Surabaya, covering over 100 kilometers of dangerous terrain." "He had no maps or compass, relying only on memory, instinct, and his knowledge of the terrain. Any misstep could mean capture—or worse."
The peci
The black cap was the courier's equipment
"His peci—the traditional black cap—became a vital tool in his mission. It was here that he hid crypted letters, concealing intelligence orders as he traveled on foot, moving through forests and hills to avoid Dutch patrols."
The second errand
Intelligence out, penicillin back
"the mission had a secondary purpose—to bring back penicillin for wounded guerilla fighters." "The guerilla forces were suffering not only from enemy fire but also from infections, with limited access to medical supplies." "Try’s priority was intelligence work—but if he was already making the journey, he had to ensure the medicine made it back too."
The cart
He fell asleep and woke at a checkpoint
"At one point, he hitched a ride on a cow-ridden cart, hoping to cover some distance while resting. The slow, rhythmic movement of the cart lulled him into sleep, an exhaustion he could not fight off." "By the time he woke up, he realized the cart was approaching a Dutch checkpoint." "He stayed hidden, pressed against the sacks of rice, his peci tightly secured on his head, shielding the crypted letter inside."
The back room
A family that took the risk of hiding him
"a Chinese-Indonesian family offered him a place to stay." "Many in the Chinese-Indonesian community quietly supported the independence movement, despite the risks of being accused of harboring rebels." "That night, as Dutch patrols moved through the village, he lay in the dark, listening to the sound of boots outside."

The Ascent

He never asked for a rank, and the vice presidency arrived the same way every posting had.

"Unlike many officers who maneuvered for promotions and coveted titles, Try Sutrisno never actively sought ranks. He took assignments as they came, excelling not through politics, but through competence, discipline, and a readiness to serve."

"He earned every promotion through service, never through negotiation." "His steady rise reflected merit over maneuvering, proving that true leadership is about readiness when duty calls, not self-promotion."

His own image for it is domestic. "Pak Try likened himself to an empty glass being filled with coffee grounds little by little. Only when the glass is full does it truly become coffee." "He reflected that many people, despite still being empty, chase positions and become overly ambitious, seeking roles beyond their competence."

In 1993 the offer came. "For Try, it was never something he had aspired to. He had not sought the position, had not lobbied for it, had not worked behind the scenes to ensure his own rise." "It was simply given."

"When people asked if he had expected it, he answered simply" — "I never asked. I was given. And I did my best within the circumstances to serve others—not myself, not my family." "Try did not see it as a reward." "He saw it as yet another duty to perform, another position to serve—not for himself, but for the country."

What It Revealed

The hardship sharpened him; what made him were the moments someone else supplied.

"Try Sutrisno’s life is a reminder of how crucial early childhood exposure is."

The hardships he faced did not break him; they sharpened him.

— Vice President General (Ret) Try Sutrisno
What the article calls the sparkling moments
"But more than hardship, what truly defined him were the sparkling moments—the moments when someone believed in him, when he was given an opportunity, when he was nurtured despite the war around him."
What they actually were
"His grandmother’s bowl of soup, rich with meat and broth, kept his body strong in a time when many children wasted away." "His mother’s sacrifices, his father’s quiet discipline, the mentors who saw his worth—these were the things that made the difference."
The claim the piece ends on
"Not every child is given that. Some grow up without enough food, without role models, without an environment that nurtures values and integrity." "Thanks to Pak Try, we are reminded that resilience is built, not given. That the right exposure at a young age can make all the difference."
