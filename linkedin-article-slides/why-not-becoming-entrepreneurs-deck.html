Why Not Becoming Entrepreneurs, Why Not You, and Why Not Now?

A country fails at entrepreneurship on four confusions it refuses to make: SME against innovation-driven enterprise, necessity against opportunity entrepreneurs, government as a silver bullet for both, and helix vocabulary in place of ecosystem priorities. The teaching failure mirrors the policy one — courses ask the questions of a mature business of students standing at the first stage of one. The corrective is a curriculum built for stage one and a competence model that begins with the heart.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

I see an abyss' deep failure signs, and four of them are confusions.

The lecture opens on its diagnosis and repeats it verbatim as its closing offer, which is how it signals that the four are the whole argument.

"I see an abyss' deep -failure signs where a country experiences: failure to distinguish between Small Medium Enterprises versus Innovation Driven Enterprises - the latter is the one propelling us from the middle-income trap; failure to differentiate between necessity-based entrepreneurs (those who become entrepreneurs due to unemployment) versus opportunity-based entrepreneurs, worsened by reliance too much on government in providing a silver bullet to all types of entrepreneurs despite their distinctive characteristics. Failure to know the priorities in building a complete ecosystem because of focusing too much on the triple or penta helix buzzword."

Each of the four is a category error rather than a shortfall of effort. Treating an SME and an IDE as the same object, or a necessity entrepreneur and an opportunity entrepreneur as the same person, guarantees that any instrument aimed at both will fit neither.

The fourth is about vocabulary standing in for work: helix language names who should be in the room without saying what has to be built first.

Rules of Engagement

Five kinds of hostile questioner will meet you in the room, and each has a counter.

Before the argument, the resistance. "In discussions on entrepreneurship, not all participants are supportive and open-minded."

1  ·  The Underminer
"Criticizes others to boost themselves, often due to insecurity." The counter is not to engage the criticism: "Respond with politeness and redirect to genuine questions."
2  ·  The Self Promoter
"Focuses on showcasing themselves rather than asking genuine questions." Counter: "Answer their questions politely without confrontation." The performance is allowed to happen and is not rewarded.
3  ·  The Critic
"Points out errors and questions credibility, often organized and meticulous." Counter: "Acknowledge their attention to detail and subtly correct errors to disarm them." The only one of the five whose attention is treated as an asset.
4  ·  The Left Fielder
"Throws unexpected questions to stump the presenter." Counter: "Acknowledge the question and offer to follow up one-on-one." The room is protected from a derailment without the questioner being dismissed.
5  ·  The Bombardier
"Bombards with numerous questions to find faults." Counter: "Clarify each question before answering." Volume is defeated by making each item answerable one at a time.
Why this comes first

"Embracing entrepreneurship is like drinking from a firehose, where new ideas struggle to break free from entrenched mindsets, as John Maynard Keynes pointed out. We must approach it with an open mind, heart, and will." The taxonomy is a claim about where the resistance sits — in the room, not in the material.

The Distinction

An SME serves a local market; an IDE takes twelve years and can fail at $970 million.

The first and most consequential of the four confusions, worked out on both sides.

Small and Medium Enterprises
•"Serves traditional markets with well-understood business ideas and limited growth potential"
•"Important for moving people out of unemployment, particularly during periods of austerity"
•"SMEs tend to be regionally focused, offering short-term returns but are unlikely to drive large-scale job creation"
•"Investment in SMEs is attractive for politicians as it allows them to directly support local constituencies"
Innovation-Driven Enterprises
✓"IDEs have global growth potential due to innovative products, but face multifaceted technical, market, and business risks"
✓"Bringing a technology to commercialization can take over 12 years", with "an average lifespan of 30 years"
✓"IDEs in the energy sector face a "valley of death," where they struggle to secure funding due to high capital requirements"
✓"IDEs in energy production must work with existing infrastructure owners who resist disruption"
What the valley of death costs

"For example, Solyndra raised $970 million in equity and received a $535 million loan guarantee before eventually declaring bankruptcy." The lecture also names why venture capital cannot close the gap: VCs "typically invest tens of millions in startups, which isn't sufficient for projects needing hundreds of millions."

The Gap

We teach Stage 5 questions to students standing at Stage 1.

The teaching failure is described as a mismatch of stage, which makes it correctable rather than merely regrettable.

The wrong focus is named precisely: "Emphasis on Stage 5 mature businesses wrongly assumes students are ready to defend against competitors and find new markets, leading to incorrect key questions." Those questions are about sustaining profitability against competition, operating margin, return on capital — "Prioritizing numbers over narrative."

What a stage-one venture actually faces is different in kind: "Focus on finding a business idea that meets an unmet market need." And the questions that follow are existential rather than financial — "Is there a market for the product or service? How big is that market? Will you survive?"

The measures change with the stage. "Metrics and measures include market size, cash on hand, and access to capital. At this stage, it's not about numbers but narrative." The lecture is blunt about where the real danger lies: "macro delusions where companies are collectively overpriced, given market size."

Behind the mismatch is a curriculum gap: "There's a need for a curriculum that matches the stage and dynamics of entrepreneurship, embodying the spirit of a pirate and the skill of a navy seal." His own answer is a 26-credit course spanning primary market research, design thinking, innovation-driven agriculture, startup legalities, valuation, team dynamics, ideation and the founder's journey — set against MIT's "over 60 courses".

Framework

Competence divides into heart, home and hands, and the heart comes first.

The order is the argument: values and resilience before ecosystem literacy, and both before technique.

1  ·  Heart — values and resilience
Five items, and not one of them is a technique: "Tenacity and Perseverance: Enduring challenges without giving up", "Resilience and Anti-Fragility: Bouncing back from setbacks and evolving", "Guerrilla Skills: Spotting unique opportunities", "Self-Efficacy and Pitching: Confidently presenting ideas", "Compelling Vision and Storytelling: Capturing attention with a strong narrative".
2  ·  Home — common language and ecosystem
Two capabilities only, and both are about the surrounding system rather than the venture: "Building and Using Networks: Developing connections with stakeholders" and "Resource Leveraging: Maximizing resources for growth". Each is mapped to specific steps of the disciplined-entrepreneurship sequence.
3  ·  Hands — practical skills
Opportunity recognition and assessment, risk management, creative problem-solving, and "Maintain Focus but Adapt: Balancing focus with flexibility", ending in "Value Creation: Delivering unique value to customers". These are the ones a course can most easily teach and the ones the model ranks third.
4  ·  Head and hands — knowledge applied
"PMR Techniques", "Reflexive Ethnography: Observing and understanding consumer behavior", the 24 steps of disciplined entrepreneurship, a team and leadership framework, and project management. Ethnography sits inside a startup curriculum, which is the least conventional item on the list.
The holistic condition

"Innovation results from the multiplication of invention by commercialization." Which forces the design: "a dual-track approach must take place" — a track of "technological essentials, scientific knowledge, hardcore engineering" iterated with one of "business essentials, decision-making frameworks, venture engineering".

Conclusion

Ninety percent of startups fail, and entrepreneurship is still a state of mind.

The lecture ends by refusing the promise it could have made, and by putting the failure statistics next to the invitation.

It's a state of mind. It's a way of thinking. It's how you think about yourself, how you think about building ventures, how you think about doing new creative things in your personal life, and your work everywhere in your life.

— Elaine Chen, quoted in Why Not Becoming Entrepreneurs, 3 May 2024
What the mindset actually consists of
"I think that to embrace the entrepreneurial spirit and mindset means that we are being adaptable. We're being flexible. We take risks. We do experiments. We're wrong a lot and it's okay because every time we're wrong about something, every time we fail, we learn and iterate."
The statistics put beside it
"90% of new startups fail, 75% of venture-backed startups fail, under 50% of businesses make it to their fifth year, 33% of startups make it to the 10-year mark, only 40% of startups actually turn a profit, 82% of businesses that fail do so because of cash flow problems, the highest failure rate occurs in the information industry (63%)."
Why teach it anyway
"As a method and mindset, non-hyped #entrepreneurship is mandatory to learn." The case is not that most will succeed — the lecture states plainly that most will not — but that the method is worth having whether or not a venture follows.
