Reflections on "Philosophy for Professionals"

An account of an executive philosophy course at STF Driyarkara, taken on a friend's recommendation and with openly low expectations, and the article is most interesting where it argues against its own enthusiasm. The course's method is what made it land: rather than teaching philosophy as a subject, it attached each philosopher to a working life, so Plato speaks to professional commitment, Hegel to work as liberation, Simmel to the broker and the designer, Marcuse to advertising, Merleau-Ponty to medicine and Midgley to science. Having reported the benefit, the article then concedes the strongest objection to it, that everything philosophy develops can be developed through entrepreneurship, mathematics, physics or anthropology instead, and accepts the concession as valid. What it claims for philosophy in the end is not exclusivity but method: logical rigour, clear definition of terms, and the construction and deconstruction of arguments, which for a doctoral student is precisely what is being examined.

Introduction

The course was taken on a recommendation and with low expectations, and the article says so.

The opening is unusually candid about the frame the author brought to it, which is what makes the reversal worth reporting.

The route in was personal: "Starting this year, a recommendation from my friend Dr. Johan Hasan at Universitas Ciputra Surabaya led me to an executive philosophy course at Sekolah Tinggi Filsafat Driyarkara."

The expectation was low and measured against a specific benchmark. He approached it "with an open mind, uncertain if it would match the impact of my previous executive courses from different institutions", adding in parenthesis a friend's advice that "toning down mentions of these schools keeps things grounded".

The reversal is stated without hedging: "Little did I know, this decision would introduce a fresh wave of insights and novel perspectives, reshaping how I view both my professional and personal spheres."

The course's organising method

The course is titled Filsafat Untuk Para Professional, philosophy for professionals, and its structure is what the article credits: each philosopher is attached to a profession or a domain of life rather than taught as a position in intellectual history. The article reproduces that structure, taking thirteen philosophers in turn with a quotation for each.

Framework

Each philosopher arrives attached to a working life, which is why the material lands.

The article works through thirteen, and the pattern in each case is the same: a philosopher, an application to a profession, and a line quoted from them.

1  ·  Plato, Epicurus, Hegel
Plato on professional commitment: "The direction in which education starts a man will determine his future life." Epicurus on diet and moderation: "Not what we have, but what we enjoy, constitutes our abundance." Hegel on work as liberation, which the article says produced a paradigm shift in how it saw its own career: "Freedom is the recognition of necessity."
2  ·  Freire and Simmel
Paulo Freire reshaped its understanding of teaching, toward fostering critical thinking and seeing the teacher as "an agent of empowerment": "Education... becomes the practice of freedom." Georg Simmel supplied a lens on professions as social interplay, applied to stock brokers and fashion designers: "Society is the web of interactions of individuals."
3  ·  Marcuse, Merleau-Ponty, Bauman
Herbert Marcuse on advertising and consumer culture: "The people recognize themselves in their commodities." Merleau-Ponty on medicine and embodied experience: "The body is our general medium for having a world." Zygmunt Bauman on tourists and wanderers, where the article resolves to choose "depth over superficiality" and engagement as a wanderer over the tourist's experience.
4  ·  Bernadine, Laudato Si', Collingwood, Midgley
Saint Bernadine of Siena on ethical entrepreneurship: "The deeds you do may be the only sermon some persons will hear today." Laudato Si' on ecological stewardship. Collingwood on history as empathetic reconstruction. And Mary Midgley on ethics in science: "To see science as one enterprise and ethics as another is no longer an option."
The one it interprets at length rather than quoting

Heidegger on retirement gets three paragraphs of exegesis. Withdrawal is read through Heidegger's concern with revealing and concealing, the primordial source through his question of Being, and the whole as "an opportunity to withdraw from the superficialities and distractions of life to deeply reconnect with and understand oneself at a foundational level."

Gap

The article then concedes the strongest objection to itself, and accepts it.

Having reported the value, the piece turns and states the case against, which is the passage that makes it worth reading rather than merely worth skimming.

The objection, as the article puts it
•Entrepreneurship confronts value, ethics, purpose and human behaviour directly
•Mathematics demands the same logical rigour and abstraction
•Physics asks about reality, causality and the structure of the universe
•Anthropology explores ethics, human nature and societal constructs
•So the insights are reachable without studying philosophy at all
What it still claims for philosophy
•Dedication to the most foundational and abstract questions as such
•A distinctive method of inquiry, not a distinctive subject
•Historical perspective on how ideas shaped disciplines and cultures
•Systematic examination of other disciplines' own assumptions
•Intellectual virtues: humility, courage, perseverance
The concession, stated in full

"In general, it's valid to say that many of the skills or insights gained from studying philosophy can also be developed through other means: from my interactions as entrepreneurs, from mathematics, from physics, from anthropology." And the position that follows it: "the value of philosophy isn't in its exclusivity but in its approach."

Details

What philosophy has that the alternatives do not is the method, stated precisely.

The article's claim narrows once the concession is made, and the narrowing is what gives it force.

The method is described rather than asserted: "The philosophical method of inquiry is distinctive. It emphasizes logical rigor, clear definition of terms, and the construction and deconstruction of arguments in a way that few other disciplines do."

Its second distinctive move is directed at other fields rather than at itself. Philosophy "often dives deep into the underlying assumptions of other disciplines. For example, the philosophy of science examines the foundational assumptions of scientific inquiry."

And the skills it produces are portable, which is the pragmatic case: "The skills you hone in philosophy—critical thinking, clear writing, argument analysis—are highly transferable and can enhance performance in other academic and professional areas."

Where it refuses to prescribe

"It's true that not everyone needs to study philosophy formally to develop critical thinking or to engage with deep questions. Many people find other paths to these skills and insights." The article leaves the choice open: "Different people resonate with different paths of inquiry and learning."

Conclusion

For a doctoral student the method is not optional, because the method is what is being examined.

The closing section shifts from the professional to the doctoral case, where the article's argument stops being a recommendation and becomes a requirement.

At the doctoral level, students are expected to contribute original research to their field. To do so effectively, they need a solid understanding of the foundational principles of research, including the philosophical underpinnings of how knowledge is constructed in their discipline.

— Reflections on "Philosophy for Professionals", 4 September 2023
Why method courses exist in doctoral programmes
The article observes that many programmes include coursework on research methods and the philosophy of science or epistemology, and gives seven reasons, beginning with the foundation of knowledge and critical thinking: these courses "foster critical thinking by requiring students to examine the assumptions, principles, and methods that underlie research."
Choosing between methods
"In many fields, there are multiple research methods available. Understanding the philosophy behind each method can help students choose the most appropriate method for their research questions." This is the argument the Stop series, published a week later, sets out at length.
Defending the choice afterward
"By understanding the philosophy and methodology of research, students can more effectively communicate their research findings, defend their research choices, and critique the work of others." A viva examines the choice as much as the finding.
What the article leaves open
"the specifics of such courses can vary widely by discipline and university", with quantitative methods prioritised in some fields and qualitative in others. The overarching goal it names is preparing candidates "to conduct rigorous, original, and meaningful research."
