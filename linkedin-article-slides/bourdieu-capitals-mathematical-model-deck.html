(2 of 4) Pierre Bourdieu's Theory of Capitals and Meritocracy - Mathematical Model

Bourdieu's four capitals — economic, cultural, social, symbolic — are convertible into one another, which is what lets advantage compound across generations. Modelled as accessible or restricted, a perfect meritocracy is the case where all four are open to everyone. Indonesia is not that case: symbolic capital flows through family name while the other three stay shut, and the cost is not only unfairness but the systematic exclusion of the talent the country needs.

Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Introduction

Talent and hard work do not explain who rises.

Bourdieu's framework moves past economic explanations of hierarchy without abandoning them.

Situation

Societies tell themselves that outcomes track ability and effort, and that the people at the top are there because they earned it.

Complication

"Bourdieu's framework fundamentally challenges the belief that success is solely the product of individual talent and hard work." Structural advantages, rooted in access to resources that are not merit, shape life outcomes and reproduce inequality across generations.

Question

If effort is not the mechanism, what is — and can it be made precise enough to model?

Answer

Four interconnected forms of capital, each convertible into the others. Those born into wealth are more likely to receive quality education, access powerful networks, and gain societal recognition — three advantages that arrive together and reinforce each other.

Framework

Four capitals decide it, and each can be spent to buy the others.

The interconvertibility is the point. It is what turns a single advantage into a compounding one.

01
Material
Economic capital
Wealth, financial assets and property — the most tangible form, and the one most directly converted into the rest. It can fund elite education or exclusive memberships in influential organizations.
02
Embodied, objectified, institutionalised
Cultural capital
Education, knowledge, skills, tastes. Bourdieu splits it three ways: dispositions acquired through socialization, objects that signify cultural value, and formal credentials that legitimize competence.
03
Relational
Social capital
Networks of relationships offering access to resources, support and influence. "these networks tend to be self-reinforcing, with elite circles remaining closed to outsiders, thereby perpetuating social inequality."
04
Perceptual
Symbolic capital
Prestige, honour and recognition. "It often disguises itself as legitimate authority or natural superiority, making privilege appear deserved rather than constructed."
Why symbolic capital is the load-bearing one

Crucially, "symbolic capital often legitimizes and reinforces the advantages conferred by economic, cultural, and social capital, making it a powerful tool for maintaining dominance in a social hierarchy."

The Model

A perfect meritocracy is simply the case where all four read 1 for everyone.

The article makes the condition binary in order to make it checkable.

"Each capital can take a value of 1 (accessible) or 0 (restricted). An ideal meritocracy exists when all capitals are accessible (1) to everyone, ensuring equal opportunity for success."

Stated that way, the ideal stops being an aspiration and becomes a specification. It requires economic resources to invest in one's future, quality education and cultural exposure to develop competencies, social networks that support opportunities, and recognition based on competence rather than background.

The policy consequences fall straight out of the specification. Redistribute economic resources fairly. Provide equal access to quality education. Break exclusive networks and expand social mobility. "Base recognition and leadership on competence rather than legacy."

"This model shows that success in a true meritocracy results when all forms of capital are universally accessible, enabling anyone to rise based on their skills, effort, and integrity" — which also means that a single zero anywhere in the vector is enough to break it.

Details

The twenty-four permutations are twenty-four ladders — and twenty-four places a system can cut a rung.

"This binary condition allows for 24 permutations (since 4! = 24) representing all possible sequences of how these capitals could combine in society." Each sequence is a way a person might mobilise one capital to reach the next.

1  ·  Starting with economic capital
Wealth enables access to quality education, prestigious networks and societal recognition. "However, in a system plagued by nepotism and favoritism, those lacking economic capital are systematically excluded from these opportunities."
2  ·  Starting with cultural capital
Skills, education and knowledge convert into financial stability or networks. "Yet, the unequal distribution of educational resources undermines this pathway, leaving many without the necessary credentials to compete."
3  ·  Starting with social capital
Connections open job opportunities and platforms for recognition. "However, when networks are hoarded by elite groups, marginalized individuals are denied entry, perpetuating inequality."
4  ·  Starting with symbolic capital
"For example, individuals with revered family names or institutional endorsements often gain preferential treatment." The pathway exposes the injustice of systems that prioritise status over competence.
What the permutations are for

They "illustrate how individuals might mobilize one form of capital to access others, creating pathways for escaping poverty or achieving social advancement. However, when meritocracy is broken and injustices prevail, these pathways become distorted, favoring some while oppressing others."

Evidence

Indonesia cuts all four: school fees, credentialism, closed networks, inherited standing.

One worked example per capital, each drawn from the article.

CASE A
Economic — unequal starting points
Wealthy families secure superior education, healthcare and career opportunities; the poor face underfunded schools and no access to credit. "High costs of education exclude millions from building the economic capital needed to break out of poverty."
CASE B
Cultural — credentialism over competence
Prestige from elite institutions outweighs actual skills. "A graduate from a local university is overlooked in favor of someone from an international institution, perpetuating inequality."
CASE C
Social — exclusive networks
"Nepotism and favoritism thrive in Indonesia, where influential networks grant access to jobs and opportunities." Key industries rely on personal referrals, sidelining qualified candidates without connections.
CASE D
Symbolic — gatekeeping power
Recognition depends on familial or elite affiliation rather than merit. "Leadership positions are frequently passed down within dynastic families, blocking opportunities for deserving individuals."

The Case

When a president allegedly bends the judiciary for his son, symbolic capital has eclipsed every other kind.

The article names one recent development and reads it straight through the framework.

This case epitomizes the collapse of meritocracy, where symbolic capital (family prestige) eclipses qualifications, experience, and public trust.

— Dr. A.R.Y. Toronata Tambun
What the article states
"A stark example lies in recent political developments: the president allegedly influenced the judiciary system to support his unqualified son as Vice President-elect." The word allegedly is the author's and is preserved.
The pattern it illustrates
"The privileged consolidate power by hoarding capital and excluding others" — the article's own gloss, filed under favoritism toward elites.
Not a finding

Implications

What follows is not only unfairness but inefficiency — the talent the country needs is priced out.

The article lists four distortions and then four consequences. The second list is the one that reaches beyond the excluded.

The distortions first. "Economic favoritism ensures that wealth determines success, regardless of talent." Educational inequities limit access to cultural capital for those from disadvantaged backgrounds. Elite gatekeeping in social networks excludes marginalised groups from influential opportunities. "Symbolic bias elevates the unqualified based on status or affiliations, sidelining those with merit."

Then the consequences. "When meritocracy fails, societal trust in institutions and governance declines, fostering cynicism and disengagement." Broken systems deny equitable access and trap individuals in poverty. The concentration of capital in elite groups perpetuates that poverty, leaving the marginalised with limited avenues for advancement.

"Talented individuals are denied opportunities to succeed based on their skills, leading to economic inefficiency and societal frustration." This is the sentence that turns a moral complaint into an economic one — the loss is borne by the whole system, not only by the people shut out of it.
