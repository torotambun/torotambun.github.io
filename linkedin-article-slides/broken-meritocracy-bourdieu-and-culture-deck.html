(3 of 4) Addressing Broken Meritocracy? Pierre Bourdieu and Culture

The capitals are not blocked by bad policy alone. Every one of Hofstede's six dimensions, as Indonesia scores on them, pushes against merit — which is why reform designed against the culture fails, and reform routed through hierarchy and the group can work. Corruption here sits in the mental model, not only in the institutions, and cultural reformation on the author's own estimate takes two to three generations.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Fixing the pathways fails if the culture that closed them is left untouched.

The article opens by naming its object: "and by culture means I mean the shared assumptions, not artifacts" — his phrasing, typo included.

Situation

The previous instalment established that Indonesia's four capitals are unevenly accessible, and that symbolic capital flows where the others are shut.

Complication

"In high power distance societies, the erosion of meritocracy is not solely the result of hierarchical structures and collectivist tendencies but is deeply intertwined with other cultural dimensions that further entrench inequality."

Question

If the blockage is cultural rather than only institutional, what kind of reform can move it?

Answer

Reform that works within existing cultural frameworks — embedding competence and ethical leadership into respected social symbols rather than asking a society to abandon them.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Diagnosis

Every one of Hofstede's six dimensions in Indonesia pushes against merit.

Not one dimension, and not only hierarchy. Six, each closing a different door.

1  ·  Power distance (PDI 78)
Deference to authority and symbolic-capital bias overshadow competence. "These cultural dynamics create rigid hierarchies where opportunities are allocated based on relationships, family prestige, and institutional affiliations rather than individual ability or merit."
2  ·  Extreme collectivism (IDV 5)
"In highly collectivist cultures, social cohesion and loyalty often take precedence over rewarding personal achievement." Talented individuals are frequently overlooked if their success threatens group harmony or challenges established hierarchies.
3  ·  Moderate masculinity (MAS 46)
A culture valuing relationships and quality of life over competition and performance. "This softer stance on competition diminishes the drive for individual distinction and reinforces the cultural norm of prioritizing social relationships."
4  ·  Uncertainty avoidance (UAI 48)
Risk-averse societies "favor stability and predictability, often resisting disruptive ideas or unconventional talent that could challenge the status quo" — strengthening reliance on familiar networks and traditional pathways.
5  ·  Long-term orientation (LTO 62)
"Gradual change is culturally preferred over rapid transformation, making it difficult to dismantle entrenched systems of favoritism and inequality swiftly." Patience for slow progress lets corrupt practice persist as social stability.
6  ·  Restraint (IVR 38)
Emphasis on self-discipline, social norms and traditional values "often leads to the suppression of assertiveness and ambition, qualities essential for advocating for fairness and change."
The synthesis

By acknowledging and addressing the full spectrum of cultural dimensions — "including hierarchical respect, collectivism, competitiveness, risk tolerance, time orientation, and restraint" — "societies can more effectively dismantle systemic favoritism and build inclusive systems where merit and competence truly determine success."

Design

So reform must travel through hierarchy and the group, not around them.

Six designs, each keyed to the dimensions it has to work with, and each stated with the capital pathway it moves.

1  ·  Economic redistribution through hierarchies
"In high power distance and collectivist societies, redistribution must occur through trusted hierarchical structures." Microfinance and community grants administered by village chiefs and religious figures. Pathway: economic to social to symbolic to cultural.
2  ·  Educational equity framed collectively
"Education must be framed as a collective benefit, emphasizing group success over individual gain." Scholarships prioritising professions that serve local needs, distributed by symbolic authorities. Pathway: cultural to symbolic to economic to social.
3  ·  Inclusive networks built through authority
"Social capital must be expanded via top-down initiatives led by those with symbolic legitimacy." Elite networks are used to include the marginalised while the hierarchy itself is left standing. Pathway: social to symbolic to cultural to economic.
4  ·  Competence integrated with status
"Meritocracy must align with existing status structures, where competence is recognized through symbolic validation" — awards and government recognition that put merit and status on the same side. Pathway: symbolic to cultural to economic to social.
5  ·  Long-term orientation for systemic reforms
Structured reforms that minimise uncertainty and match the cultural patience for gradual change, framed by how they "benefit future generations and maintain social harmony." Pathway: cultural to economic to social to symbolic.
6  ·  Addressing restraint through moral framing
"In a restrained culture, reforms must align with moral and societal norms, avoiding overt promotion of individualistic or indulgent values" — redistribution and education framed as moral imperatives tied to collective well-being. Pathway: social to cultural to economic to symbolic.

Reality Check

Then the textbook meets a country ranked 115 of 180 on corruption with a national average IQ of 85.

"But, reality strikes: text book vs context for Indonesia" Five conditions any design has to survive.

CONDITION A
Corruption, ranked 115 of 180
"Corruption is deeply embedded in Indonesia's governmental, private, and community sectors." Public funds are misallocated, procurement favours the politically connected, the judiciary is exposed to interference, and even religious organizations and cooperatives are often controlled by local elites.
CONDITION B
National IQ, 85
"The low national average IQ indicates systemic failures in early childhood development, largely driven by inadequate access to quality education, poor nutrition, and limited cognitive stimulation during formative years." Malnutrition, rural underfunding and scarce early education compound each other.
CONDITION C
Industrial absorption
"The skills taught in educational institutions often do not align with industry demands, leading to a surplus of graduates with theoretical knowledge but lacking practical, job-relevant skills." Where the formal sector cannot absorb labour, graduates move into informal, unstable, low-paying work.
CONDITION D
Public distrust and economic fragility
Citizens distrust organizations and cooperatives after repeated corruption and mismanagement, while microfinance defaults follow from "poorly structured loans, high-interest rates, and inadequate borrower financial literacy." Both make any reform harder to land.

The Turn

Corruption here is not a deviation from the norm — it is the mental model.

The article's own heading for this section: "Ooops, Corruption is within The Mental Model"

These cultural dynamics explain why corruption in Indonesia is not merely a deviation from the norm but an accepted part of daily interactions.

— Dr. A.R.Y. Toronata Tambun
How the dimensions combine
High power distance means questioning those in power is discouraged, so elites operate with minimal accountability. Extreme collectivism makes nepotism socially acceptable. Moderate uncertainty avoidance normalises bribery as a way past bureaucratic delay. Restraint discourages confrontation, so unethical behaviour goes unchallenged.
Why it resists reform
"Bribery, nepotism, and favoritism are often rationalized as necessary tools for navigating complex bureaucracies." That rationalisation is what makes it durable — "This collective mindset makes corruption deeply resilient"
The limit of the framework
Hofstede's dimensions "do not fully capture how these cultural traits evolve into deeply entrenched behaviors that perpetuate corruption and systemic inefficiency" — the author says so himself, of the framework he is using.

Recommendations

Eleven interventions earn their place by being small, collective and culturally legible.

"Recommendations: Grounded in Indonesia's Realities" Each carries the capital pathway it moves.

1
Community-owned cooperatives, transparent and small-scale
Local delivery systems run by community members under transparent rules monitored by third-party NGOs, rather than large platform models. Social to economic to symbolic to cultural.
2
Vocational training for low-skilled, high-demand jobs
Agriculture logistics, basic solar installation, food processing. "These roles require fewer cognitive skills but are critical for industrial growth." Cultural to economic to social to symbolic.
3
Incentivise group-based entrepreneurship, not individual
"In a collectivist society like Indonesia (Individualism score: 5), group-based initiatives create accountability and align with cultural norms." Agricultural cooperatives, shared hawker setups, community tourism. Social to cultural to economic to symbolic.
4
Prioritise basic infrastructure over high-tech gigs
Water, electricity, internet, cold storage and simple supply chains that lift productivity in industries people already work in. Economic to social to cultural to symbolic.
5
Embed ethical frameworks and anti-corruption safeguards
Decentralised ledgers, automated payment mechanisms and culturally adapted campaigns — blockchain-backed agricultural supply chains, transparent cooperative revenue-sharing. Symbolic to social to economic to cultural.
6
Decentralised education initiatives
Technology-enabled remote learning, skills training and certification, delivered through localized digital learning centers in rural areas with public-private partnerships. Cultural to economic to social to symbolic.
7
Industry-aligned job creation and entrepreneurship
Tax benefits for startups in emerging sectors, gig platforms and entrepreneurship in agriculture and renewable energy, supported by public-private partnerships. Social to economic to cultural to symbolic.
8
Nutrition and health-first interventions
"Launch direct meal programs for schoolchildren with fortified foods and health supplements to address deficiencies." delivered to schools through verifiable logistics. Economic to cultural to social to symbolic.
9
Educational equity and cognitive development
Early childhood interventions in nutrition and preschool alongside universal primary and secondary access, with vocational training emphasised over theoretical higher education. Cultural to economic to social to symbolic.
10
Industrial absorption and job creation
Industrial policy and SME incentives to generate the jobs graduates currently cannot find, with vocational training aligned to renewable energy and the digital economy. Social to cultural to economic to symbolic.
11
Tackling malnutrition to address IQ deficits
School meal programs and public health initiatives for rural children, paired with educational reform for early stimulation. Economic to cultural to social to symbolic.

By prioritizing practical livelihoods, essential infrastructure, and ethical governance, these strategies establish a strong foundation for sustainable, scalable growth that can gradually transform Indonesia's socio-economic landscape.

Conclusion

Cultural reformation may take two to three generations, and that horizon is the finding, not an excuse.

The article ends by naming its own timescale, which is the least comfortable thing in it.

"Indonesia stands at a critical crossroads where addressing systemic inequality and restoring meritocracy is essential for sustainable national progress." The entrenched imbalance of the four capitals, magnified by corruption and cultural norms, perpetuates stagnation and blocks upward mobility for many.

"Real change requires more than isolated reforms" — it demands comprehensive, culturally adaptive strategies that bridge theory and practice, which is what the eleven recommendations attempt.

Then the horizon. "Deep-rooted societal norms and behaviors evolve gradually over generations." True reform, especially cultural reformation, "may take two to three generations, or 50 to 75 years, to fully materialize." That estimate is stated as a requirement for persistent commitment, patience and strategic planning — not as a reason to wait.

"The path forward will undoubtedly be challenging, but by embracing these transformative reforms with a generational perspective, Indonesia can foster a more just, equitable, and dynamic society—one where every individual has the genuine opportunity to contribute and thrive based on their abilities and efforts."
