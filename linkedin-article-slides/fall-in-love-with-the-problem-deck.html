Fall in Love with the Problem, Listening and Primary Market Research: Biblical Roots Perhaps?

Answering before listening is called shameful, not merely unwise — and the Hebrew shama means hearing with the intention to act. Scripture repeatedly puts seeing and hearing before any rescue is announced, which is the same discipline primary market research demands of a founder. Falling in love with the problem is not a tactic. It is the humility that makes the solution worth having.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus

Opening

To answer before listening is not merely unwise; it is shameful.

"To answer before listening— that is folly and shame." (Proverbs 18:13, NIV)

Situation

"We live in a time that moves fast. Faster than before." In that constant rush comes a strange pressure to fix everything right now. "It feels productive. Even helpful. But is it love? Is it wise?"

Complication

Uri Levine, the founder of Waze, says something that has stuck with the author: "Fall in love with the problem, not the solution." "It's not just startup advice. It's not just business philosophy. It's something deeper. Something older. Something that sounds almost… biblical."

Question

"And I wonder—where does that come from? This mindset that slows down, listens longer, sits with pain before reaching for the answer?"

Answer

A verse that does not get quoted much at tech conferences. "Read it again. It's not just unwise. It's shameful."

The Verb

Shama means hearing with the intention to act, not perceiving sound.

In Hebrew culture, listening — shama — "isn't passive. It's not just hearing sound."

"It's not just hearing sound. It's absorbing, internalizing, responding with your full self." It is the same word and the key verb that begins the Shema in Deuteronomy 6:4 — "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one."

"It's a call to attention, not just volume. In Jewish tradition, shama implies more than sound perception. It means to hear with the intention to act, to absorb, to respond. It is both cognitive and volitional—a form of listening that demands engagement."

"So what does it say about us when we answer too soon? When we diagnose too quickly? When we assume we've heard enough, felt enough, understood enough?"

The Pattern

Scripture models seeing and hearing before any rescue is announced.

"The Good Book gives us a different model."

In Genesis 6 the world is corrupt and violence fills the earth. Before any action: "The Lord saw how great the wickedness of the human race had become… and his heart was deeply troubled" (Genesis 6:5–6, NIV). "God didn't just punish. He grieved. He felt. He sat with it."

In Exodus 3, at the burning bush: "I have indeed seen the misery of my people in Egypt. I have heard them crying out… I am concerned about their suffering" (Exodus 3:7, NIV). Only after that does he say, "So I have come down to rescue them" (Exodus 3:8a, NIV).

"Only after seeing. Only after hearing. Only after caring. That's the pattern. That's the God of Israel—not a God who rushes, but a God who listens deeply and acts carefully."

Isaiah 1:17 gives the same ordering: "Learn to do right; seek justice. Defend the oppressed" (NIV). "Notice the order: learn first. Don't act before you learn. Don't solve before you see. Justice without learning is violence wearing a suit."

The Posture

Falling in love with the problem requires humility that solutions flatter away.

"Uri Levine didn't say “solve the problem.” He said “fall in love with it.” That's a different posture."

Falling for the Solution
✗"Solutions can flatter the ego."
✗Building before the pain has been verified
✗"The startup graveyard is full of brilliant solutions that were never anchored in verified pain."
✗Speed treated as productivity, and productivity as help
✗Diagnosing quickly, and assuming enough has been understood
Falling for the Problem
✓"But falling in love with the problem? That requires humility."
✓"It means we have to get close. Close enough to feel. Close enough to be wrong. Close enough to be changed."
✓"That's harder. That's slower. But maybe that's the point."
✓Interviewing customers directly, over and over, before building
✓"Not rushing to fill silence. But learning to stay in it."
Where the operational discipline comes from

Bill Aulet, in Disciplined Entrepreneurship, "insists that no startup should begin building before engaging in primary market research." "He outlines how founders must interview customers directly—over and over—because what people say they want and what they're actually willing to pay for are rarely the same." The author learned it in practice under Prof. Patricia Cotter at MIT, who "emphasized that listening isn't passive; it's disciplined inquiry".

The Question

The Talmud is built on questions, and Passover begins with a child asking.

"In Jewish tradition, learning often begins with asking."

"That's why the Talmud is built on questions, not just answers." "That's why the Passover meal begins not with a teaching, but with a child asking: “Why is this night different from all other nights?”"

"This culture of asking has never been merely ceremonial." "Peter Drucker often reminded leaders that the most important and difficult job is to ask the right question."

"Hal Gregersen of MIT expands this further in his book Questions Are the Answer, where he argues that breakthroughs don't begin with answers—but with better questions. The very discipline of pausing to ask—intentionally, vulnerably, and iteratively—is part of what distinguishes true innovators from fast executors."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Listening, done right, is the first act of entrepreneurial faith.

"So here's a confession. The hard part is, falling in love with the problem makes us slow down. And that's not popular."

Because love doesn't skip steps. Love doesn't rush. Love lingers. Love pays attention.

— Fall in Love with the Problem, Listening and Primary Market Research
The author's own reflective statement
"Listening, done right, is the first act of entrepreneurial faith." Offered as his formulation rather than as a finding — and it is the sentence the whole piece turns on.
Why the method might be older than the method
"Which makes one wonder—maybe primary market research is not just a business tactic. Maybe it's biblical. Because long before founders were told to validate pain through interviews, the Hebrew Scriptures already modeled something deeper: that listening comes before action. That presence precedes solution. That observation is holy."
What slowing down might actually be
"But maybe… it's holy. Maybe it's God-like. Maybe real wisdom is learning to be okay with waiting. With watching. With seeing what's really happening under the surface." "And maybe, just maybe—that's when healing starts."
