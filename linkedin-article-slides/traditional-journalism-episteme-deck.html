Traditional Journalism: Trustworthy Anchor in the Digital Tide of Elections

A morning newspaper ritual is the entry point to an epistemological claim rather than a nostalgic one. The article's argument is that a newspaper's layered editorial process pushes it toward episteme, meaning knowledge that has survived scrutiny, while the undifferentiated digital stream blurs the line between that and doxa, the taken-for-granted. Its prescription is not retreat into print. Mainstream media are asked to use doxa's own instruments, the short segment and the catchy headline, as a bridge to substantial content. And the stake is dated: an election is when the distinction between opinion and knowledge stops being an academic matter.

Opening

A newspaper at six in the morning is a ritual, and the ritual is doing cognitive work.

The article opens on a recurring hotel stay and a crisp newspaper over breakfast, and it is careful that the appeal is not only sentimental. The tactile experience is set against what it calls the sterile swipes of a screen: grainy texture underneath the fingers, the rustle of a turning page.

The claim attached to that is empirical rather than aesthetic. The article reports that studies have indicated tactile engagement may play a role in bolstering comprehension and in retaining what is consumed, which converts a pleasure into a mechanism.

Two further effects are named. The absence of blue light means no eye strain and no disruption to the body's internal rhythms; and the reassuring predictability of a paper waiting at six in the morning supplies a routine, which the article treats as an anchor setting the tone of the day.

The register in which the article holds all this is deliberate. Reading a newspaper is described as a meditation in mindfulness, commanding a deliberation that digital reading does not, at a pace it calls more human and less mechanised.

What the ritual also is

"For someone like me, a newspaper is a time machine." The article adds that it transports him to a period before the digital avalanche, when information was savoured rather than consumed.

Details

Finite pages bound attention where the endless scroll does not.

The article's sharpest structural observation is that a newspaper's principal limitation is also its advantage, and it sets the two media against each other on that basis.

The newspaper
•A finite number of pages, and therefore an end
•No pop-ups competing for attention
•Room for the mind to dwell and engage deeply
•Lower cognitive load, no multitasking
•Each story demands its due, in sequence
The digital stream
•An endless scroll with no boundary
•Persistent pings and notifications
•Flitting between one item and the next
•Cognitive load of continuous multitasking
•Immediacy and brevity as the default
The paradox the article names

"The finite nature of its pages offers a boundary that digital platforms, with their endless scroll, don't." Its reading is that this limitation, paradoxically, becomes the newspaper's strength.

The Claim

Editorial layers are what make a newspaper lean toward episteme rather than doxa.

Having established the reading experience, the article moves to the production process, and this is where the argument actually sits. Well-established newspapers come with a legacy and, more importantly, with rigorous editing involving multiple layers of checks.

What those layers produce is a specific property: information that has been vetted, verified and viewed from several angles before it reaches a reader. The article sets that against the immediacy and brevity of many online sources, which is a difference in process rather than in medium.

The second effect is on the reader rather than the text. A daily or weekly publication permits what the article calls reflective consumption, allowing a reader to sit back, digest and reflect rather than absorb a constant barrage of updates.

The article is careful not to overclaim. No source is entirely free of bias or opinion, and its position is that the structured editorial process tends toward episteme rather than guaranteeing it, with reputations hinging on reliability.

What it is not arguing

The article does not dismiss social media. Its position is that balancing such platforms with vetted sources produces a more rounded perspective, and that incorporating traditional news is a conscious choice for depth and credibility rather than a nod to the past.

Framework

Foucault's episteme is an era's foundation of knowledge; Bourdieu's doxa is what a society wears without noticing.

The article traces both terms to ancient Greek philosophy and then to the two modern thinkers who reworked them.

Episteme
•Knowledge, understanding and science, in the Greek sense
•Often paralleled to justified true belief
•Has weathered rigorous scrutiny and defends itself on rational grounds
•Anchored in evidence, reason and systematic understanding
•Foucault: the conceptual foundation of knowledge in each era
Doxa
•Common beliefs or opinions favoured among the masses
•May not have undergone rigorous verification
•Exists in the realm of the taken-for-granted
•Persists because popularly accepted, not because examined
•Bourdieu: beliefs a society wears as spectacles without seeing them
The contemporary form of episteme

The article points to the scientific method as capturing its spirit: a structured approach to inquiry with a firm emphasis on evidence-based conclusions.

Why doxa is hard to challenge

On the article's reading of Bourdieu, doxa becomes an unspoken consensus that rarely faces challenge, precisely because it is not recognised as belief but accepted as self-evident truth.

Evidence

Indonesian professors stay off the platforms where their global peers publish.

The article's one worked case is a seasoned executive who returned to Indonesia after a period overseas, and who keeps both the newspaper ritual and an active presence on professional platforms. The deck refers to her by role rather than by name.

The contrast she reports is institutional. At the Ivy League institutions where she studied, LinkedIn was part of the scholarly ecosystem: professors wrote their thoughts there and shared research, so that academia met the wider world rather than remaining in journals.

In Indonesia the article finds the opposite, and it is careful that this is not a deficit of knowledge. The academic culture is rich and the knowledge plentiful; what is absent is the presence on the platform.

Her response is what the article holds up. She treats the platform as a global stage where intellectuals from premier institutions gather to share and debate, and set out to bring the depth of Indonesian academia to that forum, in the hope that others would follow.

The observation, as written

"Professors, while revered for their expertise, seldom venture into this digital professional sphere." The article calls the contrast reflective of how differently academic cultures perceive and interact with digital platforms.

Implications

Mainstream media must use doxa's tools to carry people to episteme, not merely move online.

The article's prescription is the opposite of retreat, and it is aimed at the institutions rather than at readers. The challenge for traditional newspapers is not to transition online but to adapt, evolve and compete in a stream that contests attention every second.

The instruments it names are the ones usually blamed for the problem. Quick hits, catchy headlines and trending formats are to be used not for sensationalism but as a bridge to substantial, well-researched content.

Its worked example is specific enough to build. A two-minute news segment that draws an audience with the appeal of bite-sized content and, by its end, delivers deeper insight.

The responsibility that goes with the role is stated plainly: mainstream outlets are described as guardians of authentic information, which is why the article asks them to harness doxa's appeal rather than disdain it.

The instruction

Mainstream media outlets are the guardians of authentic information, the article holds, "hence they must harness the appeal of" doxa to guide audiences toward genuine knowledge.

The Condition

Digital readiness is socio-cultural, and copying another country's model without tailoring it invites confusion.

The article separates two things usually conflated, and the separation carries its policy weight. A society is not digitally prepared because internet access is widespread or smartphones are common.

What readiness actually requires is a change in what a population does with what it receives: discerning the content, understanding it, and using it for progress rather than consuming it.

The precondition it names is human capital, and the article's list is unglamorous: people who are well-fed, educated, and led by leaders with vision. Digital readiness is placed downstream of nutrition and schooling.

The article then makes an admission most treatments avoid, and treats it as productive. Lagging behind other nations is no cause for shame; it is a starting point, and the error would be to import a model built elsewhere without fitting it to a different socio-cultural fabric.

What readiness means

"True digital readiness is achieved when a population is not just consuming digital content but discerning it, understanding it, and utilizing it for progress."

The warning against imitation

"Blindly emulating digital models from other countries, without tailoring them to our unique socio-cultural fabric, is a recipe for further confusion and potential pitfalls."

Conclusion

An election is when the distinction stops being academic.

The article closes by naming the occasion that makes the whole argument urgent, and it names a date.

In the din of election campaigns, the clear, reasoned voice of trustworthy journalism is not just valuable; it's indispensable.

— Traditional Journalism: Trustworthy Anchor in the Digital Tide of Elections, 21 October 2023
What an election does to an information system
The article describes it as a hotbed for misinformation, with stakes high and emotions higher, where power dynamics shift and vested interests inundate the public sphere with half-truths and misleading narratives.
What the role becomes
Beyond disseminating information, established newspapers and credible journalistic entities are cast as the compass by which a society finds its way through propaganda.
The test for a citizen
To value popular opinion with a discerning eye, understanding its limitations, while remaining vigilant and seeking verified knowledge. The article treats holding both as the actual skill.
Where the argument began
A newspaper at six in the morning. The article's claim is that a habit which looks like nostalgia is a practice in distinguishing what has been checked from what has merely been repeated.
