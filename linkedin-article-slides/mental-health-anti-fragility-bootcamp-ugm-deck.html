A Reflection on Mental Health and Anti-Fragility: Learning from Innovation Bootcamp of Microbiology Department Faperta UGM

Three colleagues asked, in three different forms, how to support mental health without licensing avoidance. The answer the bootcamp produced was not a position on the question but a change of practice: recognise early, protect after the fall, support just beyond current ability — and then slow the teaching down rather than asking the students to speed up.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A fellow teacher asked me how to support mental health without endorsing fragility.

A question raised in conversation that did not go away.

Situation

Mental health is undeniably important, and everyone should have access to the support they need. I also believe in cultivating resilience, where challenges are seen as opportunities for growth rather than only as obstacles.

Complication

A fellow teacher put it directly: "So many people run to mental health as an excuse. While I am supporting them, I don't endorse the weak and non-anti-fragile mentality. How do I explain this without sounding too opposing of the mental health hype?"

Question

How does a teacher hold both — real support for those who need it, and a refusal to let the language of support become a way of avoiding difficulty?

Answer

I responded: "While I fully support those who seek help for mental health, I also encourage a perspective that embraces adversity as a catalyst for personal strength." The second position does not diminish the first; it complements it.

The Questions

Two more colleagues brought me the same worry in different words.

Not one teacher's opinion — a pattern arriving from several directions at once.

1  ·  A group of teachers, on volume
"The prevalence of students claiming mental health problems is too inundating. If I think back, I might have suffered mental health too. But if I gave up, I would not reach where I am now. So, what is your advice to us about addressing mental health?" Their concern resonated: the rising emphasis on mental health might be affecting students' ability to confront challenges at all.
2  ·  What I suggested to them
"Recognize that mental health is a real concern, but it's also crucial to encourage students not to see their struggles as insurmountable barriers. Everyone faces difficulties, and while seeking help is important, fostering resilience is equally vital."
3  ·  Another educator, on counselling
"Now students go to counselors, and they become immune to difficult tasks. It is unfair and not educating, I think." Counselling is valuable, especially where students face genuine mental health challenges — and the risk named here is real.
4  ·  Where I drew the line
"It's crucial that this support doesn't inadvertently lead to students avoiding difficult tasks or becoming less resilient." Education should involve not only support but teaching students how to face challenges head-on and grow from them. "We should help them develop the skills and mindset to persevere through tough situations," I concluded, "rather than allowing them to avoid challenges altogether."

Framework

Anti-fragility names what they are asking for: strength that comes from stress itself.

A term coined by Nassim Taleb for systems or individuals that become stronger through stress and adversity.

The conversation made me reflect on my own experience. If I had given up when faced with difficulties in the past, I would not be where I am today. During my early career I encountered significant challenges that could easily have been overwhelming, and rather than treating them as insurmountable I viewed them as opportunities to grow stronger. That mindset has been a cornerstone of my journey, and it is what I want to pass on.

The distinction matters because it resolves the teachers' apparent dilemma. Anti-fragility is not the opposite of support; it is what support is for. By providing that support and instilling persistence, teachers can help students navigate mental health challenges while building a mindset that will serve them throughout life (Taleb, 2012).

Details

Recognise early, protect like a helmet, support just beyond current ability.

Three moves, in the order the article puts them.

01
Before
Recognition
Being attentive to changes in behaviour or performance. I have seen students who initially seem disengaged or withdrawn but are actually dealing with significant mental health issues. Preventive measures — fostering an open and supportive environment — can be effective, and are not always enough.
02
After
Protection
When prevention falls short the focus shifts to protection. Just as a helmet protects after a fall, ongoing support helps these students progress in the class: accommodations such as extended deadlines or alternative assessments, so they feel supported and capable of succeeding despite their struggles.
03
Throughout
The proximal zone
Support placed just beyond a student's current ability, enabling them to achieve more with the right guidance — Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development. My own mentors' guidance did not remove the challenges; it gave me the tools and confidence to tackle them.

The Reversal

The adjustment I made was to my own teaching, not to the students.

Many of my students, especially those from cultures where educational pacing is more gradual, arrived at the intensive bootcamp unprepared for the fire hose.

The fire hose, as it had been
•Intense, fast-paced and demanding
•A hallmark of my teaching approach for years
•Built for high-stakes environments where a large amount of material must be absorbed quickly
•Effective for students already conditioned to that pace
•And, I began to question, no longer the most effective given who was in the room
What I changed
•I slowed the sessions down and shortened them
•I replaced many traditional delivery sessions with storytelling
•I increased one-on-one engagement to almost a 1:1 mentor-student ratio
•I tailored the method to different levels of preparedness and cultural background
•Which is differentiated instruction, arrived at by necessity rather than by design (Tomlinson, 2001)

Evidence

Slowing down cost the programme nothing I can identify.

What came back after the change.

EVIDENCE A
The feedback
I do not think the quality of the programme was compromised. Feedback from students was overwhelmingly positive, with many performing above expectations and two excelling. The most significant feedback was that they understood the framework and experienced real growth.
EVIDENCE B
What the change matched
The adjustments reflected active learning, experiential learning and Bloom's taxonomy — moving students beyond simple recall toward application, analysis and creation (Kolb, 1984; Bloom, 1956), and constructivist learning theory's emphasis on active learning and reflection (Piaget, 1954).
EVIDENCE C
The limit stated in the article
This approach will not work for everyone. My preparation, mentally and technically, is crucial, as are the availability of mentor support and the full backing of the campus. The claim is about one bootcamp, not a method for all settings.

Conclusion

The reason it worked is the students themselves.

Where the reflection ends, and it is not on the teacher.

the most vital element is the students themselves—they are anti-fragile and celebrate life

— A Reflection on Mental Health and Anti-Fragility, 3 September 2024
What the adjustment actually taught
Teaching is not about delivering content quickly. It is about connecting with students, understanding their needs, and ensuring they are fully engaged — a step away from methods relied on for years.
The order of the answer
Adjustments may be necessary to support those who struggle, and what drives success is the resilience and enthusiasm of the students. Support is the condition; the strength is theirs.
The reciprocity in it
They rise to the challenge, and in doing so they inspire the teacher to bring his best every day. The article closes on what he owes them rather than on what they owe him.
