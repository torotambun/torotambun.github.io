Why Should Undergraduate Students Learn Strategy?

Strategy exists because organizations cannot have everything they want. An undergraduate course should build strategic literacy: recognising a strategic problem, telling a goal from a strategy, and seeing how choices, capabilities and resources connect.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

Strategy begins with scarcity, because an organization that cannot do everything it wants must choose.

"Saying that strategy is simply “good to know” is also not a satisfactory justification for making it part of a management curriculum."

Situation

Strategy is usually associated with senior management. Most undergraduate students will not decide where a company competes, or which businesses to enter or leave, immediately after graduation.

Complication

An organization wants higher revenue, higher profit, better quality, lower cost and less risk at the same time, but money, people, management attention and time are finite.

Question

What, then, justifies teaching strategy to undergraduates?

Answer

"Strategy therefore begins with scarcity. Once resources are scarce, choice becomes unavoidable."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Framework

Strategy is a coherent set of choices about where to compete, how to succeed there, and where to commit limited resources.

The working definition for an introductory class rests on three words: choices, coherence and resources.

The article defines strategy as "a coherent set of choices about where an organization will compete, how it intends to succeed there, and where it will commit its limited resources." Lafley and Martin (2013) frame strategy as an integrated set of choices, including where to play, how to win, what capabilities are required and what management systems must support them.

A restaurant that wants premium ingredients, the lowest prices, the largest menu, the fastest service, luxurious premises and delivery everywhere shows the point. Premium ingredients cost money, a large menu adds operational complexity, luxurious premises raise fixed costs, and very low prices reduce margins. "Without that logic, the statement is not yet strategy. It is a collection of desirable outcomes."

Being the best is not an answer either, since almost every company wants that. Strategy asks "best at what, for whom, and through which choices?"

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Gap

Goals, plans and slogans are not strategy, and none of the familiar frameworks is strategy by itself.

Rumelt (2011) separates strategy from aspirations, targets and lists of actions.

What is not yet strategy
✗A goal: "We want revenue to grow by 20 percent"
✗A plan: "We will open five branches, recruit twenty salespeople, and increase advertising"
✗A framework used alone: "These frameworks can be useful, but none of them is strategy by itself."
✗"A SWOT matrix can contain twenty observations without producing a meaningful decision."
What makes it strategy
✓A diagnosis of the challenge, a guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent actions that follow from that policy (Rumelt, 2011)
✓An explanation that an underserved segment exists outside Jakarta, competitors are concentrated in major cities, and an existing distribution capability can be extended economically
✓"The strategic element is not the number of branches. It is the logic connecting the environment, organizational capability, resource allocation, and expected result."
✓Analytical tools that improve diagnosis and judgment before resources are committed
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Details

Trade-offs, capability and uniqueness turn a list of attractive choices into a strategy.

Three ideas explain why the same opportunity leads different firms to different choices.

01
Lafley and Martin, 2013
Trade-offs
A choice creates consequences elsewhere. A low-cost airline decides aircraft configuration, routes, airports, turnaround time, ticketing, food and staffing differently from an airline aimed at premium international business travellers. Neither is automatically superior: the question is superior for which customer, under what conditions, and with which capabilities.
02
Barney, 2001
Capability
Sustained competitive advantage depends on resources and capabilities that competitors cannot easily reproduce or substitute. An attractive market opportunity is not automatically an attractive strategy for every company, so analysis looks outward at markets and inward at what the organization can credibly execute.
03
The article's emphasis
Uniqueness
Strategy requires a more precise answer than being the best. The article closes that point with a plain conclusion: "To be Unique it is!!!"

Process

Strategic management is a continuing process, and evaluation belongs inside it.

"Strategic management should consequently be understood as a continuing process rather than a document produced once a year."

Step 1
Understand the situation
Look at what is happening outside the organization and at what resources and capabilities exist inside it.
Step 2
Make choices
Identify alternatives and choose among them.
Step 3
Commit resources
Decide where money, people, time and management attention will go.
Step 4
Execute
Implement the strategy. Nag et al. (2007) show that the field encompasses both intended and emergent initiatives, so strategy does not unfold exactly as planned.
Step 5
Observe results
Results eventually appear and force management to ask whether the original assumptions were correct.
Step 6
Reconsider
Teece (2007) argues that firms in changing environments need capabilities to sense opportunities and threats, seize opportunities, and reconfigure assets. "Evaluation is therefore not something performed after strategy has somehow been completed. It belongs inside the strategic-management process."

Implications

An undergraduate course should build strategic literacy, not train corporate strategists.

Most graduates will enter organizations in which strategic choices have already been made.

A marketing employee may be told to concentrate on one customer segment, an operations employee to reduce cost without destroying quality, a finance employee to evaluate competing investment proposals, and a human-resources employee to recruit capabilities the organization lacks. Many of these apparently functional problems are consequences of larger choices about competitive position, capabilities and resource allocation (Barney, 2001; Nag et al., 2007).

The article judges the corporate-strategist goal unrealistic: "Its more defensible purpose, in my view, is to develop strategic literacy." Students should recognise a strategic problem, distinguish a goal from a strategy, understand why scarcity forces choices, examine the assumptions behind those choices, and ask whether the organization has the capabilities to execute them.

Implementation is part of the same discipline. "A strategy that cannot be translated into resource commitments, organizational arrangements, operating decisions, and feedback mechanisms remains largely an intention." "Strategic literacy therefore means learning to see those connections."

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Conclusion

Strategy exists because organizations cannot have everything they want, and that is where the course should begin.

The first question for an undergraduate class is why strategy exists at all, and the answer is not impressive plans or another collection of frameworks.

Strategy exists because organizations cannot have everything they want.

— Dr. Toronata Tambun, Why Should Undergraduate Students Learn Strategy?
