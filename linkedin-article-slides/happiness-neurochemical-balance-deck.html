LinkedIn Article  ·  Neuroscience · Happiness as Neurochemical Balance  ·  7 June 2021

Happiness? It is in the brain !

The article, written in Indonesian, holds that happiness is a matter of neurochemical balance across input, process and output. It lists eight supplies the brain needs, describes four chemicals (endorphin, dopamine, oxytocin and serotonin) that each evolved for a survival task and so cannot flow all the time, and links the excess or shortage of any one of them to addiction, domination, self-doubt, giving up and disease. It uses the mobile phone to show how past rewards build neural habits, calls phone addiction as dangerous as substance addiction, and concludes that happiness lies in pursuing and not in chasing it.

Introduction

The article holds that happiness is a matter of neurochemical balance across input, process and output.

Situation

The article opens with a question, what is happiness, and answers that it concerns the neurochemical balance in the brain, in three parts: a balance of input, a balance of process and a balance of output.

Complication

Imbalance makes people unhappy even in the middle of felt happiness. It also keeps them busy seeking a happiness that, the article says, is often already before their eyes.

Question

Why does happiness elude the people who look hardest for it?

Answer

Because happiness is a brief neurochemical burst that the brain is not designed to sustain. The article states its message as "Kebahagiaan dalam mengejar, bukan mengejar kebahagiaan" (the happiness is in pursuing, not in pursuing happiness).

Framework

Brain input needs eight balanced supplies, and the brain's minimum is the seed of the poverty-trap threshold.

The article calls the brain the control centre of health, whose vital functions must be assured, and says that keeping it in balance is the priority of every person. A lack, an excess or an unbalanced composition of any supply, it says, stops the brain's neurochemical and electrical processes from working well. The minimum calories or nutritional value that brain activity needs is described as very critical, and as the seed of the poverty-trap threshold.

Details

Four chemicals produce the feeling of happiness, each evolved for a survival task, so none can flow all the time.

The article puts the point in one sentence: "Kebahagiaan sebenarnya hanyalah semburan sesaat neurokimiawi di otak manusia" (happiness is only a brief burst of neurochemicals in the human brain). Humans need all four chemicals to feel comfortable, and the comfort fades when the same means trigger only one of them again and again.

1  ·  Endorphin, released by physical pain
The body's natural morphine masks pain, which let ancestors keep running from predators while wounded. It feels like euphoria, but it is stored for critical moments, since evolution could not have made humans a creature that feels it continuously.
2  ·  Dopamine, released on expecting a reward or something new
In a race it is released at the sight of the finish line. It is nature's reserve energy tank, keeping the hunter going until the prey is caught. If dopamine surged all the time, the energy would be spent by the moment it was needed, so it is kept for goals that are awaited.
3  ·  Oxytocin, released by trust in the people around
It promotes bonds between mother and child and between couples, and rises among like-minded people or in a comfortable hug. Humans are more often among people who disagree, dislike or cannot be trusted, so a constant release would serve no purpose.
4  ·  Serotonin, released on feeling important
It comes with the perception of higher status than others. Among mammals it is released when they dominate resources such as water, fruit trees or prey, and not when they yield to avoid conflict. Reaching high status through conflict usually brings wounds and bloodshed, so the brain keeps weighing the risk of pain against the satisfaction of winning.
The examples from apes

The article illustrates the chemicals with ape behaviour: apes groom each other in turns, which stimulates oxytocin, work out how to dominate troop-mates, which stimulates serotonin, and spend a long time coaxing termites from a mound, which stimulates dopamine. It adds that apes are not known to hurt themselves for endorphin, while humans are known to try all kinds of means for each of the four.

Source note

The article footnotes this section to Breuning: the 2016 book at the heading on the balance of output, and a 2011 Psychology Today post at the four kinds of chemicals.

Details

Too much or too little of one chemical is linked to addiction, domination, self-doubt, giving up and disease.

The article says that when only one or two of the four chemicals dominate, people become less human or inhuman. A fifth chemical, cortisol, is added to the list.

1  ·  Too much dopamine: addiction
Overeating, substance addiction, sexual addiction and corruption. The summary adds gambling and social media addiction.
2  ·  Too much serotonin: domination
A craving for power, narcissism, social climbing and fragility, with a tendency to exploit others, to commit sexual harassment or abuse, to thirst for political power and to oppress small people.
3  ·  Too much oxytocin: self-doubt
Low self-esteem, a hunger for acceptance and recognition, and self-deception.
4  ·  Too little endorphin: giving up
A lack of resilience and of anti-fragility, giving up easily, and mental shortcuts.
5  ·  Too much cortisol: disease
A susceptibility to metabolic disease.
Evidence note

The article cites no study for these links, and the list carries no footnote. The connections to corruption, sexual abuse and disease are stated as fact.

Details

The phone shows how past rewards build neural habits, and the article calls phone addiction as dangerous as substance addiction.

The article says that whatever has once triggered a happy chemical builds a toll road of neurons that the brain will choose later. Electricity in the brain, it says, flows like water in a storm, finding the path of least resistance, and past rewards set the brain to expect rewards in the future.

The mobile phone is the example. The brain remembers that phone use brought good news and social support, which fired dopamine, serotonin or oxytocin and wired the active neurons together. The chemicals are metabolised soon after release, so the person is driven to do more to get more, and thinks about the phone again and again. The person does not see what the phone has to do with survival, but the brain, evolved in a world where whatever felt good was good for survival, keeps seeking the feeling by the route that worked before.

Cortisol works through threat. It helps humans stay alert and, if managed well, to fight a threat. If it cannot be controlled, stopped or diverted so that endorphin is released, it is metabolised into substances that damage the body. Relief from a threat is a top priority for the inherited brain, because a predator can kill in an instant while a missed meal can be survived. The brain remembers that the phone once brought relief and expects the same relief now.

The phone looks harmless, since it adds no calories, breaks no law and does not cause memory loss. The article's verdict is "kecanduan ponsel juga sama bahayanya dengan kecanduan zat-zat lain" (phone addiction is as dangerous as addiction to other substances), and it footnotes this to the author's own 2021 LinkedIn post on social media addictions.

Evidence note

The footnote for the claim on phone addiction is the author's own post, and no study is cited for it. The passage on the phone as a whole is footnoted to Breuning's 2017 post.

Conclusion

Accepting that the chemicals rise and fall, the article concludes that happiness lies in pursuing and not in chasing it.

The conclusion repeats the opening summary, with balance of input, of process and of output, and adds the advice that follows from the mechanism.

The happiness in pursuing, not pursuing the happiness

— Happiness? It is in the brain !, 7 June 2021
Accept the ebb and flow
The chemicals evolved to promote survival behaviour and to recede as well as flow, so the article advises accepting that and enjoying them as they come and go.
The cost of expecting constant flow
Expecting all four chemicals to flow at all times leads to disappointment, and focusing on the disappointment teaches the brain to see the world through it. The cortex then scans the surroundings for evidence that something is wrong, and the evidence confirms the feeling.
The limit of control
However hard one tries, the article says, the surroundings cannot be controlled so as to guarantee the chemicals a ceaseless flow.
