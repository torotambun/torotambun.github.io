Echoes from the Past: No Traits of Innovation, however .....

This is a dissertation chapter published as an article, and it is the most heavily sourced piece in the archive from this period. Its subject is a claim made by a Dutch colonial economist and never fully buried: that people in the tropics cannot be changed to catch up economically. The chapter does not dismiss it, which is what makes it useful. Alatas demolished the laziness myth, and then established something harder, that indolence is a real and separate condition defined by evasion of effort rather than by absence of work. Koentjaraningrat showed that village culture survives relocation to the city and shifts only across a generation. The sharpest finding is structural rather than moral: the bapak could mobilise innovation and does not, because the same power distance that produces the bapak leaves the kawulo unable to reach him, which contradicts the 4CAPS+ leadership model and Javanese leadership teaching alike. The chapter closes on an elite that copies artifacts while never grasping the shared values beneath them.

Introduction

A colonial theory said the tropics cannot catch up, and the claim outlived the empire that produced it.

The chapter opens on the least comfortable source in its own literature, and it names the theory rather than paraphrasing it away. Boeke, cited through Koentjaraningrat, "proposed a very unpleasantly heard theory that the society mentality among people who live in tropical countries cannot be changed to catch up with economic progress", positing a void between an eastern economy of farmers and a western economy of plantation and commerce.

Huntington supplied the geographical version of the same determinism, holding that "people who live in tropical countries, without any harsh weather, where the nature is nice throughout the year tend to take it easy toward life".

Koentjaraningrat's own list of obstacles is reported without softening, and the chapter marks where it adds its own words: "lack of initative, preference to surrender to higher authorities, 9-5 mentality, (I lend my words), lethargic, and lazy will be a dealbreaker for advancing as a nation".

Against that stands Alatas, who "described the laziness myth across Southeast Asia. A myth of course when it was not true." The chapter's work begins where the refutation ends, because the perception persisted and the historical record shows landowners punished and exiled for sluggishness as far back as the early 1700s.

What the chapter is

The header reads "Chapter II: Literature Review - Finding Gaps". This is the dissertation's own literature review published in public, which is why it carries a formal reference list where most articles of this period carry none.

Gap

Indolence is real where laziness was a myth, and the distinction is what makes the chapter usable.

This is the chapter's most careful passage, and it is careful because the surrounding literature is not. The myth grew, it argues, "because of many people who are far from lazy but looked lazy because they are indolent which is an attitude characterized by the evasive response to circumstances which require toil and effort."

Then it fences the term on three sides, so that it cannot be used loosely. "For the avoidance of doubt: a man who chooses his work while he can IS NOT indolent. He is indolent if he avoids any type of work."

Unemployment is excluded too: "A short spell of unemployment which is outside the control of the individual is not indolence", and so is idleness with no work to do, since a shop assistant with no customers "cannot be described as indolent".

What is left is a behavioural definition with a test attached. "A person is classified as indolent if he habitually and regularly assumes that attitude." Habit and regularity, not circumstance, are what the term names.

Alatas's firsthand case, quoted at length in the chapter

"It concerns the head of a family of 5. The head of the family, a man in his early forties was regrenched from his place of work. Thereafter he refused to work, spending hi steim sleeping during the day, driving around or visiting friends." The chapter reproduces the passage with its extraction defects intact. What makes the case indolence is stated separately: "He shows no concern for the consequences of his refusing to work."

Where the chapter applies it now

"Sadly, the phenomenon of indolent is even becoming more evasive among the so-called anticipated demographic bonus pool of people." The demographic dividend is the population the argument is actually about.

Framework

Village culture survives the move to the city, and shifts only across a generation.

Koentjaraningrat supplies the mechanism that makes the whole chapter a formation argument rather than a cultural complaint. A cultural value, he holds, "if not forced, will not change at the speed it should."

Relocation does not dissolve it. "The “village culture”, even if it exists in a city, will continue to exist as the mental model of its practitioners. The roots of these values will also be passed down to their children, even though they now grow up in the city." Change arrives one generation later, not on arrival.

The mental model itself is described in terms the chapter quotes directly: "life is something bad, full of sin and suffering, but must be faced with sincere efforts and full endeavor. Working to live, not thinking too much about the future, more comfortable with stories of past glories."

And its posture toward misfortune is acceptance, with harmony as the governing value. The chapter connects this to the priyayi ideal expressed as nrimo, sabar and ikhlas, and notes it explores the same ground in the Rebel Talent sub-chapter of the dissertation.

The trait the chapter says is actually needed

Koentjaraningrat (1953) is cited for the view that "the most sought mentality for progressing and developing the nation is the trait of “conqueror”, nature, in his context", alongside his criticism of the dependency of subordinates on their bapak.

Evidence

The bapak could mobilise innovation and does not, because the distance that creates him also silences the kawulo.

This is the chapter's own contribution rather than a reading of the literature, and it turns a cultural observation into a structural one.

What the bapak relationship could do
•A recognised authority able to mobilise a group at speed
•An espoused value the author says he tends to agree with
•A channel through which innovation could be directed
•Hierarchy that removes the need to build consensus first
•Deference that would carry a direction once it was given
Why it does not
✗The same power distance index that creates the reliance
✗The kawulo perceive the distance as too wide to cross
✗The communication chain was never meant to be established
✗No fear-free psychological environment for open speech
✗A leader's own teaching contradicted by the leader's practice
The two frameworks this contradicts at once

The 4CAPS+ leadership model (Ancona & Bresman, 2023) requires sense-making, inventing, relating, visioning and purpose, with listening as a subset of sense-making. And Javanese teaching says the same: Suryodilogo (2012) records eight forces of nature, one of which advocates for leaders to be ocean-like, "emphasizing that a leader should be open-minded, an effective sense-maker always, and willing to listen and accept people for who they are." A leader who fails to create a fear-free psychological environment for open communication is, on the chapter's reading, contradicting his own teaching.

Details

Two Indonesian authorities catalogued the same obstacles decades apart, and the lists still read as current.

The chapter assembles diagnoses from the 1960s and the 1980s rather than inventing its own, which is what a literature review is for.

1  ·  Sudomo on motivation and work ethic
Quoted in Sagir (1989): weakness in motivation and willingness to work hard, "often living recklessly, or taking shortcuts, and wanting to live comfortably as an office employee, especially as a civil servant".
2  ·  Sudomo on skill and independence
A preference for white-collar over blue-collar work, and being "less skilled in work, weak in mastering technology, let alone creativity in creating new technologies". Then dependence: "still too close or dependent on parents or the family environment, weak in initiative to change their fate".
3  ·  Sudomo on status over achievement
"Indonesians are more oriented towards status or position, less oriented towards work achievement; they prefer to be graduates who go in and out looking for job vacancies just to be considered respectable, rather than achieving through entrepreneurship, creating jobs for others."
4  ·  Alisjahbana on economic mindset
Writing in 1961 and attributing the weakness to inheritance across generations: economic motives too weak against religious and social ones; reason not used in choosing objectives or tools; "Tradition is too powerful, making it impossible to achieve progress"; surplus money spent on consumption rather than on expanding a business; and familial social structure producing stagnation.
What the chapter says innovation-prone people look like instead

"curiousity on technology, on materialism, long-term view, ability to make an apt decision, ability to work as a team externally and internally, and surprisingly a resiliency." The chapter then asks whether these sound familiar and answers itself: they are the Rebel Talent concept.

Conclusion

The new elite copy the artifacts and never reach the shared values, which is the failure the dissertation is written against.

Mulder supplies the closing diagnosis, and the chapter applies it to the generation expected to lead the country by 2045.

They end up merely imitating without internalizing the needed shared values, they only imitate what appears good cosmetically, and forget altogether the actual shared values which are rooted deeply in corresponding country and cannot be copied as they are yielded over hundred of years formation in the muscle memory or mental model of its nation.

— Echoes from the Past, 16 September 2023
What changed, on Mulder's account
The elite once considered themselves superior to farmers and civil servants, "closer to God, so perfect that no improvements were necessary". Now "Many elites in developing countries admire anything that comes from the modern world outside their country", imitating manners, thought, fashion, home arrangement, recreation and even a language style that mixes in foreign words to appear modern.
The question the chapter puts to the reader
"Isn't this identity crisis clearly afflicting the young generation that is supposed to lead this country by 2045 and is eagerly awaiting its demographic bonus?" The demographic dividend and the identity crisis are treated as the same problem seen from two sides.
The author's own field evidence
The chapter cites immersing experiences from Japan, Vietnam, Norway, Israel, Singapore and the USA, submitted in the dissertation, where he observed people who "like to mimic artifacts of certain countries, unknowingly, never grasp the shared values which are the driver of the artfiacts."
What Mulder predicts follows
"policymakers will drift further away from their people, the gap will widen, and it will be marked by undistributed income and wealth." The chapter reads this as a warning about corruption and inequality rather than about taste.

Sources

This is the most heavily sourced article of this period, with a formal reference list of eight works, most of them Indonesian-language and several decades old, which is unusual in the archive and is what marks it as dissertation material rather than commentary. It confirms directly that 4-CAPS+ belongs to Ancona and Bresman, which an earlier batch had to establish by searching the author's own archive. The extracted text carries a number of typing and OCR defects, reproduced rather than repaired where they fall inside quoted passages, including regrenched for retrenched and hi steim for his time in the Alatas case. The footnote marked [1] appears twice with different years for the same Koentjaraningrat source, 1969 in the body and 1971 at the foot.
