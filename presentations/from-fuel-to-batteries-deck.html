From fuel to batteries: Trading one dependence for another

Electric motorcycles already cut emissions on Indonesia's present grid, but at under one percent of annual sales the climate effect is invisible, which makes energy security the stronger argument. Scaling to millions is an industrial conversion, not a subsidy problem, and Chinese manufacturers are already building at that scale. If the machines multiply while cells, controllers and production equipment stay foreign and coal supplies the extra power, the country will have replaced one dependence with another rather than achieving energy sovereignty.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
ITB 2025 Ganesa Widya Jasa Utama Awardee · En-ROADS Climate Ambassador

Introduction

An electric motorcycle saves 19 grams a kilometre, and the arithmetic is Indonesian.

The comparison rests on measured local machines rather than on imported averages, which is what makes it usable as policy evidence.

"An electric motorcycle in Indonesia emits about 26 grams of carbon dioxide for every kilometre it travels, compared with about 45 grams for the petrol motorcycle it replaces." The two machines are named: "A measured Indonesian comparison puts the Viar Q1 at 3.33 kilowatt hours per hundred kilometres and the Honda BeAT at 60.6 kilometres per litre" (Onggaria et al., 2023).

The calculation is shown rather than asserted. "Using those figures with Indonesia's 2024 grid factor of 680 grams of carbon dioxide per kilowatt hour, PLN's 8.55 percent network losses and a 95 percent charging efficiency gives about 26 grams per kilometre for the electric machine; including upstream gasoline emissions puts the petrol machine at about 45 grams".

Every term in that sentence is a place where a different assumption would change the answer — the grid factor, the network losses, the charging efficiency, and whether upstream refining and transport are counted on the petrol side. The column supplies all four so a reader can disagree with the number rather than with the conclusion.

The saving is real and it is small

Nineteen grams per kilometre, on today's grid, with no assumption of future decarbonisation. The column never disputes this figure; its argument is about what nineteen grams multiplied by too few machines actually amounts to.

The Gap

Showrooms sell the entire electric fleet every eleven days.

Four figures from four institutions, and the ratio between them is the whole problem.

RECORDED A
Annual motorcycle sales
"AISI recorded 6,412,769 domestic motorcycle sales in 2025" (AISI, 2026). The precision matters: this is the denominator against which every electrification claim has to be read.
RECORDED B
Electric motorcycles registered in the same year
"Kemenhub's SRUT data recorded 55,059 electric motorcycles in 2025, still below 1 percent of annual motorcycle sales" (Kemenhub, 2025). Below one percent, in the year the programme was supposed to be working.
RECORDED C
The accumulated fleet
"By June 2025, the accumulated electric two-wheeler population had reached about 196,000 units" (Kemenperin, 2025). Not annual — cumulative, across the whole programme.
RECORDED D
What that comes to
"At the annual motorcycle sales rate, Indonesian showrooms sell the equivalent of that entire electric fleet in roughly eleven days". The column marks this as its own calculation from the AISI and Kemenperin figures rather than as a reported statistic. "Its national effect, however, remains almost invisible."

The Turn

Energy security is the stronger argument: 582,000 barrels produced against 1.7 million consumed.

If the carbon case is invisible at this scale, the column looks for an argument that survives the same arithmetic — and finds one.

"That makes energy security a stronger national argument for electrification than carbon alone." The gap is stated in barrels: "Indonesia's crude oil and condensate production fell to about 582,000 barrels a day in 2024 while petroleum and other liquids consumption reached about 1.7 million barrels a day" (EIA, 2025).

And in money: "Indonesia also imported US$36.3 billion of oil and gas in 2024" (BPS, 2025).

What follows is the sentence that reframes the whole programme: "Every litre of petrol displaced by electricity therefore reduces some exposure to imported liquid fuel." Some exposure — the hedge is the column's own, and it is what keeps the claim honest at a fleet share below one percent.

Details

Motorcycles are 84 percent of the fleet, so they cannot stay secondary to the car programme.

Where the author first met the argument, and why its politics are durable.

"I first encountered that argument in a ministry presentation last year, and its political durability is easy to see." The provenance is given plainly — this is a case the state already makes to itself.

The composition of the fleet decides where the leverage sits: "Korlantas Polri counted 173.7 million registered motor vehicles in Indonesia in 2025, including 145.7 million motorcycles, or 83.9 percent of the total" (Korlantas Polri, 2025).

From which the column draws a direct instruction: "If officials want electrification to affect fuel dependence at scale, motorcycles cannot remain secondary to the electric-car programme." Four-wheel programmes attract the announcements; five-sixths of the vehicles are two-wheeled.

Evidence

A plant in Kendal was designed for a million units a year before Indonesia had built a fraction of that.

The industrial question is not whether the machines can be sold but who will make them, and the answer is already under construction.

What half a century built
•"Indonesia's combustion-motorcycle industry was built over more than half a century"
•"Suzuki began motorcycle production here in 1970, while Yamaha established Indonesian manufacturing in 1974"
•"Those decades created factories, suppliers, dealers and service networks that cannot be converted by a purchase subsidy alone"
•"Reaching million-unit electric volumes requires an industrial conversion"
What is arriving instead
✗"Chinese manufacturers are already arriving with production ambitions at that scale"
✗"Sunra Asia Pacific Hitech, a subsidiary of Jiangsu Xinri, began construction of a US$120 million plant in Kendal in 2024"
✗"Its first phase was designed for annual capacity of one million electric motorcycles"
✗One phase of one plant exceeds eighteen times the 55,059 machines Indonesia registered in 2025
The question this forces

"The policy question is therefore where the new industrial value will sit. Indonesia can reduce petrol imports and still surrender much of the value if cells, controllers, battery-management systems and manufacturing equipment remain concentrated elsewhere."

Implications

Twenty percent of sales is 1.28 million machines a year, twenty-three times last year's total.

A deliberately modest scenario, run to show that the adoption problem and the industrial problem are the same problem.

"If only 20 percent of Indonesia's 6.41 million annual motorcycle sales became electric, manufacturers would need to supply about 1.28 million electric motorcycles a year". The column flags this as its own scenario built on the AISI figure, not a forecast.

The multiple is what carries: "That is roughly 23 times the 55,059 electric motorcycles recorded through SRUT in 2025". Twenty percent is not an ambitious target; it is twenty-three times the present reality.

And the conclusion drawn from it is administrative: "At that volume, government can no longer treat consumer adoption and industrial capacity as separate problems." Subsidy programmes address the first. Nothing in the current design addresses the second.

Recommendations

Set a rising electric share, fix the credit channel, and define local content as production not assembly.

Three instruments, each aimed at a different actor, and the column names the mechanism for each rather than the aspiration.

1
Give manufacturers a volume to invest against
"Government can set a rising electric share for new motorcycle sales over several years, giving manufacturers a volume against which they can invest." A predictable share converts an investment gamble into a capacity calculation — which is what makes the industrial conversion financeable rather than hoped for.
2
Treat the loan as the decision point
"Financing also matters because about 65 percent of motorcycle purchases in 2025 were made on credit" (AISI, 2026). "That makes the loan decision one of the points where another petrol motorcycle either enters the fleet or does not." Two-thirds of the fleet is decided at a finance desk, not a showroom floor.
3
Make local content mean manufacturing
"Local-content rules should then distinguish actual Indonesian production of motors, power electronics, battery packs and battery-management systems from final assembly." Without that distinction, a screwdriver plant satisfies the rule and the value still leaves the country.

Indonesia can reduce petrol imports and still surrender much of the value if cells, controllers, battery-management systems and manufacturing equipment remain concentrated elsewhere.

The Warning

Coal rose twelve terawatt hours in 2024, and En-ROADS says extra demand can pull it further.

The column turns on its own opening figure, which is the most rigorous thing it does.

"Yet domestic production does not settle the climate side of the argument. The 680-gram grid figure is a national average" (Asian Transport Observatory, 2026). An average conceals which plant answers the marginal charge.

The mechanism is named from the simulator the author teaches with: "En-ROADS shows that transport electrification can increase demand for coal as well as renewables when those sources remain available to supply additional electricity" (Climate Interactive, 2026). Electrification moves demand; it does not choose the supply.

And the evidence that this is not hypothetical in Indonesia: "Indonesia's coal generation rose by about 12 terawatt hours in 2024" (Energy Institute, 2025). The column then states exactly what survives and what does not: "The roughly 19-gram saving per kilometre remains real, but it narrows if additional electricity continues to come from coal."

Why this belongs in a pro-electrification column

The 19-gram saving is the column's own opening claim, and it is the author who narrows it. The argument is not that electrification fails but that it can be counted as a success while the emissions move upstream to a power station.

Conclusion

Judge it by fuel imports and the generation mix, not by the subsidy count.

The test proposed uses instruments the state already operates, which is what makes it difficult to refuse.

"Government should therefore judge the programme against outcomes its own institutions already record. Kemenhub can show how many electric motorcycles actually enter the fleet, BPS can show whether fuel imports fall, and PLN can report how the generation mix changes as electric demand grows". No new data collection is being asked for.

The instruction is comparative: "Put those outcomes beside the subsidy count." And the failure condition is specified in advance, which is what turns it into a test rather than a hope — "If registrations rise while fuel imports remain high and additional electricity is still supplied by coal, the programme is recording activity rather than achieving its stated purpose."

Recording activity rather than achieving its stated purpose is the sentence a reader should carry out of the column. It describes a programme that can pass every one of its own indicators and still fail.

The Closing

Sovereignty is not achieved by changing what the country depends on.

The column ends on the distinction its title makes, and refuses the word the policy is usually sold with.

If the motorcycles multiply but batteries, controllers and production equipment still come from abroad while coal supplies the extra power, Indonesia will have reduced one exposure only by creating another. That is not at all an energy sovereignty. It is merely a change in what the country depends on.

— From fuel to batteries: Trading one dependence for another, 9 September 2026
The concession it opens with
"Electric motorcycles already work on Indonesia's present grid." The column is not arguing against the technology, and says so in the paragraph that delivers the verdict.
The three-part test
"The test now is whether higher sales translate into less imported petrol, more electric-motorcycle production inside Indonesia and cleaner electricity supplying the additional demand." Imports, production, generation mix — one for each of the three dependencies at stake.
Why the word matters
Energy sovereignty is the term under which the programme is argued for publicly. The column accepts the goal and denies that the current design reaches it, which is a harder claim than opposition.
