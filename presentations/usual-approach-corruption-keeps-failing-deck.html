Why the Usual Approach
to Corruption Keeps Failing

At a recent public discussion on corruption, the atmosphere shifted when attention turned to corporate practices. Some frowned; others crossed their arms. That shift itself raises the real question: what is being emphasized, and what is quietly receding?

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Alumnus, Harvard Business School; Affiliated Alumnus, MIT Sloan School of Management.

01  ·  Introduction

The System Rejects Visibility, Not Wrongdoing

People rarely see themselves as corrupt. They see themselves as practical, constrained, or simply adapting to reality.

Situation

The same cycle repeats after every corruption scandal: panels convene, reports circulate, rules are added, enforcement units are reorganized — cosmetic change resting on the assumption that tightening rules will eventually produce a different outcome.

Complication

Sociologist Syed Hussein Alatas argued corruption does not survive because people fail to recognize right and wrong. It survives because societies provide justifications that let wrongdoing feel acceptable.

Question

Why do public shaming and arrests so often fail to produce lasting change?

Answer

Because exposure creates disruption, not recalibration. The lesson absorbed is not that corruption is wrong, but that it must be concealed better. Real change requires accountability that is personal and applies upward, not just visible punishment.

02  ·  Gap

Adaptive Actors Get More Refined, Not Weaker

Punishment functions as feedback, not a stopping point.

Core Gap

Many people involved in corruption are not rigid or desperate. They are adaptive. They learn quickly and recover from failure. When such adaptability operates without firm internal limits, corruption does not weaken — it becomes more refined. Rules function mainly to slow behavior, not to redefine it; people adjust by finding gaps, shortcuts, and workarounds.

03  ·  Framework

What Actually Moves the Moral Boundary

Four mechanisms explain why corruption persists despite education, intelligence, and formal compliance systems.

1
Justification, Not Ignorance (Alatas)

Corruption survives because societies provide acceptable justifications — people see themselves as practical or loyal, rarely as corrupt.

2
Exposure vs. Recalibration

What produces discomfort is exposure, not the act itself. Once attention fades, careers and social standing recover — not because the act was right, but because visibility ended.

3
Consequences Over Moral Language

Organizations respond to consequences, not moral appeals. Where unethical behavior leads to real and lasting loss, firms adjust quickly; where it does not, they exploit what is allowed.

4
Leadership Clarity Removes Ambiguity

When leaders enforce rules on themselves without exception, excuses disappear. When exceptions are visible at the top, justifications multiply below.

04  ·  Details

One Company's Clarity

Behavior changed because ambiguity was removed.

A transnational oil and gas company: proven wrongdoing, the person left

Performance did not buy protection. Loyalty did not soften outcomes. That clarity removed excuses at every level of the organization.

Corruption takes different forms, same function everywhere

In some contexts it appears as cash payments and informal fees; in others, it hides behind legal complexity and privileged access. One looks crude, the other sophisticated — both redirect public outcomes toward private advantage.

05  ·  Evidence

Why Public Discussion Shifted Away from the State

The shift itself is the evidence of where scrutiny is comfortable and where it is not.

OBSERVED
A public discussion frowns when attention turns to corporate practices

Corruption requires at least two parties to function — when focus moves toward one side and away from the other, it reveals which dynamics are comfortable to examine and which are not.

STRUCTURAL
Repetition works against reform once punishment feels temporary

People stop asking whether corruption is wrong and start asking whether anything meaningful will follow. Attention shifts from substance to spectacle, and the system absorbs the shock.

06  ·  Implications

Concealment Better vs. Accountability Real

Two very different lessons a system can teach after a scandal.

✗ Exposure as the Endpoint
✗Relying on public shaming and arrests, which create disruption but rarely recalibration of internal moral boundaries
✗Adding rules and reorganizing enforcement units in cosmetic cycles that repeat the same naive assumption
✓ Accountability That Is Real and Personal
✓Enforcing rules without negotiation, applying them upward as clearly as downward
✓Narrowing the space where wrongdoing still feels normal, necessary, or excusable — not chasing full eradication
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Reduction, Not Eradication

Corruption fades not when people fear being caught, but when acting wrongly no longer fits how the system expects people to operate.

1

Treat corruption as a realistic reduction problem, not an eradication fantasy — shrink the space for excuses rather than chasing zero.

2

Build leadership accountability that applies upward without exception, since ambiguity at the top multiplies justifications below.

3

Create conditions where admission is safe enough that people can acknowledge being part of a corrupt system without it destroying their families.

Closing

Change begins when the system is safe enough for people to admit they are corrupt actors inside a corrupt system, and decide that it must no longer be accepted. Yet when admission threatens families rather than careers alone, denial becomes the rational choice.
