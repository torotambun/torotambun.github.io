Society Relies on Corporations
and Gen-Z to Build a Privacy Culture

Who would have thought that a robot vacuum cleaner could become a privacy threat? Privacy is not a technical preference. It is a human right — and human rights are not protected by devices, platforms, or regulations alone, but by moral capacity.

Dr. Toronata Tambun and Lilian Kallman
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Moral Capacity, Not Just Devices

Without internalized restraint, privacy protection remains temporary and fragile.

Situation

Washing machines, air conditioners, security cameras, smart TVs, and kitchen appliances now silently map habits, movements, and routines inside the most private spaces of the home.

Complication

Privacy protection depends almost entirely on behavior when enforcement is absent. By observable daily behavior, Indonesia still operates largely at Kohlberg's earliest moral levels — avoiding trouble and pleasing authority, not internalized restraint.

Question

If individuals and governments remain at externally driven moral levels, who is actually building a durable privacy culture?

Answer

Corporations and Gen-Z — not as ideal moral actors, but as functional ones. Corporations respond to consequence and liability; Gen-Z responds to identity and self-interest. Neither is principled universalism, but both work.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

Is This Right? vs. Is This Required?

Habituation, not apathy, explains the muted response to privacy violations.

Core Gap

When data leaks occur, the social response is telling: concern without outrage, acknowledgment without accountability. Privacy violations are treated as unfortunate events rather than moral failures that demand accountability. At moral levels one and two, the dominant question is not "Is this right?" but "Is this required?" or "Will there be consequences if I refuse?"

03  ·  Framework

Kohlberg's Moral Levels, Applied to Privacy

Different actors sit at different rungs — and the rung determines whether privacy holds up when no one is watching.

1–2
Fear of Punishment, Obedience to Authority

Indonesia's dominant daily pattern: helmets worn only when police are visible, jaywalking normalized, corruption excused as necessity when enforcement is absent.

3
Peer Norms and Self-Interest (Gen-Z)

"What data belongs to me. Who profits from it." Not principled universalism, but alert, vocal, and resistant to silent exploitation.

4
Procedural Discipline (Corporations)

Rules followed because systems demand them — standards like ISO/IEC 27001 institutionalize discipline through audits, segregation of duties, and traceability.

5–6
Principled, Internalized Restraint

Respect for rights that holds even when violations are invisible and profitable — the level neither individuals, government, nor corporations consistently reach.

04  ·  Details

New Intrusions Arrive Quietly

Biometric and genetic data are treated as casually as a loyalty card.

Cash rewards offered for retina scans

Despite retinal data being unchangeable and capable of later enabling identity fraud once compromised.

Fingerprints collected for convenience

Biometric identifiers cannot be reset, and inexpensive scanners have been shown to be insecure.

23andMe's bankruptcy exposed genetic data

Users were left scrambling to reclaim information that should never have been treated casually in the first place.

05  ·  Evidence

Consent Without Debate, Data Without Deletion

Two everyday patterns that reveal how little control citizens actually retain.

SURVEILLANCE
Facial recognition in train stations, little public debate

Installed with minimal discussion of consent, retention, or oversight; personal vaccination data was collected at unprecedented scale during Covid-19 with no sustained reckoning once the controversy faded.

ACCESS CONTROL
ID cards surrendered at Jakarta office tower entrances

Names, ID numbers, phone numbers, and photographs logged as a condition of entry — few ask who controls this data, how long it is kept, or whether it is shared.

06  ·  Implications

Ideal Actors vs. Functional Ones

The actors currently protecting privacy are not doing so for principled reasons — and that is precisely why it works.

✗ Waiting for Moral Perfection
✗Expecting durable respect for data rights from a culture where morality remains largely external
✗Relying on government, which lags behind corporations and Gen-Z, offering laws that are selective and reactive, acknowledged only after breaches occur
✓ Building on Functional Incentives
✓Embedding restraint into corporate systems through standards like ISO/IEC 27001, since failure carries real cost
✓Leveraging Gen-Z's self-interest-driven alertness to consent and misuse as a genuine check, even without principled universalism
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Privacy Is a Value, Not a Feature

Values are formed early, reinforced daily, and reflected in how societies treat rules, space, and one another.

1

Recognize that laws and installed systems alone cannot substitute for internalized or systematized restraint.

2

Support corporate procedural frameworks (audits, segregation of duties, traceability) as a realistic near-term substitute for principled universalism.

3

Treat Gen-Z's self-interest-driven vigilance as a genuine asset, not a lesser form of ethics, in the absence of stronger institutional protection.

Closing

Technology will continue to arrive. The real question is whether society arrives with it. Without moral readiness, whether principled or procedural, privacy remains vulnerable, human rights remain rhetorical, and society remains reactive rather than shaping the technology it adopts.
