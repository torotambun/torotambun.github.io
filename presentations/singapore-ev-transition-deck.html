Singapore's EV Transition Could
Spark Indonesia's Green Evolution

Singapore's Land Transport Master Plan 2040 isn’t a template Indonesia can copy at scale — but its clear vision, concrete milestones, and integrated safety and incentive design offer practical lessons for a country whose Golden Indonesia 2045 ambitions still run on 86 percent fossil fuels.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
MIT Climate Interactive EN-ROADS Simulation Ambassador and a Nuclear Engineering UGM alumnus.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Two green visions, on very different scales.

Singapore and Indonesia share the same urban-emissions problem, but not the same starting point.

Situation

Indonesia is grappling with growing environmental challenges, particularly in urban areas where transportation significantly contributes to carbon emissions, even as it pursues Golden Indonesia 2045’s goal of net-zero emissions by 2045.

Complication

Indonesia faces more complex challenges than Singapore due to its larger size, greater population, and heavy reliance on fossil fuels — 86 percent of energy consumption as of 2023 — while Singapore has already achieved one-third EV adoption and is executing a detailed 2040 transport master plan.

Question

Can a small, dense city-state’s transport strategy actually teach a much larger, more complex nation anything useful?

Answer

Yes — not as a scale template, but as a model of clear vision, detailed execution, and stakeholder engagement, particularly in incentive design, safety regulation, and integrating active mobility with EV infrastructure.

02  ·  Gap

Indonesia has plans; it has fewer concrete milestones.

Ambition is not the scarce resource here — specificity is.

Core Gap

Indonesia may have transportation plans that address urban emissions, but looking to other models for inspiration can enhance its approach. Singapore’s Land Transport Master Plan 2040 sets concrete, dated milestones — ceasing diesel car and taxi registration by 2025, 40,000 public EV chargers, 90 percent of trips on foot, cycle, or public transport by 2040. Indonesia’s own transportation and energy targets are comparatively less specific, particularly on the infrastructure and incentive design that Singapore has already proven out.

03  ·  Framework

Three Transferable Lessons From Singapore's Plan

Each lesson is a strategy, not a scale — and strategies transfer even when scale doesn’t.

01
Concrete, Dated Milestones

By 2025, ceasing diesel car and taxi registration; by 2040, a fully electric bus fleet and 40,000 public EV chargers — specificity that turns ambition into an executable plan.

02
Incentive-Driven Adoption

The EV Early Adoption Incentive and Vehicle Emission Scheme offer discounts and rebates up to $25,000, driving Singapore to one-third EV adoption by 2023 — the leader in Southeast Asia.

03
Safety Built Into the Transition

The Electric Vehicle Charging Act and EV Battery Safety Taskforce ensure rapid EV adoption does not compromise public safety — a regulatory layer Indonesia’s still-developing EV market could adopt early.

04  ·  Details

The Numbers Behind Both Countries' Ambitions

Different starting points, complementary resources.

6,500 charging points versus an early-stage network

Singapore had 6,500 public charging points by 2023, the highest density in Southeast Asia, while Indonesia’s EV infrastructure development is still in its early stages — a gap Singapore’s milestone-driven approach could help close.

86 percent fossil fuels versus a 3,686 GW renewable potential

Indonesia’s heavy fossil-fuel dependence sits alongside an estimated 3,686 GW of renewable energy potential — positioning it to become ASEAN’s green energy export hub even as its domestic EV transition lags.

A 2 GWac solar partnership, already in motion

Indonesia’s cross-border solar energy partnership with Singapore demonstrates the two countries’ transitions are already complementary — Indonesia supplying renewable energy, Singapore modeling its use.

05  ·  Evidence

Sources Behind the Argument

Two national transport and energy strategies ground the piece’s comparison.

EVIDENCE A
Singapore's Land Transport Master Plan 2040

The plan’s “20-Minute Towns and a 45-Minute City” vision and Walk, Cycle, Ride initiative anchor the piece’s account of integrated, milestone-driven transport planning.

EVIDENCE B
Golden Indonesia 2045

Indonesia’s vision to become the world’s 4th largest economy while achieving net-zero emissions by 2045 frames the ambition Singapore’s example is meant to inform.

EVIDENCE C
Singapore's EV incentive schemes

The EV Early Adoption Incentive and Vehicle Emission Scheme, offering discounts up to $15,000 and rebates up to $25,000, demonstrate concrete policy tools that lowered EV adoption barriers.

EVIDENCE D
The Electric Vehicle Charging Act

Taking effect December 2023, this act mandates licensed operators and approved chargers — a regulatory model for ensuring safety keeps pace with EV market growth.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: What Indonesia Can and Can't Copy

The mistake is copying scale; the opportunity is copying strategy.

✗ Treating Singapore as a Scale Model
✗Assuming Singapore’s small, dense city-state solutions transfer directly to Indonesia’s larger, more complex context
✗Overlooking Indonesia’s own comparative advantage in renewable resource supply
✓ Treating Singapore as a Strategy Model
✓Adopting concrete, dated milestones and incentive-driven adoption mechanisms adapted to Indonesian scale
✓Building safety regulation into the EV market early, rather than retrofitting it later
✓Leveraging Indonesia’s renewable resource base as a regional complement to Singapore’s urban mobility leadership

07  ·  Conclusion

A Complementary, Not Copied, Green Evolution

Four recommendations for adapting Singapore’s lessons to Indonesian scale.

1

Set concrete, dated EV and transport milestones, adapted from Singapore’s master-plan approach to Indonesian scale and geography.

2

Introduce incentive schemes that lower the cost barrier to EV adoption, particularly in dense urban centers like Jakarta.

3

Build safety regulation into the EV market's growth phase early, rather than after infrastructure is already in place.

4

Leverage Indonesia’s renewable energy potential and raw material reserves domestically, not only as exports to partners like Singapore.

Closing

If Indonesia can apply these lessons while leveraging its vast renewable resources, it has the potential not only to achieve its goals but to emerge as a regional leader in sustainability.
