Is Indonesia Today at Its Richest?
Ask Children

Days from our 80th independence anniversary, an economist called Indonesia the richest in its history. Test that claim against two outcomes families feel daily: stunting and dry-season water reliability. The question is moral as much as economic.

Dr. Toronata Tambun — Singapore
Harvard Business School Alumni and Affiliated Alumnus of MIT Sloan School of Management.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Test "richest" against the tape and the tap

GDP per capita is near $4,900. Whether that reads as richness depends entirely on which numbers you ask.

Situation

In March 2025, economist Ricardo Hausmann said Indonesia is at its richest in its history. GDP per capita sits near US$4,900, the economy is projected to grow 4.7–4.8 percent, and reserves stand at US$152.5 billion.

Complication

Stunting sits at 19.8 percent, and half of national GDP comes from river basins under high or severe dry-season water stress. A midwife's measuring tape and a dry neighborhood tap tell a different story than the growth chart.

Question

If richness is measured by whether a society protects its own children and their water, does Indonesia's number still hold?

Answer

Judged by child growth and water reliability, Indonesia's outcomes sit closer to countries with a fraction of its income than to the profile of a truly rich nation. "Richest in its history" loses weight against these two tests.

02  ·  Gap

Aggregate Growth, Household Fragility

The macro numbers move. The two indicators families feel daily do not move nearly as fast.

Core Gap

Poverty stands at 8.5 percent on Indonesia's own line, but 68.2 percent under the World Bank's upper-middle-income yardstick. Housing and education matter, but they move slowly after prosperity rises. Stunting and water reliability are early warnings of whether a society protects its own — and by 2025, the stunting target of 14 percent remains unmet at 19.8 percent. That gap is not an achievement. It is a plan not yet delivered.

03  ·  Framework

Why These Two Measures

Nutrition and water are not just welfare indicators — they are leading signals for everything else.

1
Early Warning, Not Lagging Indicator

Nutrition and safe water in the first years shape brain growth, immunity, and school readiness — unlike housing or education, which move slowly after prosperity rises.

2
Foundation for Everything Else

Reliable dry-season supply determines harvests, clinics, and hygiene. If these fail, the rest is window dressing. If they improve, everything else lifts.

3
Nutrition–Water–Productivity Loop (R1)

A reinforcing loop: healthier children grow into more capable adults, raising productivity and funding better services, which in turn improve child health.

4
Household Energy–Cost Loop (B1)

A balancing loop: reducing exposure to volatile import prices eases pressure on family budgets and protects spending on food and water.

04  ·  Details

The Numbers Behind the Claim

Stunting, water stress, and import dependence, side by side with the growth story.

Stunting: 19.8% in 2024, target 14% in 2025

A truly rich country would already be locked on single digits, with its slowest districts shielded from relapse.

Half of GDP from water-stressed basins, rising to two-thirds by 2045

Water shortfalls can shave 2.5 percent off GDP. Reliability in the driest month is what households feel; without it, crops fail, clinics strain, hygiene breaks.

Import dependence: fuel 57%, LPG 77%, sugar 73%, soybeans 85%

Rice and corn stay near 8–9 percent imported. A confident nation would not let core needs swing on world prices and shipping schedules.

05  ·  Evidence

A Fraction of the Income, Comparable Outcomes

Two countries with far lower GDP per capita post similar or better child-welfare results.

KENYA
$2,200 per capita, stunting in the high teens

Kenya, at roughly half Indonesia's income, keeps stunting in the high teens under comparable water stress.

UGANDA
$1,070 per capita, 26% stunting

Only six points worse than Indonesia's 19.8 percent, on roughly a fifth of the income — a gap that should not exist if "richest" carries real weight.

06  ·  Implications

A Flattering Line vs. A Plain Promise

Two ways a nation can respond to its own growth numbers.

✗ Hiding Behind the Aggregate
✗Citing GDP per capita and reserve levels as proof of richness while stunting and water stress remain untracked at the top of any dashboard
✗Treating core-needs import dependence (fuel, LPG, sugar, soybeans) as background risk rather than a national-security concern
✓ Making a Plain Promise and Keeping It
✓Publishing stunting and dry-season water-hour data monthly, making them nonnegotiable in cabinet meetings, provincial plans, and state enterprise budgets
✓Funding maternal and child nutrition, primary healthcare, sanitation, and water system upkeep as priorities, not afterthoughts subject to budget raids
✓Tying funds to visible milestones: fuller growth monitoring, fewer underweight infants, more working latrines, more dry-season water hours

07  ·  Conclusion

Let This Be the Year the Promise Began

Real strength starts with feeding the child and keeping the water running.

1

Put stunting and dry-season water reliability at the top of every dashboard, and treat them as feedback loops where today's changes shape tomorrow's results.

2

Fund basics — nutrition, healthcare outreach, sanitation, water upkeep — as protected priorities, running existing infrastructure (puskesmas, posyandu, village funds, water utilities) at full and steady use.

3

Set leak-reduction targets and treat import-heavy household energy as a child-welfare risk, easing the Household Energy–Cost Balancing Loop that squeezes family food and water budgets.

Closing

Back at the clinic, the tape will tighten again next month. In the dry season, the canal will drop again. A confident nation does not hide behind a flattering line; it makes a plain promise and keeps it. On the 80th anniversary of independence, let "richest" mean every family can count on both.
