Shared Values for Sustainable Progress: Learning from Norway

Norway’s clean rivers, respected pedestrians and open door to refugees are downstream of shared values built over two generations — friluftsliv, dugnad, equality — not something a poorer country can import as infrastructure alone.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Dosen Industri, Sekolah Tinggi Elektro dan Informatika, Institut Teknologi Bandung (STEI-ITB)
© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun

When people return from travel abroad, they marvel: “Oh, how green it is!” “Their rivers are so clean.” They notice the absence of trash on mountain trails, the respect shown to pedestrians, the orderly queues. I heard the same admiration from myself on a recent visit to Norway.

Many wish to replicate the artefacts — the parks, the clean water, the preserved forests, the kindness toward refugees. Can they sustain the practice without the underlying values? I believe the answer is, most likely, no. These shared values are deeply ingrained, automatic system-1 reflexes, embedded in muscle memory — and it takes at least two generations to build that foundation, starting within families from early childhood.

© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01
Friluftsliv

“Open-air life” — a cultural concept of connecting with nature through hiking, skiing, fishing, kayaking, treated as harmony with the environment rather than leisure.

02
Traditional practice

Høsting — the ancient practice of foraging berries and mushrooms sustainably — and Sami reindeer herding, both encoding respect for ecological balance.

03
Equality and collective responsibility

A belief that everyone has equal access to nature and its resources, extended into environmental policy as a shared commitment.

04
Dugnad

Voluntary community work — cooperation and shared responsibility as a civic reflex, not an occasional favor.

Economic growth reinforces this loop: as wealth and wealth distribution improve, governments can invest in waste management and environmental education, and citizens gain the bandwidth to value their surroundings. Norway’s largest-in-the-world sovereign wealth fund financed exactly this — the transition from a country that once discarded rubbish in nature to one that prioritizes cleanliness.

The same shared-values lens explains Norway’s receptiveness to refugees. A Vietnamese friend who immigrated as a high-schooler is now a successful entrepreneur in Oslo’s shipping supply-chain innovation ecosystem — she points to a complementing ground between Vietnamese and Norwegian cultures: strong work ethic, and the value placed on family, met by Norway’s balanced approach to work and leisure and its concept of dugnad.

But first and foremost: feed the people with nutritious food. Culture eats strategy for breakfast, and individuals need basic needs met before they can engage in higher-order activities like climate policy or human rights. A typical Norwegian frokost — whole grains, dairy, cured meats, boiled eggs, smoked salmon — is not a lifestyle detail; it is the floor beneath the culture that can then afford to think about the environment.

To make progress in less-developed countries, the sequence matters: nutrition, financial assistance, equal access to world-class education, before the conversation about single-use plastic or artificial intelligence can even begin. As the saying goes, the belly carries the feet.

Before attempting to emulate the state-of-the-art artefacts found in developed countries, meet people’s basic needs first — nutrition foremost. Only on that foundation can a sustainable, strategic culture take root.
