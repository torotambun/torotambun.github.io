Indonesia's Nuclear Horizon:
Diplomatic Finesse Needed

Indonesia is a technology-adopting, not a technology-inventing, nation attempting an unprecedented thorium reactor MVP in a neighborhood that includes the Philippines’ abandoned Bataan plant and Singapore’s cautious, diplomacy-first approach — making regional diplomatic finesse, not reactor choice alone, the real determinant of success.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
MIT Climate Interactive EN-ROADS Simulation Ambassador and a Nuclear Engineering UGM alumnus.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Six decades of ambition, still short of a working reactor.

From the 1954 Atomic Energy Council to BRIN’s 2024 small modular reactor push, Indonesia’s nuclear story is one of recurring starts.

Situation

Indonesia has pursued nuclear ambitions since the 1960s and is now moving toward small modular and thorium-based reactors, with BRIN announcing two major 2024 projects.

Complication

By Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations theory, Indonesia is a technology consumer, not an innovator — yet it is exploring a thorium molten salt reactor design not commercially implemented anywhere in the world, in a region where the Philippines’ only attempt was abandoned and Singapore has moved with extreme caution.

Question

What actually determines whether Indonesia’s nuclear push succeeds, rather than repeating the region’s cautionary tales?

Answer

Diplomatic finesse — transparent regional communication and safety cooperation modeled on Singapore’s ASEAN/IAEA-anchored approach — matters as much as the reactor technology chosen.

02  ·  Gap

Coverage debates reactor type. It skips regional diplomacy.

Cost and technology dominate the conversation; the neighborhood Indonesia is entering barely does.

Core Gap

Public discussion of Indonesia’s nuclear ambitions centers on which reactor to build and what it costs — thorium versus small modular reactors, $700 million versus $7–9 billion. Rarely treated as a first-order requirement is the fact that Indonesia’s neighbors are watching closely: the Philippines built and never commissioned its only nuclear plant, and Singapore has spent a decade building nuclear expertise and diplomatic channels before committing to anything. Indonesia’s success depends on engaging that regional context, not just choosing a reactor.

03  ·  Framework

Three Forces Shaping Indonesia's Nuclear Path

Each force independently raises the stakes for how Indonesia navigates its neighborhood.

01
The Innovation-Adoption Paradox

Indonesia is not known as a leading innovative country by Rogers’ adoption curve, yet it is pursuing an unprecedented, commercially untested thorium molten salt reactor — a high-end minimum viable product for a country positioned as a technology consumer.

02
The Regional Precedent

The Philippines’ Bataan Nuclear Power Plant, a 620 MW facility completed in 1984, was never commissioned after Chernobyl and political change — a live cautionary tale sitting in Indonesia’s own neighborhood.

03
The Diplomacy Requirement

Singapore’s decade-long, ASEAN- and IAEA-anchored approach to nuclear readiness — explicit about safety, security, and regional collaboration — is the model of the diplomatic finesse Indonesia’s push has yet to demonstrate.

04  ·  Details

What the Numbers Say About Scale and Ambition

Cost comparisons make clear how much bigger today’s ambitions are than anything Indonesia has previously built.

From $50 million to $304 million

The 30 MW Multipurpose Reactor GA Siwabessy, inaugurated in 1987 for a $50 million contract, would cost approximately $304 million in 2024 dollars — and that was a research reactor, not a power plant.

$700 million versus $7–9 billion

A 100 MW modular reactor such as the NuScale Power Module is estimated at around $700 million, while a proven 1 GW reactor such as the CANDU or Westinghouse AP1000 can cost $7–9 billion — a scale gap that argues for starting modular and small.

$14 billion for Vogtle 3 and 4

The first newly constructed nuclear units in the United States in over three decades cost approximately $14 billion combined — a reminder that even the world’s most advanced nuclear economy finds large-scale builds difficult and rare.

05  ·  Evidence

Sources Behind the Argument

A diffusion-of-innovation framework, a domestic policy record, a regional cautionary tale, and a diplomatic model ground the piece’s claims.

EVIDENCE A
Rogers’ Diffusion of Innovations

Everett Rogers’ theory of how societies adopt new technology frames Indonesia as a consumer, not innovator — raising the question of why it is attempting an unprecedented high-end MVP.

EVIDENCE B
BRIN’s 2024 dual-track projects

Indonesia’s National Research and Innovation Agency announced an Accelerator Driven System in Serpong and a fourth-generation small modular reactor developed with Tsinghua University’s INET — concrete evidence of the current push.

EVIDENCE C
The Philippines’ Bataan plant

Built in 1984, never commissioned, and still inactive today — a regional precedent for what happens when safety concerns and political change overtake a nuclear program.

EVIDENCE D
Dr. Vivian Balakrishnan at the IAEA

Singapore’s Minister for Foreign Affairs, in a 2022 IAEA General Conference speech, stated Singapore’s commitment to working “closely with our ASEAN neighbors and the IAEA to strengthen nuclear safety, security and safeguards in our region.”

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Two Ways to Frame the Nuclear Decision

The choice of frame determines what gets managed and what gets overlooked.

✗ Technology-First Framing
✗Debate centers almost entirely on reactor type and cost
✗Regional neighbors’ reactions treated as an afterthought, not a planning input
✓ Diplomacy-First Framing
✓Transparent, ASEAN- and IAEA-anchored regional communication built in from the start
✓Sequencing that starts with smaller, modular reactors to build public and regional trust incrementally
✓Explicit lesson-drawing from Pakistan and India’s geopolitical navigation
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Navigating the Neighborhood, Not Just the Technology

Four recommendations for a nuclear push that treats diplomacy as infrastructure, not afterthought.

1

Sequence with smaller modular reactors first, building public trust incrementally before scaling to larger designs.

2

Institutionalize regional diplomatic channels through ASEAN and the IAEA, modeled on Singapore’s proactive approach.

3

Draw explicit lessons from Pakistan and India’s experience navigating geopolitical scrutiny during nuclear development.

4

Maintain transparent, ongoing communication with Malaysia, the Philippines, and Singapore throughout the program’s development.

Closing

Diplomatic finesse will be essential as Indonesia navigates this complex landscape, balancing national aspirations with regional and global responsibilities.
