Traditional Journalism: Trustworthy
Anchor in the Digital Age

As Indonesia heads into the 2024 election, the newspaper’s slow, vetted editorial process — anchored in “episteme” rather than the unverified “doxa” of the digital feed — is not nostalgia but a civic necessity that mainstream media must extend into digital formats, not abandon.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
A lecturer of industrial entrepreneurship at the Bandung Institute of Technology School of Electrical Engineering and Informatics (STEI-ITB) and a Harvard Business School graduate.

01  ·  Introduction

A newspaper ritual, read against a news cycle built for speed.

A personal habit — a crisp newspaper at 06:00 every morning — becomes the entry point for a question about which information a democracy can actually trust.

Situation

Digital platforms now dominate information consumption, prized for immediacy, endless scroll, and constant notification.

Complication

Speed has displaced verification. The sprawling internet blurs the line between fact and opinion just as the 2024 presidential election — a hotbed for misinformation and high-stakes narrative warfare — approaches.

Question

Can a traditional, slower medium still function as a trustworthy anchor when the information environment itself has gone digital?

Answer

Yes — because newspapers’ layered editorial process keeps them closer to “episteme” (verified, reasoned knowledge) than to “doxa” (popular, unverified opinion), and that distinction becomes indispensable, not optional, during an election cycle.

02  ·  Gap

Digital readiness is treated as an infrastructure problem, not a cultural one.

Adoption metrics — internet access, smartphone penetration — are mistaken for the finish line, when the real test is discernment.

Core Gap

A society is not digitally prepared merely because of widespread internet access or smartphone adoption. True digital readiness is achieved only when a population is not just consuming digital content but discerning it, understanding it, and utilizing it for progress — a socio-cultural transformation, not a technological feat. Most commentary on Indonesia’s digital transition skips this distinction entirely.

03  ·  Framework

Three Forces Behind the Newspaper’s Continued Authority

Each force is independent of the others; together they explain why a slower medium still anchors trust.

01
Episteme vs. Doxa

Newspapers, through rigorous multi-layer editing, lean toward “episteme” — researched, verified, reasoned knowledge — while the sprawling internet, with its countless competing voices, more easily collapses into “doxa,” unverified popular belief (Bourdieu; Foucault).

02
The Ritual of Trust

The finite, tactile, notification-free newspaper reduces cognitive load and produces reflective, paced consumption — conditions that measurably support comprehension and retention, unlike the endless scroll.

03
The Election Stakes

The 2024 presidential election is a seminal, high-emotion event where vested interests exploit the information vacuum with half-truths — raising the cost of losing a reliable, verified anchor precisely when it is needed most.

04  ·  Details

Three Cases That Ground the Argument

A cross-cultural academic contrast, a cognitive-science observation, and a proposed format bridge illustrate the stakes.

Vitri’s two academic cultures

An executive educated at Ivy League institutions in the US observes that LinkedIn was integral to scholarly life there — professors actively shared research and engaged the public. Returning to Indonesia, she finds professors largely absent from that same platform, despite comparable expertise — a gap in how credible knowledge reaches the public.

The tactile-comprehension effect

The physical newspaper’s finite pages, absence of blue light, and lack of pop-up interruption reduce the multitasking cognitive load that digital platforms impose — with the deliberate pace shown to support deeper comprehension and retention.

The two-minute bridge segment

A proposed format — a bite-sized news segment that lures with immediacy but delivers depth by its end — shows how digital tools can be used as a bridge to substantial reporting rather than a replacement for it.

05  ·  Evidence

The Conceptual Foundations Behind the Argument

Two classical concepts anchor the doxa/episteme distinction that runs through the piece.

EVIDENCE A
Foucault’s episteme

Michel Foucault’s historical and philosophical work frames “episteme” as the conceptual foundation of knowledge in each era — the bedrock on which knowledge claims and power structures stand.

EVIDENCE B
Bourdieu’s doxa

Pierre Bourdieu portrayed “doxa” as deeply ingrained beliefs a society wears as spectacles without recognizing them as beliefs — accepting them instead as self-evident truth.

EVIDENCE C
The ancient Greek root of episteme

“Episteme” traces to ancient Greek philosophy, often paralleled to “justified true belief” — knowledge anchored in evidence, reason, and systematic understanding, as embodied by the scientific method.

EVIDENCE D
The 2024 election as live test case

The approaching presidential election is cited directly in the piece as the concrete, time-bound stakes that make the doxa/episteme distinction more than an academic exercise.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: What Counts as “Digital Readiness” Today

The current measure stops at access. A complete one would measure discernment.

✗ What Digital Readiness Currently Measures
✗Internet access and smartphone adoption rates, treated as the finish line
✗Content volume and virality, rewarding the appeal of “doxa” over verified reporting
✓ What a Complete Measure Would Include
✓Population-level discernment — the ability to evaluate, not just consume, digital content
✓Mainstream outlets bridging bite-sized digital formats to verified, in-depth reporting
✓Human-capital investment — a well-fed, educated population led by visionary leaders — as the actual precondition for readiness

07  ·  Conclusion

Bridging, Not Abandoning, the Anchor

Three recommendations for mainstream media navigating the 2024 election cycle.

1

Design bite-sized digital formats explicitly as a bridge to verified, in-depth reporting — not as a self-contained substitute for it.

2

Treat digital transformation in newsrooms as a socio-cultural training problem, not merely a technology rollout.

3

During election cycles, actively promote vetted, layered-editorial sources as the counterweight to viral misinformation.

Closing

In the din of election campaigns, the clear, reasoned voice of trustworthy journalism is not just valuable; it is indispensable.
