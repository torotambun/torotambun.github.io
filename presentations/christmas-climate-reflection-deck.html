Christmas Reflection
on Climate Crisis

Climate discourse has fractured into believers, skeptics, and realists talking past one another — but a two-millennia-old tradition organized around three relationships, to the Supreme Being, to others, and to the inner self, offers a lens for engaging the crisis that the debate itself cannot supply.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
HBS Alumni and Affiliated Alumni of MIT Sloan School of Management.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

A holiday dismissed on four different grounds.

Each critique is answered by the same overlooked category: tradition as knowledge.

Situation

Christmas is a season that calls for reflection, renewal, and love, its universal themes centered on relationships between the self and the Supreme Being, the self and others, and the self and the inner self.

Complication

Christmas is often dismissed as commercialized, borrowed pagan ritual, theologically inauthentic, or empirically irrelevant to tangible crises like climate change — critiques that miss the deeper essence of tradition and history as ways of knowledge acquisition.

Question

Can a two-millennia-old religious tradition offer anything to a scientific, politically fractured debate like climate change?

Answer

Yes — not as a solution to climate divisions, but as a relational framework that reframes how those divisions are engaged, grounding action in stewardship, justice, and inner alignment rather than ideological camp.

02  ·  Gap

The divide over climate change is really a divide in relationship.

Believers, skeptics, and realists argue about data. The deeper fracture is elsewhere.

Core Gap

Climate change divides those who engage with it into broad categories: those certain of human-caused urgency, those who see it as part of natural cycles, realists seeking pragmatic solutions, skeptics questioning its scale or validity, and those who fear the narrative itself is deceitful propaganda. These divisions remain stark, but they point to a deeper issue: fractured relationships. Christmas traditions offer an opportunity — not to solve these divisions, but to provide a lens through which to better engage them.

03  ·  Framework

Three Relationships, One Relational Framework

Each relationship reframes a different dimension of the climate question.

01
Self & the Supreme Being

A call to honor creation as a trust — "The Lord took the man and placed him in the Garden to work it and to guard it" (Genesis 2:15) — treating the Earth as preserved for future generations, not exploited.

02
Self & Others

Climate change disproportionately displaces the most vulnerable; "love your neighbor as yourself" becomes a call to active solidarity, climate justice advocacy, and support for sustainable development.

03
Self & the Inner Self

"Take heed to yourself and guard your soul diligently" — addressing climate change begins with reflection on habits, values, and priorities, not policy or activism alone.

04  ·  Details

Why Tradition Counts as Knowledge

Two millennia, billions of lives, is itself a form of evidence.

Over two millennia, billions of individuals

Christmas has been celebrated by billions, if not trillions, of individuals cumulatively since the birth narrative became part of sacred scripture — enduring wisdom accumulated across generations, not a single dataset.

Mindfulness practice as a modern parallel

Gen Z's embrace of meditation and gratitude reflects a heightened awareness of intentional living that complements, rather than contradicts, Christmas's message of love, hope, and renewal.

Recurring crises, not new ones

Wars, displacement, migration, persecution, famine, and hate crimes have persisted for hundreds of thousands of years — recurring struggles that call for moments of pause and contemplation, not resignation.

05  ·  Evidence

Sources Behind the Argument

Scripture and the sociology of tradition as a knowledge category.

EVIDENCE A
Genesis 2:15

"The Lord took the man and placed him in the Garden to work it and to guard it" — the piece's grounding text for stewardship over exploitation.

EVIDENCE B
The "love your neighbor" commandment

This universal call to solidarity frames the piece's argument that climate justice is an extension of, not separate from, relational ethics.

EVIDENCE C
"Tradition" and "history" as ways of knowledge acquisition

This epistemological framing — tradition as a legitimate category of knowledge alongside the empirical and doctrinal — is the piece's central methodological move.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Solving the Debate, or Reframing the Engagement?

The relational framework was never meant to settle the argument.

✗ Expecting Christmas to Resolve Climate Divisions
✗Treating the relational framework as a rebuttal to skeptics or a data source for believers
✗Dismissing tradition-based reflection as irrelevant to empirical or policy debates
✓ Using It as a Lens for Engagement
✓Grounding action in stewardship, solidarity, and inner alignment regardless of one's position in the climate debate
✓Treating fractured relationships, not contested data, as the deeper issue worth addressing first
✓Rising above divisions of belief, perspective, or ideology to embrace shared responsibility for the world
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Transformation Begins With Love

Three relationships, held together, as a path toward reconciliation.

1

Honor creation as a trust, treating the Earth as preserved for future generations rather than a resource for endless consumption.

2

Advocate actively for those most affected by climate change, supporting policies that address climate justice and sustainable development.

3

Reflect on personal habits, values, and priorities as the starting point for sustainable action, not merely policy or activism.

4

Rise above divisions of belief, perspective, or ideology, embracing shared responsibility to care for the world.

Closing

Christmas is not merely a day — it is a way forward, offering a lens for reflection, action, and enduring connection in the face of the challenges humanity faces.
