Beyond ‘Greenflation’: Multi-Solving
and Equity in Climate Discourse

A debate question that treats “greenflation” as a vocabulary test exposes climate discourse stuck at the memorizing tier — when En-ROADS modeling shows that reaching even 2°C, let alone the Paris Agreement’s 1.5°C, requires a specific, quantified stack of policies applied together, with equity built in from the start.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
An En-Roads MIT Climate Interactive Simulation ambassador. The views expressed are his own.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

A debate-stage question at the “memorizing” tier.

“What is greenflation?”, posed on national TV, becomes the opening for a much harder quantitative question.

Situation

The question “What is greenflation?” was posed during a vice-presidential debate — relevant to climate change, but pitched at a basic definitional level.

Complication

This represents the “Memorizing” category in Bloom’s Taxonomy, a focus on elementary concepts over the in-depth policy analysis that COP28’s actual pledges require.

Question

What would it actually take — quantifiably — to move from today’s pledges to the Paris Agreement’s 1.5°C goal?

Answer

A staged, cumulative stack of policies — renewables, nuclear, efficiency, methane cuts, CCS/CDR, electrification, fossil-fuel subsidy reform, coal phase-down, and large-scale afforestation — modeled through En-ROADS, is what actually closes the gap; and closing it responsibly requires multi-solving and equity built in from the start, not bolted on after.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

Individual pledges are mistaken for sufficient policy.

Ambitious-sounding commitments, taken one at a time, obscure how far short of 1.5°C they leave the world.

Core Gap

Public and media climate discourse treats individual pledges and buzzwords like “greenflation” as if they constituted substantive policy debate — without the quantitative, cumulative modeling needed to know whether any given combination of policies actually reaches 1.5°C. En-ROADS simulations show even the sum of COP28’s major pledges, taken together, lands closer to 1.5–1.6°C only after every major lever is pulled — not from any single pledge alone.

03  ·  Framework

Three Disciplines Behind a Rigorous Climate Conversation

Each discipline addresses a different failure mode in how climate policy gets discussed.

01
The Memorizing-Tier Problem

Bloom’s Taxonomy diagnoses debate-level discourse as stuck at basic recall — raising concern about leadership’s ability to navigate genuinely complex policy tradeoffs.

02
Cumulative Modeling Discipline

En-ROADS requires stacking policies — not evaluating them one at a time — to see their combined, quantified effect on projected global temperature by 2100.

03
Multi-Solving and Equity

En-ROADS’ guiding questions — who needs to be involved, how can we strengthen connections — insist that policy design account for a wide range of needs and viewpoints from the outset.

04  ·  Details

The Stack, Modeled Step by Step

Each policy layer, applied cumulatively through En-ROADS, chips away at the temperature projection.

Renewables, nuclear, and efficiency

Tripling renewable and nuclear capacity pledges, plus a 5% annual efficiency boost across new equipment, together move the needle by roughly 0.6°C — bringing the trajectory down to 2.7°C.

Methane, CCS/CDR, and electrification

Building on the 2021 Global Methane Pledge, plus a conservative CDR/CCS “speed limit” and electrification subsidies, layers on further reductions — bringing the trajectory to roughly 2.2°C.

Fossil-fuel subsidy reform and coal phase-down

Reversing an estimated $1.3 trillion in fossil fuel subsidies (IMF, 2022) into a tax, and halting new coal infrastructure in major developing coal-reliant economies, brings the trajectory to roughly 1.9°C.

Oil-and-gas transition and afforestation

A gradual oil-and-gas transition, deeper methane cuts, and afforestation across roughly 400 million hectares are what the En-ROADS simulation shows is required to reach a 1.5°C scenario — a feasible but unprecedented combination.

05  ·  Evidence

Four Sources Behind the Argument

Each source supplies a specific quantified claim, not a general impression.

EVIDENCE A
The En-ROADS Climate Simulation

A collaboration between Climate Interactive and the MIT Sloan Sustainability Initiative, En-ROADS is the modeling tool behind every temperature figure cited in the piece.

EVIDENCE B
COP28’s Global Stocktake and pledges

The COP28 conference in Dubai produced the renewables-tripling, nuclear-tripling, and efficiency-doubling pledges the piece stress-tests through simulation.

EVIDENCE C
IMF (2022) fossil fuel subsidy estimate

The International Monetary Fund calculated approximately $1.3 trillion in fossil fuel subsidies globally in 2022, the baseline for the piece’s reverse-subsidy tax proposal.

EVIDENCE D
The Paris Agreement’s 1.5°C goal

The Paris Agreement’s more ambitious target, against the COP28 pledges’ sub-2°C trajectory, is the benchmark that motivates the piece’s call for additional strategies.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: What Climate Discourse Currently Rewards

Ambition, stated individually, is not the same as a modeled pathway to 1.5°C.

✗ What Discourse Currently Rewards
✗Buzzword definitions and standalone pledges treated as sufficient progress
✗Ambition assessed one policy at a time, without cumulative modeling
✓ What a Rigorous Pathway Requires
✓Quantified, cumulative modeling of policy combinations against the 1.5°C benchmark
✓Equity and multi-solving designed into policy from the outset, not added afterward
✓Public debate pushed beyond Bloom’s “memorizing” tier toward genuine policy analysis

07  ·  Conclusion

From Buzzwords to a Modeled Pathway

Three recommendations for raising the level of Indonesia’s climate conversation.

1

Push public climate debate beyond Bloom’s “memorizing” tier toward genuine, quantified policy analysis.

2

Use simulation tools like En-ROADS to test whether policy combinations actually close the gap to 1.5°C, not just sound ambitious individually.

3

Design climate policy explicitly for multi-solving and equity from the outset, not as an afterthought.

Closing

These comprehensive strategies go beyond superficial dialogue to effectively address the complexities of climate change with a focus on multi-solving and equity for all stakeholders.
