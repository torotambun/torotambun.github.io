What Japan’s Basic Values Teach About Building an Innovation Nation

Innovation labs that copy Japan’s co-working spaces, its garbage sorting, its spotless toilets, are copying artefacts. What actually produced Japan’s innovation record are the basic values underneath them — and those take two generations to plant, not two quarters.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Dosen Industri, Sekolah Tinggi Elektro dan Informatika, Institut Teknologi Bandung (STEI-ITB)
© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun

Ryuta Takeda, CEO of Leave a Nest — one of Japan’s most storied techno-parks — hosted me on a public holiday and walked me through the history of his movement. What struck me is how many self-declared “innovative labs” in this country try to learn from places like his and end up copying artefacts: the warehouse-style co-working space, the business model, the event format — without ever asking why those things worked in the first place.

The why, in Japan’s case, traces back further than most people assume: a long history of investing in human capital that predates World War II. Investment in human capital — not reconstruction aid, not export policy alone — is the factor that best explains Japan’s postwar “economic miracle,” and the same held for Germany and Italy, the other objects of the Marshall Plan. The working ethic, the productive mindset, the long-term horizon, the respect for both mind and hand were already there.

© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun

Hofstede’s national culture index gives a helicopter view of what that substrate looks like: Japan scores 95 on Masculinity, 92 on Uncertainty Avoidance, 88 on Long-Term Orientation, and just 42 on Indulgence — a culture built for maximum predictability, generational patience, and restraint. Those four numbers explain more about Japan’s innovation record than any policy paper.

01
Queuing & respect for others’ space

From a 1988 exchange year in Tokyo to visits decades later, the same discipline holds: people queue without complaint, give way to the needy, and treat a stranger’s space as worth protecting.

02
Cleanliness as gratitude

Garbage sorting at every block is not a chore, it is an ascetic practice. “Not being wasteful is not just about avoiding waste — it embodies a spirit of gratitude towards objects” (Matsumoto, 2018).

03
Respect for pedestrians

Sidewalks function almost as sacred space, governed by an informal nine-commandment etiquette — priority to pedestrians, no phone-checking while walking, take your trash home.

04
Spotless public toilets

Rooted in a Zen belief that a toilet is where a monk once attained enlightenment — hence the discipline to leave “not even a fingerprint behind.”

05
Public gardens

Not mock-up gardens, but places where, as one source put it, “what a person sees in a garden reflects what is in their heart” — nature as a place of dialogue, not decoration.

06
Fences, not walls

Japanese construction fencing is designed to harmonize with its surroundings rather than divide from them — “an intuitive understanding of the emotional relationship between human beings and their environment.”

My host family from that 1988 exchange offers the clearest evidence that this is not aesthetic decoration. Otosan was diagnosed with lung cancer, lost his voice for a year after surgery, injured his back gardening, then ruptured his kneecap. His wife was diagnosed with breast cancer while caring for him and lost her hair to radiotherapy. When I visited, I found two conquerors, not two victims — their first dinner out in three years, Otosan’s first drive on his own in years, a driving-license renewal passed “with excellence” two weeks after being wheeled in for the test.

Growth mindset instead of fixed mindset. No time for self-pity. Acting on what is in their hands instead of complaining about what is beyond them. Support from their nucleus people. None of that is an “artefact” you can install in a co-working space over a weekend.

The basic values are where you start, before any strategy, before any innovation roadmap. For a nation, that may take two generations to build. Do not jump the queue — if you do, you are at your peril.
