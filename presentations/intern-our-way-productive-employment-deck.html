We cannot intern our way to productive employment

Indonesia cannot subsidise its way into graduate employment while the productive base that must absorb those graduates has stopped gaining economic weight. The binding constraint is production capacity, not worker readiness.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Director, Yayasan Mens et Manus  ·  Doctorate, Bandung Institute of Technology (ITB)
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
Introduction

A skills programme aimed at a demand-side problem

Six months of subsidised placement can supply experience, mentoring and certification. It cannot supply the orders, machinery and payroll capacity that turn a graduate into an employee.

Situation

Indonesia faces persistent graduate unemployment. The 2026 government programme places graduates inside host companies for six months, with the state paying an allowance equal to the applicable local minimum wage, plus work-accident and death protection and competency certification.

Complication

The base that should absorb those graduates is not growing. Manufacturing’s share of GDP peaked near 22.7 percent in 2000 and stood at about 19.1 percent in 2025 — a fall of 3.6 percentage points. Meanwhile only 29.2 percent of participants reach even the reported offer-or-recruitment outcome.

Question

Is the state financing induction into jobs, or financing six months before unemployment returns? And if firms lack the capacity to hire, does making labour temporarily cheaper change anything durable?

Answer

Redirect public money from lowering the cost of temporary labour to removing the specific obstacles that stop commercially viable firms from adding production capacity — a Production-to-Jobs Compact, where the employer hires because output and orders justify another position on the payroll.

Gap

Employability policy prepares workers for firms it has not checked can hire them

The skills conversation has a well-developed supply side and an assumed demand side. The assumption is doing the load-bearing work.

In 2019 I took part in a focus-group discussion on the skills Indonesian workers would need in the years ahead. Employers wanted technical competence, problem-solving, communication, digital familiarity and closer links between training providers and industry. Every item concerned the worker. The discussion gave less attention to whether enough firms would have orders, machinery and payroll capacity to employ those workers afterward.

Core Gap

Policy treats graduate unemployment as a readiness deficit in workers, when the evidence points to an absorption deficit in firms. A programme designed against the wrong constraint can run at full capacity, report strong completion, and still leave the employment problem exactly where it was — because nothing it funds increases the number of positions an employer can afford to carry.

Asymmetry Worth Naming

The 2026 programme is highly favourable to host companies. Government pays the participant’s allowance, the accident and death protection, and the certification. The company provides placement, a mentor and a learning programme. The state bears most of the direct financial cost of having the participant inside the firm — which is precisely the arrangement most likely to attract firms that would not otherwise hire.

Framework

Four forces the internship instrument does not touch

Each is independently sufficient to blunt the programme. Together they explain why absorption stays low regardless of how well the training is delivered.

01
Structural
The productive base has stopped gaining weight
Manufacturing’s share of GDP fell from about 22.7 percent in 2000 to about 19.1 percent in 2025. Fewer firms are accumulating the machinery, orders and working capital that create permanent positions. Training does not reverse a structural trend in industrial capacity.
02
Cultural
Stable employment is the rational first destination
In groups I have studied, between 93 and 99 percent of students preferred stable employment to entrepreneurship. Historical accounts of premodern and colonial Indonesia help explain the persistence: institutional position offered income, protection and standing, while independent enterprise exposed families to commercial uncertainty.
03
Interpretive
Micro-enterprise is being misread as preference
Indonesia’s millions of micro and small enterprises are not proof that workers chose entrepreneurship over employment. Many exist because their owners needed immediate income once stable employment was unavailable, and stay hand-to-mouth rather than becoming firms that accumulate capital and hire others.
04
Instrumental
The subsidy lowers cost without creating a position
A six-month, state-funded participant reduces a firm’s labour cost for six months. Nothing in that transaction obliges, or enables, the firm to add a permanent line to its payroll. The instrument acts on price when the constraint is capacity.

Details

Five unintended effects that can reduce net job creation

A longer policy paper identifies eighteen possible unintended effects. These five deserve particular attention, because each can either shrink net employment or make the programme look more successful than it is.

1  ·  The six-month revolving door
If one publicly funded participant leaves and another enters the same function, a company can keep receiving subsidised labour indefinitely without ever creating an additional permanent position. The programme reduces the firm’s labour cost in perpetuity while its payroll never moves.
2  ·  Displacement of ordinary entry-level employment
An intern can perform work that would otherwise go to a newly recruited employee. Firms that once paid for graduate induction can shift part of that cost to government. Entry-level wages face pressure too: a company choosing between an employee whose wage it must pay and an intern whose allowance government finances faces a clear cost difference.
3  ·  Deadweight expenditure
Some firms would have recruited or trained the same graduates without public support. In those cases government pays for activity that would have happened anyway. The spending is real; the additional employment is not.
4  ·  Outcome gaming
Completion, certification, a job offer and sustained employment are four different outcomes, and only the last one matters. A programme can report strong completion while many participants still fail to remain in paid work after the placement ends.
5  ·  Adverse selection of hosts
Firms with genuine vacancies may simply recruit directly. The firms most attracted to the subsidy may be those least likely to hire afterward. If so, the programme concentrates among companies interested in cheap labour rather than companies building a real recruitment pipeline — the opposite of the intended sorting.

Evidence

What is on the record

Four checkable claims. None requires assuming bad faith by any participating company — the last comes from the ministry’s own evaluation.

EVIDENCE A
Deindustrialisation is measured, not alleged
World Bank data put manufacturing’s share of GDP at about 22.7 percent in 2000 and about 19.1 percent in 2025 — a decline of 3.6 percentage points from the peak. The absorptive base has been contracting in relative terms for a quarter-century.
EVIDENCE B
The cost split is documented
Ministry of Manpower socialisation material sets out the arrangement: government pays an allowance equal to the applicable local minimum wage, together with work-accident and death protection and competency certification. The company provides placement, mentor and learning programme.
EVIDENCE C
29.2 percent is past normal attrition
An induction period is not a guarantee of employment, and some participants will not be hired for reasons of performance, fit or business conditions. But when only 29.2 percent reach even the reported offer-or-recruitment outcome, the programme may be preparing far more graduates than participating firms can or will absorb.
EVIDENCE D
The ministry’s own evaluation flags the pattern
The Ministry of Manpower’s 2026 legal evaluation of the broader apprenticeship system describes apprentices performing core production functions, working under conditions similar to regular employees while receiving only an allowance, and facing weak enforcement and barriers to complaints.
What This Does Not Claim

These risks do not prove that every participating company behaves this way. They establish that firm-level outcomes should be examined before repeated subsidised cohorts become routine.

Implications

A programme can be fully visible and still unmeasured

Everything the programme currently counts happens during the six months. Everything that determines whether it worked happens after.

Visible and Counted
✓Completion rates for each cohort
✓Competency certificates issued
✓Allowances disbursed and protection provided
✓Placements filled and host companies enrolled
✓The reported offer-or-recruitment figure at exit
Missing and Decisive
✕Whether the host’s payroll actually grew
✕Whether ordinary entry-level hiring fell as placements rose
✕How much spending was deadweight — hiring that would have happened anyway
✕Employment status at 3, 6 and 12 months, with wages, hours, contract status and social protection
✕Whether any of it moves manufacturing toward the RPJMN target
The Plans Already Say This

Law No. 59 of 2024, establishing the RPJPN 2025–2045, names early deindustrialisation, low productivity and high informality among Indonesia’s development challenges. The RPJMN 2025–2029 uses a manufacturing baseline of 18.67 percent of GDP and targets 21.90 percent by 2029, while seeking to raise the formal-employment share from 23.96 percent to 46 percent. Those are linked targets: formal jobs will not rise at that scale unless productive firms expand, and firms do not expand because more graduates hold certificates.

The Fiscal Trade-off

Money spent financing temporary labour cannot also finance machinery, supplier qualification or industrial facilities. If public spending makes workers temporarily cheaper while companies still lack the assets needed to hire them permanently, the underlying employment constraint remains untouched — and has been paid for twice.

Conclusion

Fund the capacity to hire, not the price of not hiring

Keep apprenticeships as genuine induction and learning. But stop asking them to do work that only productive capacity can do.

1
Screen the host before approving the placement
Kemnaker should compare a host’s payroll, recent entry-level hiring, layoffs and previous internship cohorts before approving another subsidised placement. A firm whose payroll never moves is not a recruitment pipeline.
2
Make repetition trigger review
Repeated internships in the same occupation should trigger review, especially where ordinary hiring is falling at the same time. That is the signature of the revolving door.
3
Split the cost once the work becomes production
Public funding can pay for structured learning and independent certification. But once participants contribute to normal production, the company should bear part of their labour cost.
4
Follow participants after they leave
Publish employment status at three, six and twelve months, together with wages, working hours, contract status and social protection. Without those data, a high completion rate can coexist with repeated returns to unemployment.
5
Remove the obstacle to the production line, not the wage bill
Consider a domestic supplier with a buyer ready to purchase more, who already holds the order but lacks the machinery to raise output. The entrepreneur puts in equity; a bank or leasing company independently assesses the machinery and working-capital requirement. Where a commercially viable project still faces a defined financing obstacle, government can provide a limited guarantee on qualifying machinery finance, pay for required supplier certification, or resolve a site or infrastructure constraint.
6
Adopt a Production-to-Jobs Compact
Kemnaker identifies and certifies workers for the occupations the new production line requires — but the employer hires them because additional output and customer orders justify another position on the payroll. The company still carries the commercial risk and pays its workers from sales. Public money creates productive capacity rather than repeatedly lowering the cost of temporary labour.

The programme does not need 100 percent absorption, but it should produce a clear majority. If only around three in ten reach even the reported offer-or-recruitment stage, government should ask whether it is financing induction into jobs — or simply financing six months before unemployment returns.

— Closing argument, The Jakarta Post, 29 August 2026
