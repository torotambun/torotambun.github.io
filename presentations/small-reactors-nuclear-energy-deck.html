Small Reactors, the Future of
Indonesia's Nuclear Energy

The structural lens reveals what political and cultural analysis missed: the Java-Bali grid already carries a 4 GW electricity oversupply, and Indonesia's 2050 energy roadmaps exclude nuclear entirely — so the real question is not whether to build large reactors, but what becomes of the nuclear workforce Indonesia has already trained.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
MIT Climate Interactive EN-ROADS Simulation Ambassador and a Nuclear Engineering UGM alumnus.

01  ·  Introduction

A third lens, prompted by audience pushback.

Two earlier columns on Indonesia's nuclear ambitions drew a consistent, cynical, and ultimately valid piece of feedback.

Situation

Two prior columns applied political and cultural lenses to Indonesia's nuclear energy option, published 6 and 20 July.

Complication

Audience feedback consistently pointed to a hard structural fact: Indonesia has other priorities — economic growth, education, healthcare, trade, political stability — ahead of untapped nuclear resources.

Question

Is that feedback actually valid, and if large-scale nuclear isn’t materializing, what happens to the workforce already trained for it?

Answer

The feedback holds up under the structural lens — but the answer isn’t abandoning nuclear engineering, it’s redirecting it toward small modular reactors and microreactors sized for remote areas and data centers.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

The debate skipped whether the grid even needs the power.

Political and cultural readiness were examined at length; grid supply and demand were not.

Core Gap

The Java-Bali region currently carries a 4 GW electricity oversupply, down from 7 GW at the end of 2022, driven by industrial demand and economic growth that has consistently underperformed the 7–8 percent rate assumed by the 35,000 MW megaproject. National growth has instead run around 5–6 percent. Indonesia’s long-term energy roadmaps to 2050 do not prioritize nuclear energy in the mix at all. None of this — the supply-demand reality nuclear would have to fit into — was addressed by the political or cultural analysis alone.

04  ·  Details

The Numbers Behind the Pivot

Grid oversupply on one side, a concrete small-reactor market on the other.

A 4 GW oversupply against a 5–6 percent growth reality

The 35,000 MW megaproject was built on an assumed 7–8 percent growth rate; actual growth of 5–6 percent left Java-Bali with a 4 GW electricity oversupply, prompting ESDM to delay commercial operation dates on new plants to ease the burden on PLN.

77 MW per SMR against an 80 MW data center demand

NuScale’s small modular reactors each generate 77 megawatts of continuous, carbon-free power — close to the up-to-80-megawatt demand of AI-focused data centers, compared with the 32 megawatts a typical data center uses.

1 MW, deployed in days

Radiant Industries’ microreactors generate 1 megawatt and can become fully operational within a few days — well suited to edge data centers, microgrids, and cleaner alternatives to diesel backup generators.

05  ·  Evidence

Sources Behind the Argument

Domestic energy policy, international commitments, and small-reactor industry developments ground the piece’s claims.

EVIDENCE A
ESDM's commercial-operation-date delays

The Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources is delaying the commercial operation dates of several new power plants specifically to alleviate the take-or-pay burden on PT PLN — direct evidence of the oversupply constraint.

EVIDENCE B
Kyoto Protocol and Paris Agreement ratification

Indonesia ratified the Kyoto Protocol in 2004 and the Paris Agreement in 2016, yet continued building new coal power plants — a gap between climate commitments and energy-mix reality that frames the piece’s skepticism.

EVIDENCE C
NuScale Power's SMR-data center framing

NuScale highlights the compatibility of its small modular reactors with the energy requirements of the data industry, positioning data centers as a prime target for small nuclear.

EVIDENCE D
Radiant Industries' microreactors

Radiant’s 1-megawatt units, deployable within days, demonstrate a concrete near-term path for redirecting nuclear engineering expertise toward smaller, flexible applications.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: What Happens to a Trained Workforce?

The risk is not that nuclear power fails to launch — it’s that trained engineers are left with nowhere to go.

✗ Treating This as the Same Question as Before
✗Debate framed only as “should Indonesia build nuclear power plants,” ignoring grid oversupply
✗A trained nuclear engineering workforce left with no defined pathway if large-scale plants don’t materialize
✓ Reframing as a Niche-Energy, Workforce-Utilization Problem
✓Redirecting expertise toward SMRs and microreactors for remote cities and data centers
✓Matching reactor scale to actual, unmet demand rather than the oversupplied national grid
✓Preserving the investment already made in nuclear education and training

07  ·  Conclusion

A Bright Future for a Smaller Nuclear Footprint

Four recommendations for closing this three-part series.

1

Redirect nuclear engineering investment toward small modular reactors and microreactors sized for remote and off-grid applications.

2

Target AI data centers and power-hungry server infrastructure as a concrete near-term market for small reactors.

3

Build an explicit transition pathway — including renewables integration and carbon capture — so nuclear engineering expertise isn’t lost to unemployment or emigration.

4

Read the political, cultural, and structural lenses together going forward, rather than debating each energy question in isolation.

Closing

By focusing on these smaller-scale applications, Indonesia can efficiently utilize its trained workforce, manage its energy surplus, and align with sustainability goals.
