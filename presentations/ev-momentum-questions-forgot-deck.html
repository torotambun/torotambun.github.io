Indonesia's EV Momentum
and the Questions We Forget to Ask

From 14,000 battery electric vehicles in 2022 to a projected 374,000 this year, Indonesia's numbers show motion. Whether they show direction, or merely activity mistaken for authority, is a different question.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
EN-ROADS Climate Simulation Ambassador; Doctoral Researcher, SBM-ITB.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Visibility is not the same as clarity

The regulatory architecture is real. Whether it adds up to transformation is the open question.

Situation

Indonesia is pushing hard on vehicle electrification: from 14,000 domestically produced BEVs in 2022 to a projected 374,000 by year-end, nearly IDR 20 trillion in new investment, and Presidential Regulation 79/2023 laying out local content requirements, tax incentives, and production guarantees.

Complication

As an EN-ROADS climate simulation ambassador, the author notes a sobering structural fact: even a full global EV switch would cut global temperature by roughly 0.1°F — and only if electricity comes from clean sources. Indonesia's grid remains heavily coal-reliant.

Question

What problem are we actually solving — emissions, employment, or sovereignty? And does the current strategy solve any of them fully?

Answer

Each goal requires a different missing piece: grid decarbonization for emissions, skills evolution for employment, and upstream control — not just local assembly — for sovereignty. Without these, Indonesia risks confusing volume for progress.

02  ·  Gap

Activity Is Not Authority

A factory onshore does not automatically mean decision-making onshore.

Core Gap

Local content targets measure where production happens, not who owns the technology, designs the systems, or controls the supply chain. A country can assemble what it does not command. The EV Tech Summit presentation from the Coordinating Ministry for Infrastructure and Regional Development does not yet address upstream control — who owns the battery design, who sets software standards, who controls the mining-to-manufacturing flow. Sovereignty without these levers may remain performative.

03  ·  Framework

Three Goals, Three Missing Pieces

Electrification serves different purposes depending on which problem you think you're solving.

1
If the Goal Is Emissions

BEVs must be tied to a decarbonized grid, and transport policy must include public mobility and equity — not just tailpipe-to-smokestack emissions transfer.

2
If the Goal Is Employment

The country must invest not only in how many people are hired but how their skills evolve — battery systems, embedded software, power electronics, safety testing.

3
If the Goal Is Sovereignty

Factories must be followed by learning curves, standards ownership, and the ability to shape — not just receive — the global EV roadmap.

04  ·  Details

The Regulatory Architecture

Presidential Regulation 79/2023 is structured, and far from symbolic.

Local content targets: 40% by 2026, 60% by 2029, 80% thereafter

Manufacturers must provide production guarantees equivalent to the number of BEVs they import, tied to tax incentives (reduced VAT and PPnBM) and enforced via bank guarantees.

1.5 million jobs, IDR 190 trillion in GDP contribution

Indonesia's automotive sector is a real economic backbone, and the country leads ASEAN in car sales — but the employment argument is framed through scale, not structure.

BYD has seven months left to fulfill its plant commitment

If delivered, a milestone — but presence alone is not depth. If the plant operates mainly as an assembly site while key components and decisions stay imported, Indonesia risks remaining an import-based market in structure.

05  ·  Evidence

The Sovereignty Reframe, and Its Limits

One of the presentation's strongest moves — and its biggest blind spot.

STRENGTH
BEVs framed as a sovereignty tool, not just a carbon solution

Indonesia imports oil and LPG but generates electricity domestically. Electrifying transport reduces dependence on volatile global fuel markets — a trade-driven argument, arguably more politically grounded than a climate-driven one.

BLIND SPOT
155 GW of solar potential, still largely untapped

The presentation does not map a policy mechanism to accelerate grid decarbonization alongside vehicle electrification, leaving BEVs at risk of becoming just another fossil-fueled load.

06  ·  Implications

Movement vs. Meaning

Two paths, only one of which ends in structural transformation.

✗ Volume Mistaken for Progress
✗Treating BEV production numbers as the end goal, without asking whether the grid, workforce, or supply chain are catching up
✗No urban mobility logic — no mention of public transit integration, rural access, or transport equity
✗Assuming a factory onshore equals decision-making onshore
✓ Structural Transformation
✓Planning the transition from assembler to innovator, not assuming it happens on its own
✓Building vocational pathways, technical certification, and university R&D investment alongside factory jobs
✓Pursuing upstream control — battery design, software standards, mining-to-manufacturing flow — not just assembly presence
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Define What Works Here

Adoption without authorship is not sovereignty.

1

Tie BEV expansion explicitly to grid decarbonization targets and public mobility policy — not just production volume.

2

Build the workforce pipeline deliberately: vocational pathways, certification, and university R&D for battery systems, embedded software, and power electronics.

3

Pursue upstream control over design, standards, and supply chain — the difference between hosting production and owning it.

Closing

The task now is not just to adopt what works elsewhere, but to make sure that we define what works here. Not just to produce what others design, but to make sure we don't remain dependent on other countries for parts, tools, or technology.
