Palm Plantations Cannot
Replace Forests, Mr. President

President Prabowo's claim that oil palm plantations can substitute for forests — "Oil palm is a tree, right?" — collapses under system dynamics analysis: natural forests sequester two to three times more carbon and support ten times more biodiversity than the monocultures meant to replace them.

01  ·  Introduction

"Oil palm is a tree, right?"

A president's reassurance meets a system that doesn't reassure back.

Situation

President Prabowo Subianto stated that Indonesia should increase palm oil plantations without fear of deforestation, reasoning that "oil palm is a tree, right?" — a claim made as Indonesia lost roughly 27.7 million hectares of tree cover between 2001 and 2020.

Complication

Critics note weak enforcement of forestry policy, but the deeper flaw is conceptual: a plantation may contain trees, yet it lacks the ecological complexity and functionality of a natural forest.

Question

Can oil palm plantations functionally replace the forests they clear?

Answer

No — ecologically, climatically, and socially, natural forests and palm plantations are not interchangeable, and treating them as such accelerates carbon release, biodiversity loss, and displacement rather than preventing it.

02  ·  Gap

A tree is not a forest.

The conflation is the whole error.

Core Gap

Prabowo's claim that palm plantations can replace forests is fundamentally flawed when viewed through ecological, climatic, and societal lenses. While a plantation may contain trees, it lacks the complexity and functionality of natural forests — a critical difference that underpins the broader environmental and societal consequences of deforestation. Natural forests sequester two to three times more carbon than palm plantations; peatlands alone can release up to 55 times their stored carbon during conversion.

03  ·  Framework

Three Disparities Between Forest and Plantation

Carbon, biodiversity, and soil — each tells the same story.

01
Carbon Sequestration

Natural forests sequester two to three times more carbon than palm plantations, storing it in both biomass and soil; peatland conversion can release up to 55 times the stored carbon, making palm cultivation a net emitter in its early years.

02
Biodiversity

Indonesia's rainforests host over half the world's terrestrial species, including orangutans and Sumatran tigers; palm plantations support less than 10 percent of that biodiversity, reducing rich ecosystems to biologically impoverished monocultures.

03
Soil & Water Quality

Natural forests enrich soil through organic recycling over centuries; plantations strip nutrients, require heavy fertilizer, and pollute nearby water systems with agricultural runoff.

04  ·  Details

The Feedback Loops Sustaining Deforestation

Reinforcing loops drive expansion; balancing loops offer the only counterweight.

The economic-incentive reinforcing loop

Global demand for palm oil drives plantation expansion, generating short-term economic gains for local economies and governments — financial benefits that reinforce further deforestation.

The certification-governance loop

Consumer and regulatory demand for sustainably sourced palm oil encourages certification adoption, but weak enforcement in poorly governed regions undermines certification credibility and allows non-compliance to go unchecked.

The degraded-land balancing loop

Incentivizing agricultural expansion onto already-degraded land reduces deforestation pressure on pristine forest — but only succeeds with strong institutional frameworks, monitoring, and sustained investment.

05  ·  Evidence

Sources Behind the Argument

Global forest data and climate modeling ground the ecological claims.

EVIDENCE A
Global Forest Watch tree-cover data

Indonesia's loss of approximately 27.7 million hectares of tree cover between 2001 and 2020 — a 17.3 percent reduction, roughly the size of New Zealand — anchors the piece's account of deforestation's scale.

EVIDENCE B
CarbonBrief peatland emissions reporting

Reports on peatland draining and burning for palm oil plantations releasing substantial greenhouse gases support the piece's carbon-release argument.

EVIDENCE C
The En-ROADS Climate Simulation

Modeling on deforestation's acceleration of climate change and the limits of afforestation to offset large-scale forest loss underpins the piece's systemic argument.

EVIDENCE D
Malaysia's MPOCC certification model

Malaysia's Malaysian Palm Oil Certification Council, while imperfect, illustrates the piece's point that stricter sustainability regulation is achievable in the region.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Substitution or System Redesign?

Treating plantations as forest-equivalents guarantees the wrong policy response.

✗ Treating Plantations as Forest-Equivalent
✗Expanding palm oil plantations on the assumption that tree cover alone offsets deforestation's ecological cost
✗Prioritizing short-term economic gain from exports over the compounding cost of biodiversity and carbon loss
✓ Aligning Reinforcing and Balancing Loops
✓Directing agricultural expansion onto already-degraded land rather than pristine forest
✓Strengthening certification enforcement so sustainability standards translate into real practice, not just paperwork
✓Harmonizing economic incentives with environmental realities through robust institutional frameworks

07  ·  Conclusion

Economic Development and Ecological Stewardship Can Coexist

Four steps toward aligning the loops rather than fighting them.

1

Direct new agricultural expansion onto degraded land rather than pristine forest, supported by strong institutional monitoring.

2

Strengthen enforcement of sustainability certification so standards translate into verified practice, not symbolic compliance.

3

Align economic incentives for producers with the true long-term cost of carbon release and biodiversity loss.

4

Reject the framing that a plantation and a forest are interchangeable, and build policy on their actual ecological difference.

Closing

For Indonesia to lead on the global stage, it must show that economic development and environmental stewardship can coexist. Failing to act risks losing valuable ecosystems permanently, trading long-term environmental stability for short-term economic benefits at the expense of future generations.
