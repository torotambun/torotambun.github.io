Addressing Mental Health and
Building Resilience in Education

Educators face a false choice between supporting students’ mental health and building their resilience — but a teaching bootcamp’s pivot from a rigid “fire hose” method to individualized, anti-fragile pacing shows the two are not opposites; they succeed together, or not at all.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
HBS alumnus and affiliated alumnus of MIT Sloan School of Management, teaching entrepreneurship in the Faculty of Agriculture, UGM.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

A bootcamp, a frustrated colleague, and a question with no easy answer.

As World Suicide Prevention Day approaches, a teaching journey offers a way through an increasingly common tension in the classroom.

Situation

Educators face a dual challenge: addressing the increasing mental health needs of students while encouraging them to develop the resilience necessary to thrive through adversity.

Complication

A fellow teacher’s frustration captures the bind: “So many students run to mental health as an excuse. While I support them, I don’t endorse a weak, non-resilient mentality. How can I address this without opposing the mental health movement?”

Question

Are support and resilience actually in opposition, or has education been framing them as a trade-off when they aren’t one?

Answer

They work together, not in opposition — the goal is helping students grow stronger through life’s difficulties, using individualized support paired with maintained discipline and expectations, rather than avoiding difficulty altogether.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

Teachers are left without a way to tell genuine need from avoidance.

Cultural stigma pushes in one direction; fear of causing harm pushes in the other — and educators are caught in between.

Core Gap

In Indonesia, mental health issues among students are often stigmatized, meaning students may either avoid seeking help when they need it, or use the notion of mental health as an excuse to avoid challenges. At the same time, teachers are increasingly hesitant to apply necessary pressure or enforce discipline, fearing it might be perceived as harmful to a student’s mental health. Neither cultural stigma nor conflict-avoidance gives educators a workable way to distinguish genuine struggle from avoidance — leaving the balance between support and accountability largely undiscussed.

03  ·  Framework

Three Principles for Holding Support and Resilience Together

Each principle addresses a different part of the tension teachers described.

01
Individualized Response

Each student’s needs should be approached individually — some will need direct support, while others may benefit from being encouraged to develop resilience.

02
Anti-Fragility

Individuals grow stronger through stress and challenges; support should be available for those who need it, but persistence and adaptive capacity must be developed too.

03
Discipline Alongside Support

Discipline remains a crucial aspect of education — students need structure and expectations, and the pressure of deadlines and challenges helps them build resilience.

04  ·  Details

What Changed When the Fire Hose Slowed Down

A concrete pedagogical pivot, described in three parts.

The problem: a one-size-fits-all fire hose

Many students, especially those from more gradual-paced educational backgrounds, struggled with the fast, intensive “fire hose” teaching method — a one-size-fits-all approach that wasn’t effective given the diversity of the cohort.

The adjustment: slower pacing, more storytelling, one-on-one engagement

Slowing sessions down, adding storytelling, and increasing one-on-one engagement allowed each student to be met where they were — in many cases, it felt like individual mentoring rather than group instruction.

The result: performance and real growth

Students not only performed well but demonstrated real growth, understanding the framework deeply and applying it in meaningful ways — evidence that adjustment and rigor are not mutually exclusive.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Two Ways Educators Respond to the Tension

The instinct to pick a side — support or resilience — is itself part of the problem.

✗ Treating Support and Resilience as Opposites
✗Teachers hesitant to apply pressure or enforce discipline, fearing it will be seen as harmful
✗A one-size-fits-all teaching pace applied regardless of student background or need
✓ Treating Support and Resilience as Complementary
✓Early recognition of struggle, paired with individualized support — tailored deadlines, storytelling, one-on-one engagement
✓Maintained discipline and expectations, treated as part of how resilience is actually built
✓A culture where seeking help is normalized alongside using adversity to grow

07  ·  Conclusion

Support and Discipline, Not Support or Discipline

Four recommendations for educators navigating this tension in their own classrooms.

1

Recognize early signs of struggle — changes in behavior or academic performance — and respond to each student individually.

2

Maintain discipline and expectations alongside emotional support; growth requires both structure and responsibility.

3

Adjust pacing and teaching methods — storytelling, one-on-one engagement — when a rigid approach is failing a diverse cohort.

4

Build open conversations about mental health into the classroom without treating resilience-building as its opposite.

Closing

As World Suicide Prevention Day approaches, honoring those we’ve lost is not only about acknowledging their struggles but about building a future where strength, resilience, and mental health coexist.
