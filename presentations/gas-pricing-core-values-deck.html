Transforming Indonesia's Core Values:
Lessons From the Gas Pricing Challenge

Indonesian manufacturers pay up to $12.52 per MMBTU for gas that costs $3 in Thailand or Vietnam — not because the country lacks reserves, but because a national preference for short-term fixes keeps its energy market trapped in the same reinforcing feedback loop system dynamics predicts.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
HBS Alumni and Affiliated Alumni of MIT Sloan School of Management.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

A resource-rich nation, paying scarcity prices.

The puzzle isn’t geological. It’s behavioral.

Situation

Indonesia's manufacturing sector faces gas prices three to four times higher than regional competitors like Thailand and Vietnam — up to $12.52 per MMBTU in Java versus roughly $3 next door — despite the country’s natural gas abundance and surplus electricity.

Complication

Policymakers keep reaching for short-term fixes — like Floating Storage and Regasification Units (FSRUs) — that relieve supply pressure temporarily but carry high operating costs, leaving the underlying causes of high prices untouched.

Question

Why does a nation with such energy potential struggle with high domestic gas prices?

Answer

The answer lies in the complexity of Indonesia’s energy market — a system trapped in reinforcing feedback loops that a national inclination toward short-term thinking keeps policymakers from breaking.

02  ·  Gap

Quick fixes are treated as solutions, not symptoms.

The gap isn’t a missing policy tool — it’s a missing mental model.

Core Gap

Indonesia's constitution holds that natural resources should serve the people's welfare, yet current policy prioritizes immediate export revenues over affordable domestic energy, while subsidies once meant to broaden access have become selective. What's missing from the national conversation is systems thinking: an understanding that FSRUs, pipeline bottlenecks, and export contracts interact as reinforcing and balancing loops, not as isolated line items to be patched one at a time.

03  ·  Framework

The Feedback Loop Behind High Gas Prices

Three linked dynamics keep the system locked in place.

01
The Reinforcing Loop

Supply constraints raise prices; higher prices raise manufacturers’ costs; rising costs cut output, which should ease demand — but persistent supply constraints keep prices high regardless, trapping industry in a cycle of declining output and escalating cost.

02
Exports Over Domestic Supply

Long-term export contracts and pipeline bottlenecks reduce the gas available to local industry, keeping domestic prices elevated even when local demand falls — a self-reinforcing cycle that weakens industrial competitiveness.

03
The Delayed Balancing Loop

Infrastructure expansion — more pipeline capacity — could ease prices, but requires years of planning and construction. In the gap, costly stopgaps like FSRUs fill the space, treating symptoms while root causes remain untouched.

04  ·  Details

The Price Gap, in Numbers

The arithmetic of a market working against its own industry.

$12.52 versus roughly $3 per MMBTU

Industrial gas users in Java pay up to $12.52 per million British thermal unit, while prices in Thailand and Vietnam hover around $3 — a three-to-fourfold gap that directly erodes the cost competitiveness of Indonesian manufacturing.

Abundant reserves, constrained supply

Indonesia has natural gas abundance and surplus electricity, yet long-term export commitments and inadequate pipeline infrastructure keep domestic gas supply tight — scarcity is a market design outcome, not a resource shortfall.

FSRUs: relief now, cost later

Floating Storage and Regasification Units temporarily raise domestic supply but carry high operational costs that keep prices elevated — a stopgap that delays, rather than resolves, the underlying infrastructure gap.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Technical Fix or Cultural Shift?

Treating the wrong layer of the problem guarantees the cycle repeats.

✗ Treating It as a Technical Problem
✗Adding another FSRU or another subsidy line each time prices spike, without addressing pipeline capacity or export contract structure
✗Treating each price crisis as an isolated event rather than the predictable output of a reinforcing loop
✓ Treating It as a Cultural-Systemic Problem
✓Recognizing that short-term thinking is the shared assumption driving policy choices, not just an unfortunate side effect of them
✓Committing to multi-year infrastructure investment even though its payoff arrives after the current policy cycle
✓Reconciling export contracts and subsidy design with the constitutional mandate to serve domestic welfare

07  ·  Conclusion

Real Wealth Is a Mindset, Not Just a Reserve

Escaping the loop requires patience the system has not yet rewarded.

1

Prioritize pipeline capacity and infrastructure expansion over repeated short-term supply patches, even though the payoff arrives years later.

2

Revisit long-term export contracts so that domestic industrial needs are weighed alongside international revenue commitments.

3

Redesign subsidies to align with the constitutional principle that natural resources serve the people's welfare, rather than selective sector benefit.

4

Cultivate a national mindset that values endurance and systemic thinking over the recurring appeal of the quick fix.

Closing

Real wealth lies not just in the resources beneath the land but in the mindset that governs their use. It's time for Indonesia to grow beyond quick solutions and embrace a future that reflects a commitment to the long-term prosperity of its people.
