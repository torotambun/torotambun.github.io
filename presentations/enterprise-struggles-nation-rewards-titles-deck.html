Why Enterprise Struggles
in a Nation That Rewards Titles

Why are we still surprised when young Indonesians choose stable jobs over entrepreneurship? For centuries, power — not enterprise — was the fastest road to wealth. Corruption is not a new stain. It is an old inheritance.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Structural, Not Psychological

If many still chase stability, it is because the past taught their families where safety lived.

Situation

Research among university students shows most dream of stability: fixed salary, clear ladder, predictable life. Policymakers demand a surge of young entrepreneurs.

Complication

The author initially assumed the cause was modern — family pressure, fear of risk, lack of role models. But history reveals a structural inheritance: in old kingdoms and colonial administrations, office meant privilege, and income came from the seat, not the work.

Question

Why does a society that calls for more entrepreneurs keep producing cautious civil servants instead?

Answer

Because the system still rewards office over enterprise. Young Indonesians are not lazy or weak — they are rational actors reading an environment that has rewarded authority over value creation for centuries.

02  ·  Gap

Be Bold, But Do Not Challenge the System

The state sends mixed signals, and the environment punishes failure harshly.

Core Gap

Ministries hold bootcamps and launch incubators, yet the same system still rewards conformity, hierarchy, and seniority. The environment rarely punishes mediocrity but harshly punishes failure — society still sees bankruptcy as moral weakness rather than a learning curve. In a system built around authority, permission precedes initiative, and entrepreneurship needs oxygen, not inspection.

03  ·  Framework

Why Safety Reads as Filial Duty

A student is not only choosing between start-up and salary; they are choosing between two moral worlds.

1
Historical Structural Inheritance

Generations watched authority, not enterprise, bring prosperity. That habit did not vanish after independence — it only changed uniforms, from royal courts to today's bureaucracy.

2
Symbolic Capital (Bourdieu)

Alongside economic, social, and cultural capital, symbolic capital explains why holding titles — minister, vice minister, director general — remains the highest form of recognition.

3
"Cari Aman" as Moral Duty

In many families, safety is not cowardice but responsibility. Predictable income is proof of love, not fear; failure risks social standing and family trust.

4
Rent-Seeking Over Innovation

When authority is the main route to prosperity, people compete for access, not ideas. Bureaucracy expands while private enterprise stays small and under-capitalized.

04  ·  Details

Emotional Codes, Wired Early

By university age, the lessons are already deep.

"Yang penting stabil." "Gaji tetap." "Jangan bikin malu keluarga."

These phrases form early, embedding the moral weight of safety long before a young person ever weighs a career decision.

A gathering of Indonesian graduates from leading universities abroad

The accomplished speakers were mostly civil servants — power and prestige once again stood at the center, even among the most internationally educated.

Formal pay was small; "customary fees" were the real income

In old kingdoms and colonial administrations, officeholders were free to collect customary fees, charge for access, and take percentages from trade — income came from the seat, not the work.

05  ·  Evidence

One King Tried to Change It

Culture resisted command — and that resistance is itself the evidence.

HISTORICAL
Mangkunagara IV steered his court toward trade

One of the most forward-looking kings of his time tried to redirect his court toward entrepreneurship. Yet culture resisted; changing deep habits takes more than ideas.

SOCIOLOGICAL
Ogburn: culture shifts across two or three generations

Real change requires leaders with both vision and longevity, able to hold direction for 25 to 75 years — a continuity rare in today's short political cycles.

06  ·  Implications

Blaming Youth vs. Fixing the System

The fault is not in the youth but in the structure they read accurately.

✗ Blaming the Youth
✗Calling young Indonesians lazy, entitled, or weak for preferring civil service or corporate jobs over start-ups
✗Importing entrepreneurship attitudes from the U.S. or Europe without accounting for Indonesia's different reward structure
✓ Fixing What Gets Rewarded
✓Reducing the cost of failure and ensuring stable regulations, so honest effort is protected from privilege
✓Changing the narrative around entrepreneurs from reckless gamblers to responsible builders whose failure can still bring honor
✓Recognizing Indonesia's high power-distance culture as a hidden advantage when leadership is virtuous and steady
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

A Generation of Visible Fairness

Culture remembers what it rewards.

1

Reduce the cost of failure and ensure stable, predictable regulations — the fragile ground that makes start-ups riskier than they need to be.

2

Protect honest effort from privilege, so people no longer need to chase authority to survive economically.

3

Change the narrative around risk and failure across a generation — where merit outpaces privilege and rules apply to everyone.

Closing

Young Indonesians are not lazy, entitled, or weak. They are rational. They understand the system better than many adults admit. We should stop blaming them for wanting stability and instead ask what kind of system makes stability the only sensible choice.
