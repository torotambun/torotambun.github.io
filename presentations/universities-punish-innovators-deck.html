Universities May Be Killing
Their Innovators Softly

For six years embedded in university-based entrepreneurship programs, one pattern kept surfacing: the lecturers who carried the entrepreneurial work of the campus were rarely visible when formal academic evaluation took place.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Doctoral Researcher in Corporate Innovation Ecosystems, School of Business and Management, Institut Teknologi Bandung (SBM-ITB).
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Central to the Work, Invisible to the Record

They mentored students through market rejection, restarts, and uncertainty. Yet their entrepreneurial labor barely entered the promotion record.

Situation

Lecturers who built ventures with students, opened industry networks, and stayed involved when projects failed were central to incubators and venture studios across campus.

Complication

When promotion cycles arrived, what counted were journal publications, formal teaching loads, and community service reports. Companies built and ventures guided were largely absent from the record.

Question

Why does a university that brands itself as an innovation engine measure its academics as though venture work does not exist?

Answer

This could not be fixed by how individuals worked, but by how the system measured their work — the promotion architecture never adapted to a world where innovation depends on entrepreneurial labor.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

Structurally Absent, Not Weakly Weighted

Venture creation, ecosystem building, and financial leadership were not undervalued — they had no place to appear at all.

Core Gap

The promotion architecture counted teaching hours, indexed publications, and community-service reports. It did not recognize venture creation, ecosystem building, institutional entrepreneurship, or financial leadership. As a result, every hour invested in innovation reduced the outputs that could be converted into academic progression — placing entrepreneurial contribution and academic survival into direct competition.

03  ·  Framework

A Measurement System Frozen in an Earlier World

Universities increasingly present themselves as innovation engines while the rules of academic success remain unchanged.

1
Innovation = Invention × Commercialization

Not every inventor becomes an innovator. Innovation begins when invention meets commercialization — when ideas reach paying customers, a distinction the promotion system never encoded.

2
A System Anchored in an Earlier Academic World

Teaching, research, and community service were once sufficient measures of academic work. Venture building and ecosystem development came later, but professorship credits never adapted.

3
Declared Priorities vs. Rewarded Behavior

Innovation is promoted in policy and branding, yet the measurement system has no way to register this work in promotion calculations — creating a widening gap between what is demanded and what is rewarded.

4
Direct Competition for Time

Publishing more required stepping away from ventures. Sustaining ventures weakened academic standing. No combination of individual adjustments could reconcile the conflict.

04  ·  Details

The Contradiction Reaches the Classroom

Students respond to lived experience. Teaching assignments continue to reward publication credentials instead.

Students seek practitioners, institutions reward publishers

Students in entrepreneurship programs respond more strongly to instructors with lived entrepreneurial experience, yet teaching assignments continue to privilege publication credentials.

Practitioners marginalized, then they leave

Practitioners who carry precisely the experience students seek remain marginal to academic careers because their work cannot be translated into promotion metrics — universities increasingly teach entrepreneurship without entrepreneurs.

Specialization needed, uniformity enforced

Entrepreneurial, administrative, financial, teaching, and research functions already operate daily inside universities, but no category exists for them in the promotion system.

05  ·  Evidence

Six Years of Direct Observation

A pattern that repeated consistently across campus after campus.

OBSERVED
Central to incubators, absent from the record

Lecturers built external credibility for their institutions through daily work no brochure could manufacture — yet none of it entered professorship credit calculations.

CONSEQUENCE
Capable ecosystem builders quietly leave

Their departure does not signal personal failure. It reflects a system unable to count their work — even as innovation centers on campus multiply.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
06  ·  Implications

More Programs vs. Redesigned Merit

Adding funding and encouragement will not resolve a problem rooted in how contribution is counted.

✗ Adding More Innovation Programs
✗Expanding funding, incubators, and encouragement while the promotion system continues to count the same things it always has
✗Forcing all academics into a single career mold defined by publications, teaching hours, and reports
✓ Redesigning How Merit Is Counted
✓Acknowledging differentiated academic orientations — teaching-focused, research-focused, entrepreneurial-focused, administrative-focused
✓Aligning evaluation weights to declared roles, so venture development counts the way scholarly output counts
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Counting What Is Publicly Celebrated

A system that repeatedly fails those doing the work it publicly celebrates must change how it counts them.

1

Assess entrepreneurial academics on venture development, ecosystem building, and industry engagement — not on publication counts alone.

2

Assess administrative and finance-oriented academics on institutional stewardship and sustainability, recognized as a distinct career track.

3

Reform promotion architecture before reform of programs — the innovators leaving campuses today are not failing individually; the system is failing to count them.

Closing

If universities genuinely seek to sustain innovation ecosystems, they must reform how contribution is evaluated. When a system repeatedly fails those doing the work it publicly celebrates, universities must change how performance credits are counted in their promotion system.
