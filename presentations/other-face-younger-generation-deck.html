The Other Face of the Younger Generation

Behind Indonesia’s celebrated “demographic bonus” sits a majority of millennials in blue-collar, low-wage work with no path into the innovation-driven economy the country needs — and the fix has to start in early childhood, not in another skills workshop.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Dosen Industri, Sekolah Tinggi Elektro dan Informatika, Institut Teknologi Bandung (STEI-ITB)
© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun

During 2020 to 2022 I met, intensively, the country’s future leaders studying in its most prestigious institutes and universities — bright, smart, English as a second language, visionary, diligent. They are the 10 percent bracket of university graduates absorbed into industry, the “upper-middle class, latte-sipping, future white-collar” image the mainstream media likes to project.

But a more articulated piece of journalism (Tehusijarana, 2020) portrays a far less visible majority of the country’s much-vaunted demographic bonus: millennials with only a high-school education, working blue-collar or low-level service jobs, earning around US$150 a month. Statistics Bureau data from 2017 put 20 percent of millennials in blue-collar work and 25 percent in sales or service, against just 1.4 percent in managerial roles. Only 14.4 percent have any post-secondary education at all.

© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun

A 2020 Jakarta Post documentary followed one such worker — a graveyard-shift employee earning US$107 a month, all of it going to a motorcycle and a smartphone, with a $3 daily allowance for food and no savings. He is the face the demographic-bonus narrative leaves out.

The industries that could absorb these graduates — innovation-driven enterprise, heavy machinery, capital-intensive manufacturing — are crowded out by a predominant rent-seeking business model, useful for the consumption side of the GDP equation but starving the investment side. Indonesians are early adopters of consumerism and laggards in the harder, 15-year work of building an innovation ecosystem.

S
Short term

Produce as many graduates as possible trained in the seven core technologies reshaping modern life — biotech, wireless mesh networks, nanotech, pervasive computing, robotics, machine learning, 3D printing — applied to Indonesia’s own industries: agriculture, fisheries, forestry, energy, tourism.

M
Mid term

Mobilize existing graduates, industrialists and retirees who already have system-dynamics, entrepreneurial and structured-logic skills, and pay them properly to teach in elementary, junior-high and senior-high schools — narrowing the knowledge and motivation gap among today’s teachers.

L
Long term

Start from early childhood — nutrition, safety, and the neuron-pruning window of the first five years — then invest through grades 1–12 in system-dynamic thinking, entrepreneurial action, and the basic values of innovation: novelty, authenticity, perspective, diversity, curiosity.

There is no popular short-term panacea here. A transformational map from the World Economic Forum’s Strategic Intelligence points the same direction: lifelong learning pathways, quality basic education, 21st-century curricula, digital fluency, STEM education. The European Parliament’s eight lifelong-learning competences — from mother-tongue communication to sense of initiative and entrepreneurship — line up with the same proposal.

The open question is whether the country’s elementary curriculum actually pivots around those competences, or whether it stays siloed in mechanical routines that produce neither street-smart nor in-class-smart citizens — people with a noble reason for existence: compassion toward others and the environment.

© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun

This writing is meant to raise urgency, not close the case: a call to design a measurement index for innovation-proneness rooted in the basic values of each region’s culture, and to weigh the culture factor with the same rigor system dynamics brings to any other precursor of ecosystem success. Without it, the region — the country — risks losing its identity to the next link in the global domino effect.
