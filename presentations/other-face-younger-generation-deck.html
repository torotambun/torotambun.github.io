The Other Face of
the Younger Generation

Behind Indonesia’s celebrated “demographic bonus” sits a majority of millennials in blue-collar, low-wage work with no path into the innovation-driven economy the country needs — and the fix has to start in early childhood, not in another skills workshop.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Dosen Industri, Sekolah Tinggi Elektro dan Informatika,
Institut Teknologi Bandung (STEI-ITB)
© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

A paradox between who the author meets and who the data describes.

A SCQA reading of the core argument, drawn from 2020–2022 teaching at ITB and STEI-ITB.

Situation

Bright, English-fluent, diligent students at the country’s most prestigious institutes represent the 10 percent bracket of graduates absorbed into industry — the image mainstream media projects of Indonesia’s millennials.

Complication

The Jakarta Post’s own 2020 reporting shows a majority of millennials with only high-school education, working blue-collar or low-level service jobs at roughly US$150 a month — a population the prestigious-campus image obscures entirely.

Question

What does Indonesia actually need to invest in, given this gap between the visible bracket and the majority?

Answer

A three-horizon investment — short-term technical skilling, mid-term teacher mobilization, long-term early-childhood formation — with the long-term horizon as the one that actually closes the gap.

02  ·  Research Gap

The demographic-bonus narrative reports the bracket, not the majority.

Public discourse on Indonesia’s demographic bonus is anchored to its most visible, least representative cohort.

Core Gap

Statistics Bureau data from 2017 shows around 20 percent of millennials in blue-collar work and 25 percent in sales or service, against just 1.4 percent in managerial roles; only 14.4 percent have any post-secondary education. The industries that could absorb these graduates — innovation-driven enterprise, capital-intensive manufacturing — are crowded out by a predominant rent-seeking business model.

© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun
03  ·  Framework

Three Investment Horizons

Each horizon targets a different point in the pipeline, and only the third addresses root cause.

S
Short Term

Train graduates in the seven core technologies reshaping modern life — biotech, wireless mesh networks, nanotech, pervasive computing, robotics, machine learning, 3D printing — applied to Indonesia’s own industries.

M
Mid Term

Mobilize existing graduates, industrialists and retirees who already have system-dynamics, entrepreneurial and structured-logic skills, and pay them properly to teach in elementary, junior-high and senior-high schools.

L
Long Term

Start from early childhood — nutrition, safety, and the neuron-pruning window of the first five years — then invest through grades 1–12 in system-dynamic thinking, entrepreneurial action, and the basic values of innovation.

04  ·  Details

The Blue-Collar Majority, in Numbers

Three data points anchor the gap between the visible bracket and the actual majority.

The documentary persona

A 2020 Jakarta Post documentary subject earns US$107 a month on a graveyard shift, all of it going to a motorcycle and smartphone, with a $3 daily allowance for food and no savings.

Greater Jakarta’s education floor

In Greater Jakarta, the highest per-capita-income region in the country, around 25 percent of men aged 20–34 and 38 percent of women have only junior-high diplomas or lower (ANU/UI joint study, 2010).

The rent-seeking crowd-out

Trillion-dollar dry powder from risk-capital institutions amplifies commerce based on demographics rather than innovation — capital that could otherwise fund long-term, innovation-driven enterprise development.

05  ·  Evidence

The Case for Starting in Early Childhood

Four sources anchor the long-term recommendation specifically.

EVIDENCE A
MIT’s ecosystem model

Budden & Murray (2019), An MIT Approach to Innovation, frames the innovation ecosystem as Risk Capital, Large Corporation, Government, Universities and Entrepreneurs working organically together.

EVIDENCE B
WEF’s education-skills map

Welpe & Rank (2023), Strategic Intelligence, World Economic Forum, names Lifelong Learning Pathways, Quality Basic Education, 21st Century Curricula and STEM Education as the theme’s core levers.

EVIDENCE C
The EU’s eight competences

European Parliament and Council (2006) lists eight lifelong-learning competences, from mother-tongue communication to sense of initiative and entrepreneurship — a checklist against which Indonesia’s own elementary curriculum can be measured.

EVIDENCE D
Seven remaking technologies

Segars (2018), “Seven Technologies Remaking the World,” anchors the short-term technical-skilling recommendation in a named, citable list rather than a generic “digital skills” gesture.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Where Policy Currently Aims

Most policy attention concentrates on the visible bracket the least in need of intervention.

✗ Where Attention Currently Goes
✗Showcase programs for the already-visible 10 percent bracket at elite institutes
✗Short-term digital-skilling workshops with no early-childhood counterpart
✗Rent-seeking capital absorption treated as economic dynamism
✓ Where Investment Should Go
✓Early-childhood nutrition and safety as the foundational, non-negotiable layer
✓Redeployed graduate and retiree talent teaching in K-12, properly compensated
✓Innovation-driven enterprise capital, not rent-seeking capital, as the growth engine
© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Three Horizons, One Sequence

The three recommendations below are not alternatives — they are a required sequence.

1

Fund early-childhood nutrition and safety as the non-negotiable first layer of any innovation-workforce strategy.

2

Redeploy graduates, industrialists and retirees into K-12 teaching roles, compensated to match their market value.

3

Redirect risk-capital incentives toward innovation-driven enterprise, away from rent-seeking platform businesses.

Closing

Despite the enormous effort in generating economy as measured by GDP, the steps taken so far have proven to fail: less than 1 percent startup sustainability, less than 7 percent innovation-driven entrepreneurs created, less than 12 percent graduate absorption. This writing is meant to raise urgency, not close the case.
