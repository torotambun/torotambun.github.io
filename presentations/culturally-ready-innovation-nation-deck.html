Fostering a Culturally Ready,
Innovative and Safe Cybernation

Indonesia’s enthusiasm for AI risks repeating an old mistake — adopting a technology without the cultural readiness to absorb it, or the cybersecurity posture to defend it. Early education, not policy mimicry, is the actual precondition.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Dosen Industri, Sekolah Tinggi Elektro dan Informatika,
Institut Teknologi Bandung (STEI-ITB)
© 2023 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

AI is not new. The readiness gap is.

A SCQA reading of the argument that automation anxiety has a long, patterned history.

Situation

Automation has reshaped human progress since the printing press, industrial automation, and the calculator — each provoking the same fear of displacement. AI itself was coined in 1950, when Indonesia was still an infant sovereign nation.

Complication

Countries that jump into AI deployment without cultural readiness face economic disparity, technological dependency, job displacement, and data inequality — while countries that fall behind face geopolitical disadvantage and cybersecurity exposure.

Question

Where should a developing nation actually begin, if the goal is a culturally ready, innovative and safe cybernation?

Answer

Begin with early education — STEM, teacher development, basic needs — run in parallel with immediate cybersecurity investment. Neither can wait for the other.

02  ·  Research Gap

“Parallel action” names the gap; it does not close it.

Policy discourse treats AI readiness and AI adoption pressure as sequential. They are not — and the honest tension has to be held, not resolved by rhetoric.

Core Gap

Readiness for AI is a multi-generational project, while the pressure to adopt AI now is immediate. Most policy language papers over this with “parallel action,” without specifying which institutions carry which half of the parallel track, or how they are funded simultaneously rather than sequentially.

03  ·  Framework

Two Risk Sets, One Response

The argument identifies risk on both sides of the adoption decision — falling behind and jumping in unprepared.

A
Risk of Falling Behind

Geopolitical disadvantage as AI-capable nations gain military and economic leverage; cultural erosion as imported technology norms dilute tradition; a new imperialism built on technological dependency; wider cybersecurity exposure and cross-border domino effects.

B
Risk of Jumping In Unprepared

Economic disparity as AI infrastructure costs outpace developing economies; technological dependency compromising sovereignty; job displacement in labor-intensive sectors; unchecked algorithmic bias and privacy violation under weak regulatory frameworks; compounding skills and data-inequality gaps.

04  ·  Details

The Early-Education Agenda

Four commitments define what “begin with education” concretely requires.

Early childhood and STEM access

Nurture creativity and problem-solving from early childhood; ensure equal access to quality education across urban and rural divides — the same sequencing principle that runs through this author’s other education writing.

Teachers and infrastructure

Continuous professional development for teachers, paired with investment in the facilities and technology needed to deliver it — preparation before curriculum, not curriculum before preparation.

Basic needs and entrepreneurship

Nutrition and healthcare as a precondition for engagement and creativity; grants, incubators and capital access for AI-era startups and R&D that tackles national challenges directly.

05  ·  Evidence

The Historical Pattern Behind the Argument

Four precedents establish that automation anxiety is recurring, not novel to AI.

EVIDENCE A
The printing press

Threatened the leaders of its day — the first documented instance of a technology triggering displacement anxiety in an information-gatekeeping elite.

EVIDENCE B
1800s industrial automation

Raised fears of mass job displacement — a fear that reshaped labor markets but did not eliminate labor itself.

EVIDENCE C
The 1970s calculator

Disrupted math education specifically — the closest historical analogue to AI’s disruption of writing and reasoning instruction today.

EVIDENCE D
AI’s actual age

The term “artificial intelligence” was coined in 1950 — when Indonesia was still an infant sovereign nation. The current moment is a continuation, not a rupture.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: What “Parallel Action” Requires to Be Real

The plan is right in principle. It fails if either track is treated as optional.

✗ Where Parallel Action Fails
✗Funding AI adoption while treating early-education investment as a future-budget item
✗Building cybersecurity capacity only after a breach, not alongside education investment
✗Leaving basic needs (nutrition, healthcare) as a separate ministry’s problem, disconnected from the AI agenda
✓ Where Parallel Action Holds
✓Fund cybersecurity infrastructure and early education from the same budget cycle, not sequentially
✓Treat teacher preparation as a prerequisite to any new AI-curriculum rollout
✓Connect basic-needs investment explicitly to the AI-readiness agenda, not as a separate track

07  ·  Conclusion

Security and Readiness Are One Investment

Three recommendations for treating cybersecurity and cultural readiness as a single line item.

1

Fund early-childhood STEM access and cybersecurity infrastructure from the same budget cycle, not sequential ones.

2

Require teacher preparation to precede, not follow, any new AI-curriculum rollout.

3

Build entrepreneurship and R&D support explicitly around AI-era national challenges, not generic startup incentives.

Closing

Indonesia can stand at the forefront of technological innovation only if it treats cybersecurity and cultural readiness as one investment, not two separate line items.
