The Unseen Toll: Mental Health
Challenges Facing Lecturers

Universities have built counseling services, workshops, and peer-support programs for students in distress — while lecturers, who juggle teaching, research, administration, mentoring, and family life on modest pay, are left to manage the same pressures largely alone.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
HBS Alumni and Affiliated Alumni of MIT Sloan School of Management.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Everyone asked about students. Who asked about the lecturers?

The mental health conversation in academia has a one-sided audience.

Situation

Discussions about mental health in academia focus almost entirely on students — rising anxiety, depression, and stress — prompting institutions to introduce counseling services, workshops, and peer-support programs, often modeled on Western best practice.

Complication

The well-being of lecturers, who wear the roles of educator, researcher, administrator, philanthropist, entrepreneur, and family member simultaneously, has been largely overlooked, with institutional support rarely reaching them.

Question

What about teachers' mental health — does anybody care?

Answer

Institutions must extend the same individualized, protective, and reciprocal support they built for students to lecturers — before burnout erodes teaching quality, research output, and the academic mission itself.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

Policies built to protect students now leave lecturers exposed.

A power shift meant to correct one imbalance has created another.

Core Gap

The historical view of students as the weaker party in the academic relationship led to policies safeguarding them — but those same policies have increasingly left lecturers vulnerable to exploitation, including verbal abuse, undermined authority, and parents deflecting blame for academic struggles onto educators who are already stretched across six or more roles. There is no reciprocal protection shielding lecturers the way policy shields students.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
03  ·  Framework

The Six Roles Lecturers Carry at Once

Each role alone is demanding. Together, on modest pay, they compound.

01
Educator & Researcher

Teaching that adapts to diverse learning styles, paired with pressure to produce innovative, regularly published, funded research — demands comparable to those in any high-performance research environment.

02
Administrator & Philanthropist

Managing assessments, compliance, and departmental planning, alongside embedded-but-unrecognized community outreach and mentoring obligations — largely invisible labor that still consumes significant time.

03
Entrepreneur & Family Member

Securing grants and industry partnerships while balancing family responsibilities on modest compensation — a load made heavier still by job insecurity in adjunct or part-time positions.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
04  ·  Details

Where the Institutional Support Gap Bites Hardest

Emotional labor and job insecurity compound the six-role burden.

Emotional labor, largely unacknowledged

Lecturers support students academically and personally, often without acknowledgment of the toll — a burden compounded by student bullying, verbal abuse, and efforts to undermine their authority with little institutional recourse.

Job insecurity on top of role overload

Adjunct and part-time lecturers work short-term contracts with limited benefits, an uncertainty that further deteriorates mental health in an already stressful environment.

Institutional consequences of inaction

Burnout degrades teaching quality and student engagement; absenteeism rises as fatigued lecturers take time to recover; overworked lecturers have less capacity for research, stifling institutional innovation and academic reputation.

05  ·  Evidence

Sources Behind the Argument

The piece draws on the asymmetry between existing student support infrastructure and its absence for lecturers.

EVIDENCE A
Existing student mental health infrastructure

Counseling services, mental health workshops, and peer support programs built for students — often emulating Western practice — establish the baseline of institutional capability the piece argues should extend to lecturers.

EVIDENCE B
The six-role workload description

The piece's own enumeration of lecturers' simultaneous roles — educator, researcher, administrator, philanthropist, entrepreneur, family member — grounds its account of structural overload.

EVIDENCE C

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Generic Wellness or Individualized Support?

A copied program is not the same as a designed one.

✗ Generic Well-Being Programs
✗Offering the same one-size-fits-all wellness resources built for students, without adapting to lecturers' distinct stressors
✗Leaving lecturers to manage bullying, emotional labor, and job insecurity without formal reporting channels
✓ Individualized, Reciprocal Support
✓Personalized assessments that identify each lecturer's specific stressors, rather than generic diagnosis
✓Reciprocal feedback mechanisms letting lecturers assess student professionalism and engagement, not just the reverse
✓Clear anti-bullying policy with formal, non-retaliatory reporting channels for lecturers
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

A Code of Mutual Accountability

Four steps toward parity between student and lecturer support.

1

Implement individualized diagnosis and tailored support plans for lecturers, mirroring the personalized approach already used for students.

2

Introduce reciprocal feedback mechanisms so lecturers can assess student professionalism and engagement, balancing existing accountability structures.

3

Strengthen anti-bullying policy with clear guidelines and anonymous, non-retaliatory reporting for incidents involving students or parents.

4

Formally recognize lecturers' emotional labor through counseling access and workshops, and establish a code of mutual accountability for students and lecturers alike.

Closing

The mental health of lecturers is critical to the success of the academic system. By implementing reciprocal feedback, protecting lecturers from bullying, recognizing emotional labor, and creating a code of mutual accountability, institutions can build a more balanced, supportive, and productive academic environment — benefiting both students and lecturers alike.
