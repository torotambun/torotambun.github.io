Start-Ups Deferred:
Entrepreneurial Curriculum Revisited

Indonesian students are not abandoning entrepreneurship for salaried work — they are combining both, building side ventures alongside structured employment through deliberate sequencing that most curricula still treat as a fallback rather than a strategy.

Dr. Toronata Tambun
Practitioner Lecturer in Engineering Innovation and Entrepreneurship at STEI-ITB, Affiliated Alumni of MIT Sloan School of Management.
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
01  ·  Introduction

Interest is rising. The startup rush is not.

Students are choosing sequencing over a single leap.

Situation

Entrepreneurship is gaining traction as a serious career aspiration among Indonesian university students, no longer perceived as marginal or informal but integrated into how young people imagine their professional futures.

Complication

That interest is not translating into full-time startup creation. Instead, a significant number of students intentionally run side businesses while continuing structured employment, especially in state-owned enterprises and multinational corporations.

Question

Does this pattern reflect hesitation, or a deliberate new career logic that entrepreneurship education has not caught up with?

Answer

It is deliberate sequencing — students building income stability, experience, and networks before deciding if full-time entrepreneurship is viable, and institutions must be redesigned around this hybrid logic rather than the startup-founder ideal.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
02  ·  Gap

Curricula still chase the founder myth

Side ventures are treated as fallback, not core competency.

Core Gap

Educational programs remain built around the idea that the startup is the goal. Most curricula still treat side businesses as transitional or fallback options — out of step with how students are actually planning their careers. Those who combine entrepreneurship with employment need to learn how to manage parallel commitments and build sustainable part-time ventures. These aren't auxiliary skills; they are core competencies for the new entrepreneurial career logic.

03  ·  Framework

The 4-Layer Entrepreneurial Career Pathway Model

Used as an interpretive guide to map how students actually decide.

01
Exposure

Students process prior exposure to entrepreneurship — family business ties versus purely academic or observational contact — which shapes initial confidence.

02
Reflection on Trajectory

Students reflect on where entrepreneurship fits relative to their existing or intended employment trajectory, rather than treating it as a binary choice.

03
Structural Response & Form

Students respond to structural influences — financial buffer, family expectations, risk — and land on specific entrepreneurial forms, most commonly hybrid or side-business pathways.

© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
04  ·  Details

What the Applied Learning Actually Shifted

Capability grew. Ambition for a full-time leap did not automatically follow.

Background Divide

Students with prior business ownership or family business ties began with strong entrepreneurial confidence; those with only academic exposure initially hesitated.

Applied Learning Effect

Activities like prototyping, pitching, and customer interviews shifted mindsets — entrepreneurship became thinkable, but still not something to pursue full time.

Capability → Optionality, Not Ambition

Gaining entrepreneurial capability doesn't automatically produce startup ambition; it produces career flexibility, a willingness to operate across multiple tracks.

Exposure Can Be Designed

Students without entrepreneurial family backgrounds showed major growth when given access to experiential tools — exposure isn't inherited, it can be built.

05  ·  Evidence

Grounding the Argument

Drawn from applied classroom observation at STEI-ITB.

EVIDENCE A
4-Layer Entrepreneurial Career Pathway Model

The interpretive framework applied to student decision-making across an entrepreneurship course, revealing hybrid and side-business pathways as the dominant, purposefully constructed outcome.

EVIDENCE B
Applied learning activities

Prototyping, pitching, and customer-interview exercises tracked across students with and without prior entrepreneurial exposure, showing convergence in confidence when exposure was designed rather than inherited.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Founder Ideal or Layered Career Architecture?

Only one matches how students are actually building their careers.

✗ The Founder-Ideal Curriculum
✗Treating side businesses as transitional stepping stones toward an eventual full-time startup
✗Withdrawing institutional support at graduation, penalizing students who are structuring a longer runway
✓ The Layered Career Curriculum
✓Teaching students to manage parallel commitments, navigate income regulations, and build sustainable part-time ventures as core skills
✓Designing experiential exposure deliberately for students without entrepreneurial family backgrounds
✓Extending mentoring and institutional pathways beyond graduation to match students' actual timing logic
© 2026 Dr. Toronata Tambun
07  ·  Conclusion

Institutions Must Catch Up to the Students

The pivot has already happened; the curriculum has not.

1

Redesign entrepreneurship education around hybrid and side-business pathways as the primary model, not the fallback.

2

Build applied, experiential exposure deliberately so entrepreneurial confidence is not limited to students from business-owning families.

3

Extend mentoring and institutional support beyond graduation to match the long-term runway students are already building.

Closing

The students are not waiting to be convinced. They've already made the pivot. The only question now is whether the institutions built to support them are ready to catch up.
