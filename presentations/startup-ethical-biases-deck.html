The Biases Behind Indonesian
Start-Ups' Ethical Pitfalls

The eFishery scandal — a claimed 400,000 fish feeders against a verified 24,000 — wasn't the product of deliberate malice, but of overconfidence bias, sunk cost fallacy, and moral licensing, biases rooted in childhood formation long before any founder faced an investor.

01  ·  Introduction

A $600 million discrepancy, and a familiar human pattern.

Fraud rarely starts as fraud. It starts as a justified small step.

Situation

The eFishery fraud scandal exposed deep flaws in Indonesia's corporate governance, with the company inflating 2024 revenue by nearly $600 million and claiming 400,000 fish feeder units against a verified 24,000.

Complication

eFishery is not an isolated case — many founders operate under extreme pressure where ethical lines blur, and fraud rarely results from deliberate malice but from cognitive biases, personal history, and environmental pressure that distort judgment.

Question

What actually drives founders toward fraud, and can it be prevented rather than merely punished after the fact?

Answer

Fraud is a preventable failure rooted in cognitive bias and childhood ethical formation, not an inevitable feature of ambition — awareness, structured safeguards, and early moral education can shift the trajectory toward integrity.

02  ·  Gap

Ethical resilience is rarely built in adulthood.

By the time a founder faces investors, the pattern is often already set.

Core Gap

Ethical resilience is rarely built in adulthood; its roots lie in early childhood. Neuroscientific research confirms that synaptic pruning and myelination strengthen neural pathways based on repeated experience, meaning moral reasoning, if reinforced early, becomes an instinctive response under stress. Children who observe integrity in caregivers internalize similar behaviors; those raised where dishonesty is normalized develop cognitive patterns that make unethical decision-making feel justifiable. Corporate governance frameworks, built for adults, cannot retroactively install what childhood formation never provided.

03  ·  Framework

The Cognitive Biases Behind Founder Fraud

Each bias makes a small ethical lapse feel survivable.

01
Overconfidence & Optimism Bias

Founders believe they can manage small ethical lapses without long-term consequences, convincing themselves the risks of fraud are minimal because future success will justify present deception.

02
Sunk Cost Fallacy & Loss Aversion

The more time and resources invested, the harder it becomes to walk away; deception starts to feel like a necessary step to protect prior effort as fear of financial failure triggers manipulation of reports.

03
Social Proof & Moral Licensing

Founders who believe "everyone inflates their numbers to survive" feel less moral conflict doing the same, while self-styled hardworking visionaries begin to feel entitled to bending the rules.

04  ·  Details

The eFishery Case, in Coverage

Global media traced the scale and speed of the exposure.

400,000 claimed versus 24,000 verified

The Jakarta Post (January 22, 2025) confirmed eFishery inflated 2024 revenue by nearly $600 million and exaggerated fish feeder operations at this ratio — a discrepancy CNN Indonesia estimated at Rp9.7 trillion.

Ten senior executives implicated

Tech in Asia reported at least ten senior executives were implicated, while Outlook Business detailed how a whistleblower exposed the fraud — underscoring the role of internal accountability structures.

Socioeconomic background and rule-bending

Fergusson, Swain-Campbell, and Horwood (2004) found individuals from disadvantaged backgrounds reported higher rates of rule-bending behavior, while You and Khagram (2005) found income inequality increases corruption through affluent access to resources — distinct mechanisms, same underlying cognitive distortions.

06  ·  Implications

Diagnosis: Moral Weakness or Preventable Failure?

Treating fraud as an isolated moral failure blocks the actual fix.

✗ Fraud as Individual Moral Failure
✗Punishing the founder after the fact without addressing the cognitive and structural conditions that enabled the deception
✗Assuming ethical resilience can be installed through corporate compliance training alone
✓ Fraud as a Preventable Systemic Pattern
✓Keeping decision logs and engaging external advisors to counteract bias-driven decisions before they compound
✓Reframing failure as a learning opportunity to reduce loss aversion and sunk cost fallacy
✓Investing in early childhood ethical formation as the actual root-level intervention

07  ·  Conclusion

Fraud Is Preventable, Not Inevitable

Four safeguards, from founder practice to childhood formation.

1

Keep a decision log to document critical choices, allowing founders to track when cognitive biases may have shaped their reasoning.

2

Engage external advisors and structured reporting mechanisms, reducing the likelihood of bias-driven decisions going unchecked.

3

Foster an open company culture where employees can question decisions without fear of retaliation, preventing self-serving bias from taking root.

4

Invest in early exposure to ethical reasoning and critical thinking, since resilience formed in childhood shapes decision-making under pressure decades later.

Closing

Fraud is not simply a result of moral weakness. Recognizing biases, reinforcing ethical behaviors, and fostering resilience can ensure that ethical leadership is not just an aspiration but an ingrained practice, shaped early in life and reinforced throughout a founder's journey.
